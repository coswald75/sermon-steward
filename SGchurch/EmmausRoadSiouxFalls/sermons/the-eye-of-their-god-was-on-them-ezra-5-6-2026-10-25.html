1 · Develops the ballast metaphor to illustrate how stabilizing truth functions in the soul during trials

Ballast is the weight in the bottom of a ship that pulls the ship upright whenever the wind or the waves toss it. A ship without ballast is easily tipped over. A ship with weighty ballast has stability. Ballast is heavy, so it pulls that storm-tossed ship back to center. Do you have weighty truth deep down in your soul functioning to give you stability in the storms of life?

2 · Establishes the sermon's purpose: Ezra 5–6 is a God-given story that functions to convince us of Romans 8:28 by providing narrative proof, loading the soul with stabilizing truth for mission and trials

And Ezra 5 and 6 is one of those stories God has given us in His Word to convince us of the truth summarized in passages like Romans 8, It's a true story from history. These things really happened. This is a historical story inspired— the record, the account of it is inspired by the Spirit of God and it's meant to load your soul with ballast, with the weighty truth that God does in fact cause all things to work together for good for those who love him. It will produce in you hope and peace and joy, along with gratitude and comfort and security. And if you need more proof than Ezra 5 and 6 that God is at work in all things, the cross of Christ is the ultimate proof, the ultimate proof. Jesus, who was crucified and killed by lawless men in rebellion against God, accomplish your salvation so that when the enemies of God thought that they had triumphed over God himself, God had actually triumphed over all his enemies and over all of your sin. God is at work in all things for your good.

29 · Transitions to the third point (God's favor satisfies) and introduces the theme of joyful celebration at the temple's completion

Finally, God's favor satisfies his people. The climax of the story in Ezra 6:16-22 is where God's work for his people results in their joy and their celebration and their worship. When the work is done and the temple is rebuilt, verse 16 says in chapter 6, and the people of Israel, the priests and the Levites and the rest of the returned exiles celebrated the dedication of this house of God with joy. Joyful celebration is the theme of this conclusion. Verse 22 says at the very end, for the Lord had made them joyful. The Lord had made them joyful.

30 · Describes the dedication sacrifices and Passover celebration, noting the remnant's smaller scale yet genuine worship and sense of living through their own exodus

So they gather in the temple and they dedicate it with sacrifices, bulls and rams. And, and it's, it's like Solomon's dedication, but this one, they offer hundreds of sacrifices, whereas Solomon had offered tens of thousands. It's minuscule in comparison, but it's a testimony that God had preserved a remnant of his people. For the praise of his glory. And so they offer sacrifices for the atonement of their sin, knowing God had been gracious to them. And they keep the Passover and the Feast of Unleavened Bread, these celebrations that recalled God's work of redemption through the Exodus. Just imagine for these returning exiles, they must have felt like they had lived through their own Exodus as they celebrated the Passover there in Jerusalem.

31 · Clarifies that the exclusion in Ezra 4 was about purity, not ethnicity; anyone who separated from sin to worship God was welcomed

Look at verse 21. It says the Passover was eaten by the people of Israel who had returned from exile and also by everyone who had joined them and separated himself from the uncleanness of the peoples of the land to worship the Lord, the God of Israel. This verse corrects a possible misreading of chapter 4, verse 3, where the response from those in Jerusalem to their opponents who came to them was, you have nothing to do with us in building a house to our God. We alone will build to the Lord, the God of Israel. That might sound mean or exclusive. It was discerning. They realized that these were enemies who were opposing them. Chapter 6:21 is clear. This was never about ethnic superiority. This was about moral purity, separation from sin, devotion to God in worship, and to anyone who would leave their idols and leave their sin and leave their wicked ways and worship the one true God, the door was wide open. Come. And worship with us.

32 · Connects the inclusive worship in Ezra 6 to Zechariah's prophecy of nations seeking God, which is fulfilled in the multi-ethnic church today

In fact, this is the initial fulfillment of God's plan to save the nations, which the prophet Zechariah foretold in these days in Zechariah 8, when he said, thus says the Lord of hosts, peoples shall yet come. Even the inhabitants of many cities, the inhabitants of one city shall go to another saying, let us go at once to entreat the favor of the Lord and to seek the Lord of hosts. I myself am going. Many peoples and strong nations shall come to seek the Lord of hosts in Jerusalem, to entreat the favor of the Lord. Thus says the Lord of hosts, in those days, 10 men from the nations of every tongue shall take hold of the robe of a Jew saying, let us go with you, for we have heard that God is with you. That's the witness that's going to go out to the nations. God is in the midst of these people. And that prophecy that Zechariah gave in That day is now fulfilled in the Church of Jesus Christ, where God is present with us so that the nations would see gatherings of God's people here in Sioux Falls and in Nairobi, Kenya, and say, God is in your midst. Can we come? Can we hear from him? Can we learn about him? Can we worship him with you?

33 · Applies the exiles' joy to believers, arguing we should have even greater joy in Christ's finished work and God's unreserved favor

If these exiles so rejoiced with exuberant joy, at the completion of the physical temple, how much more should you and I rejoice today when we have the word that Jesus spoke from the cross when he said, it is finished. And if these exiles so rejoiced when God turned the heart of the King of Persia toward them in favor, how much more joy should we have knowing that God has dealt once for all with all of his wrath toward us, that he offered his own Son in our place for our sins, so that now his heart is turned with unreserved favor toward us, to show us nothing but love and mercy and compassion and goodness all the days of our lives. And we will dwell in the house of the Lord forever. How great should be our joy and our satisfaction in our great Redeemer.

34 · Closes with diagnostic questions calling the congregation to rest in God's goodness and promises, with the fruit being visible peace, joy, and agency in the world

Have you tasted the goodness of God in, in Jesus Christ? Are you convinced right now that God is working in all your circumstances for his glory and for your joy? I'm not asking if, if your circumstances right now are ideal or if your life right now is trouble-free. I'm asking, are you right now resting by faith in the good and gracious God, the God who did not spare his own Son but graciously gave him up for you? Are you resting in the God who promises to work all things for your ultimate good? Are you stirred by his word? Are you sustained consciously by his providence in your everyday life? And are you satisfied by his favor toward you in Christ. The fruit of that will be in our lives for God's glory and our witness in this world, overflowing peace and joy and boldness and courage and agency and action in this world to the glory of our God.

35 · Closing prayer asking God to stir his people by his word, producing agency and fruit for his glory and the good of the world

Let's pray. Father, it is our confidence and it is our joy to know that you stir your people by your word. So we just pray. I think of Martin Luther's words again: I did nothing, the word did everything. God, would you produce in your people, in the people of Emmaus Road Church this morning, and your people gathered in local churches all over the city, all over this country, around the world— we think of our brothers and sisters who have already been up this morning and worshiped you in Nairobi, Kenya. God, let your word dwell richly in your people and stir your people out of complacency, out of lethargy, out of sin, out of compromise. Stir your people and produce in us agency and action and fruit that would abound to your praise. Your glory. For the good of this world. For the good of our neighbors. In Jesus' name. Amen. Let's stand together.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Ezra 5:1–2, the prophets Haggai and Zechariah spoke God's word to the people, and as a result the work on the temple resumed. What does this tell us about the power and function of God's word in the life of his people, especially when they are discouraged or facing opposition?
    Ezra 5:1–2
    → Can you think of a time when God's word, spoken or read, has stirred you or someone you know to action or renewed faith? What made that word effective?
  2. When the Persian officials arrived to investigate the temple construction (Ezra 5:3–6:12), the Jews had no legal documentation—yet the decree of King Cyrus was discovered and King Darius not only allowed the work to continue but funded it. How does this sequence of events demonstrate God's providence at work 'behind the scenes' in human government and circumstance?
    Ezra 5:3–6:12; Proverbs 21:1
    → What does Proverbs 21:1 add to our understanding of how God was working through these Persian kings?
  3. The sermon claims that 'divine sovereignty does not eliminate human responsibility; rather, God's sovereign decree makes human work possible and effective.' How do you see both God's sovereignty and human responsibility working together in Ezra 5–6? What would have been missing if either one had been absent?
    → How does this balance—God sovereignly accomplishing his purposes *through* human faithfulness—speak to the way you think about your own work and obedience?
  4. The completion of the temple resulted in joyful celebration (Ezra 6:22). The sermon suggests this foreshadows the greater joy believers have in Christ. What is it about seeing God's purposes accomplished—and knowing he sustained us through opposition to get there—that naturally produces joy and gratitude?
    Ezra 6:22
    → How might remembering past instances when God sustained you through difficulty shape the way you approach current challenges?
  5. In Romans 8:28, Paul writes that 'God causes all things to work together for good for those who love him.' How does the history recorded in Ezra 5–6 serve as concrete evidence that this promise is true? What does it mean for us to trust this truth when we face our own seasons of delay, opposition, or uncertainty?
    → What specific situation in your life right now requires you to believe that God is working all things for your good, even if you cannot yet see how?
  6. The sermon emphasizes that God works for his people in three ways: his word stirs them, his providence sustains them, and his favor satisfies them. As you look at your own spiritual life this week, in which of these three dimensions do you most need to see God at work right now—and how might the example of Ezra's community encourage you to wait for and trust his working?
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how God works for his people through his stirring word, his sustaining providence, and his satisfying favor—anchored in the historical proof of Ezra 5–6 that Romans 8:28 is true.

Monday Ezra 5:1-2

When the temple work had stalled under enemy pressure, God spoke through the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, and their word stirred Zerubbabel and Joshua to resume building with vigor. We see here that God's word is never merely informative—it is alive, active, and efficacious, rousing the discouraged to faithful action even when the cost is real. This is the first and foundational way God works for his people: his word pierces apathy and awakens obedience.

Tuesday Proverbs 21:1

The king's heart is a stream of water in the hand of the Lord, yet the king still makes real decisions and acts. This captures the paradox at the heart of Ezra 5–6: God moved King Darius to investigate the Jews' work, but Darius genuinely deliberated and genuinely chose to favor them. Our responsibility to act is not weakened by God's sovereignty; it is grounded in it—we work knowing that God himself ordains both our effort and its fruit.

Wednesday Ezra 6:14

The people built and finished the temple "in accordance with the decree of the God of Israel and the decrees of Cyrus." Cyrus's original decree, long buried and forgotten by men, was not forgotten by God; he brought it to light at precisely the right moment, turning governmental opposition into governmental provision. When we feel invisible, when our faithfulness seems to go unseen, we rest in the truth that our all-knowing God sees and acts; nothing escapes his notice, and he makes known what is hidden at the perfect hour.

Thursday Ezra 6:22

The people celebrated the completion of the temple with great joy, for the Lord had caused the king of Assyria to turn his will toward them and aid the work. This joy is not sentimental; it is the proper, corporate response of a people recognizing God's concrete kindness and favor toward them. As we gather together and taste God's faithfulness in our lives, we are given a foretaste of the joy that awaits us in Christ, the true and greater temple in whom all God's purposes find their fulfillment.

Friday Ezra 5:3-6:12

What looked like catastrophe—the cessation of rebuilding, the king's investigation, the apparent vulnerability of the small Jewish remnant—God wove into the means of their deliverance and provision. This narrative is not theoretical comfort; it is concrete, historical evidence that Romans 8:28 is true for us as it was for them. When life's storms threaten to crush us, we are invited to remember how the God who stood for his people then stands for us now, making all things—even our opposition, our delays, our weakness—to work together for our good.
