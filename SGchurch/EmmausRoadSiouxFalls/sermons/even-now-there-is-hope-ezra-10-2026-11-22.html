Even Now There Is Hope

Ezra 10:1-44 November 22, 2026 Pastor Ryan Chase
Thesis Even in the worst spiritual collapse, there is audacious hope because sin can be confessed and forsaken, people are morally responsible to respond rightly, change is possible by God's grace, and God is merciful—demonstrated supremely in Christ who satisfies both God's justice and His mercy.

21 · Establishes the theological principle that while self-transformation is impossible (citing Jeremiah 13:23), God specializes in transforming people by His grace (citing Titus 2:11-12)

From a human vantage point, nothing seems less possible to us than change for sinners. You might feel that personally yourself, some sin you struggle against. There might be somebody in your life you look at and you just think that, that person is never going to change. How many times have you said that about yourself or about someone else? Because it is impossible to save yourself or to change yourself. Jeremiah 13:23 says, can the Ethiopian change his skin or the leopard his spots? Then also you can do good who are accustomed to do evil. You, you can't do it. But transforming people by his own grace is exactly what God does. And he does it all the time. Titus 2:11-12 says, for the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation for all people and training us to renounce ungodliness and worldly passions and to live self-controlled, upright, and godly lives in the present age. True repentance leads to real reform and lasting change, which gives hope.

22 · Signals the fourth and final reason for hope, pivoting from human agency and divine transformation to God's character

And finally, there's hope because God is merciful.

23 · Establishes that though Ezra 10 never uses the words 'mercy' or 'grace,' the entire movement of the chapter is saturated with hope in God's mercy—and this is the foundational reason all other reasons for hope work

Though the words mercy, forgiveness, compassion, grace are never explicitly mentioned here in Ezra 10, the movement of the story from the public oath to the solemn assembly to the careful examination, all of it is brimming with hope in the mercy of God. And this is really the only reason to hope. There would be no hope for change if it wasn't for the mercy of God. And those first 2 points, our sin and our moral responsibility, would not leave us with hope apart from the grace of God. But God is merciful.

24 · Identifies Shechaniah's impulse in verse 3 to make a covenant with God as the right response to sin, contrasting this with Adam and Eve's impulse to hide—raising the diagnostic question of whether the congregation runs to God or from God in sin

And Shechaniah expresses this in verse 3 when he says, therefore, let us make a covenant with God. His impulse is exactly right in that moment. What are you most tempted to do when you sin? Is it to turn back to God or is it to run from God like Adam and Eve who hid themselves and who attempted to make coverings for their nakedness and their shame with fig leaves? Do you run to God for mercy or do you run from God?

25 · Shows Ezra in verse 11 expressing the same hope—calling people to confess to the Lord and turn to Him rather than away

Ezra expresses this same hope in verse 11 when he urges the people, now then make confession to the Lord, the God of your fathers, and do his will. Turn to to him, not from him.

26 · Identifies verse 14 as the people's greatest expression of hope in God's mercy—their belief that God's fierce wrath could be turned away motivated their repentance and reform

And the people, the people give the greatest expression of this hope in the mercy of God in verse 14. Let all in our cities who have taken foreign wives come at appointed times until the fierce wrath of our God over this matter is turned away from us. What a line. Until the fierce wrath of our God over this matter is turned away from us. They were motivated to take steps and to amend their ways because they believed that the fierce wrath of God could be turned away.

27 · Reorients modern sensibilities by arguing that divine wrath is not the scandal—the scandal is that God's wrath can be turned away

Most people today are offended at the idea that there's a God who expresses fierce wrath toward sin. What should shock us is not the fact that Israel fell so quickly back into sin after returning from their exile. All people have sinned and fallen short of the glory of God. We should not be shocked that they fell so quickly into sin, nor that God expresses just wrath against that sin. We should rather be amazed that the fierce wrath of God can be turned away from sinners.

28 · Traces the source of Israel's audacious hope to God's self-revelation to Moses in Exodus 34—God is merciful and forgiving but will not clear the guilty, requiring atonement through sacrifice

Where did Ezra, where did Shechaniah, where did the people of Israel get this audacious hope that their sins could be forgiven? They got it from God himself, who revealed himself to Moses saying in Exodus 34, the Lord, The Lord, a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness, keeping steadfast love for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, but who will by no means clear the guilty. That is, God is not going to sweep sin under the rug. He's not going to overlook sin, but he has made a way to atone for sin through sacrifice and repentance and faith. And God taught this to his people Israel by giving them the sacrificial system.

29 · Returns to the text to show the guilty men bringing the prescribed guilt offering (a ram) according to Leviticus 6:6, explaining that the sacrificial system taught that forgiveness requires blood, though animal blood could not actually remove sin

According to verse 19, the guilty men pledged themselves to put away their wives, and their guilt offering was a ram of the flock for their guilt. That's the sacrifice prescribed in Leviticus 6:6. God, in his mercy, had made a way for guilty sinners to be reconciled to him through the blood of an innocent substitute. Not that the blood of rams and bulls and goats could take away sin, But the sacrificial system taught the world that without the shedding of blood, there is no forgiveness for sin, not for theirs and not for yours.

30 · Establishes that the OT sacrifices pointed forward to God's promise of a coming Savior, citing Isaiah 55 as evidence of God's repeated appeal for sinners to return and receive abundant pardon—God pleads with sinners to come

And so guilty sinners were to bring an offering, acknowledging their sin, demonstrating their trust in God's mercy, and showing their hope, not in rams, but in God's promise that one day he would send a Savior who would come to this world to die in our place. Again and again, God's Word reveals God's merciful desire to forgive sinners. Just consider God's appeal in Isaiah 55: Seek the Lord while he may be found, call upon him while he is near. Let the wicked forsake his way and the unrighteous man his thoughts. Let him return to the Lord that he may have compassion on him and to our God, for he will abundantly pardon. Do you hear God just pleading appealing. Think about this. Think about your soul. Think about the guilt of your sin. Think about your inability to do anything about it. Think about the mess that you experience in life and consider that there is a God who is so compassionate and gracious and merciful. He will abundantly pardon you if you turn to him and trust in him.

31 · Builds anticipation by citing three OT promises of complete forgiveness (Psalm 103, Micah 7, Isaiah 43), then raising the theological tension: how can God both show mercy and maintain justice?

He removes our transgressions from us as far as the east is from the west, Psalm 103. He casts all our sins into the depths of the sea, Micah 7. He blots out our transgressions and he, the omniscient God who knows all things says, I will remember your sins no more. Isaiah 43. How can he do that? How can God show us mercy without perverting his justice, which demands that our sins be punished? How can God uphold his justice without destroying us?

32 · Resolves the tension by reading Romans 3:21-26, showing that Christ's propitiatory sacrifice allows God to be both just (punishing sin in Christ) and justifier (forgiving those who believe)

Listen to this answer in Romans 3 as we close. But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the law and the prophets bear witness to it. All the commands, all the sacrifices, all the rituals, all pointed to this: the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ. For all who believe. For there's no distinction, for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God. If you look at that list in Ezra 10, it starts with the priests themselves, then the Levites, the gatekeepers, and the singers. There are no exceptions. Nobody based on their class, their status, their role, their responsibility, nobody was immune. Guilty of sin. There's no distinction. All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God and are justified by his grace as a gift through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus, whom God put forward as a propitiation— that is, a sacrifice that absorbs wrath and secures favor— by his blood, to be received by faith. And this, this was to show God's righteousness, because in his divine forbearance, in his patience, he passed over former sins. He passed over the sins of the Israelites right here in Ezra 10 because the bulls and the goats and the rams could not pay for their sins. So he passed over those sins until he would send his Son, and it was to show his righteousness at the present time so that he might be both just— that is, the God who punishes sin— and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus at the same time.

33 · Brings the sermon to its doxological climax, marveling at how God upheld both justice and mercy through Christ's substitutionary death, and explicitly connects this to Ezra 10's refrain: 'even now there is hope in spite of all this' because Christ died for sins

Oh, the mercy of our God, the wisdom, the goodness of God. He upheld the glory of His justice by punishing the sins of all His people, past, present, and future, in the innocent substitute of His own Son, Jesus Christ. And He made a way to display the glory of His grace and His mercy by forgiving and justifying everyone who will turn from their sin and rely on Jesus alone in faith. And That is why even now there is hope in spite of all this. There's hope because Christ died for our sins.

34 · Closing prayer asking God to cause the congregation to abound in hope by pouring His Spirit into their hearts as they trust in Christ, whose blood atones for all sin and reconciles sinners to God

Let's pray. Father, your word calls you the God of hope. You're the God of hope and we want that hope, hope that comes from knowing you, hope that comes from trembling at your word, from acknowledging our sin and confessing and forsaking it, hope that comes from looking not to ourselves, not to our works, not to our record, but to Jesus Christ our Savior, whose blood was sufficient once for all to atone for all of our sins so that we might be reconciled to you and have peace with you. Would you cause us to abound in hope as you pour your Spirit into our hearts, as we trust in you, as we treasure Christ our Savior? Amen. Let's stand.

5-day reading plan

This week we walk from Israel's crisis of unfaithfulness through repentance to God's merciful restoration, discovering that genuine hope rests not in human reformation but in Christ's perfect atonement.

Monday Malachi 2

Malachi's rebuke of unfaithful priests mirrors Israel's crisis in Ezra 10—both fail to see their own moral culpability, blaming circumstances rather than confronting sin. When we, like Israel, finally recognize that our deepest problem is not external but our own sinful choices, we regain moral agency and therefore hope. The gospel invites us into this clarity: we are not victims of fate but responsible agents capable of genuine repentance.

Tuesday Romans 3:23

Paul's declaration that all have sinned does not end in despair but opens the door to confession and restoration—a door Ezra's generation walked through. We share their universal human condition, yet the scandal of the gospel is that sin, the most serious problem we face, is precisely the one problem God has permanently solved. What makes sin hopeful is not its triviality but its forgivability through Christ.

Wednesday Hebrews 10:4

The Old Testament sacrifices could never make sinners perfect—they pointed forward to what animal blood could not achieve. Israel's willingness to put away their pagan spouses showed genuine repentance, yet even their rigorous obedience could not cleanse their guilt. We too discover that our moral effort, however earnest, cannot transform us; only Christ's perfect sacrifice, offered once for all, accomplishes the transformation our hearts long for.

Thursday Isaiah 43:25

God's proclamation that He blots out transgressions for His own sake reveals the staggering nature of forgiveness—it is not earned by our repentance but granted by God's character. Ezra 10 shows the severity of sin's consequences, yet God's mercy toward a repentant people demonstrates that even when His justice demands response, His grace moves to restore. In Christ, we witness this marvel made concrete: wrath turned away, guilt removed, fellowship restored.

Friday Nehemiah 10:10

Here, years after Ezra's painful reforms, we see transformed people—including priests who had compromised—now leading covenant renewal with genuine devotion. Their restoration from guilty sinners to faithful servants of God demonstrates that change is not theoretical but actual, wrought by God's grace. As we look to our own struggles with sin, the same God who remade Israel's leaders invites us into genuine reformation, not by our strength alone but by His Spirit working in us to will and to work for His good pleasure.
