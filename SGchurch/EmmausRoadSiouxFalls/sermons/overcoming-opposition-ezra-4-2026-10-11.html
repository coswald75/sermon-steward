1 · Introduces a secular framework (the emotional cycle of change) as an analogy for understanding the pattern of enthusiasm, disillusionment, and perseverance that will be demonstrated in Ezra 4

Back in the late '70s, 2 business thinkers laid out a chart that they called the emotional cycle of change. In their view, there is a predictable emotional path people tend to go through when they begin anything new. So here it is quickly, and maybe this will track onto your own experience. It begins with uninformed optimism. This is by far the most common, uh, starting point for anybody, and I hear it all the time. This is going to be great. This job is perfect. What could go wrong? All benefits are in view and no cost has been seen yet. But upon starting the thing, you enter stage 2 pretty quickly, which they call informed pessimism. Reality begins to set in. The complexities and obstacles become apparent or revealed. There is— you start saying, okay, this is different and a little bit harder than I anticipated. And eventually you land at a 3rd place, which they call the crisis of meaning. It's very dramatic. Why am I doing this? This is the low point. This is a point of decision. Will I continue forward or bail? If you quit, you begin though right back at phase 1 in uninformed optimism, about whatever it is you think is better than what you're currently going through. But if you stay, you move to what they call informed optimism. You press on and change, and this begins to set in. This is hard, but I can see how this could work. You begin to understand the costs and the benefits, and you begin seeing progress. And eventually you get to this stage of success where you become established. You start to develop mastery in whatever you've been working towards, and you get a handle on whatever it is that you're doing.

14 · Applies the theological principle to the listener's life by shifting the focus from external circumstances to internal heart response, framing opposition as a test of faith that requires self-examination

18 · Establishes the theological stakes of the conflict—the people of the land represent the same syncretistic idolatry that caused the exile, and the encounter is a test of whether Israel will maintain distinctiveness or compromise—and applies this to the contemporary church's temptation toward religious pluralism

Such a battle is to be fought in every generation, not least in a world of multi-faith, of syncretism, and widespread biblical ignorance. We live in that world today, and that age-old temptation to dilute the gospel of Jesus Christ in the name of niceness, to mix it with the various beliefs of the world under the banner of tolerance is ever-present. The American church over the last 60 years has bought the lie of religious pluralism. You can have Jesus, that's good for you. We love Jesus, but he's just one of the, the many equal and valid faiths. So don't get all judgmental or intolerant, or we will oppose you. My friends, the gospel of Christ and the worship of the triune God are offensive and sin— to a sinful and rebellious world.

They refused to compromise, and still the work ceased. We don't have much here in this chapter to inspire us towards hope. The enemies in the land have tried to infiltrate, they've discouraged, they've sought to intimidate, and the result is that the building is stopped. And the enemy opposition, as we've said, it's going to keep on. It's ongoing through the entire story. However, even here in Ezra 4, we see a seed of hope. Look again at verse 24. Then the work of the house of God that is in Jerusalem stopped, and it ceased until the 2nd year of the reign of Darius, king of Persia. Worlds change and can change in a single word: until. Until. It gives us a glimpse into what God is up to. The opposition will not be final. It will not thwart His work. Just like their exile, what felt like a hopeless situation, in reality it had an end date. The opposition we face will only last as long as the sovereign Lord allows to accomplish His greater purposes. The Lord will not abandon us. He will complete His work in us. And my friends, the reality that is in this life, we will face that opposition. Peter warned all Christians in 1 Peter 4 when he says, beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you, as if something strange were happening to you.

23 · Establishes the cross and resurrection as the ultimate demonstration that God accomplishes His greatest victories through opposition, using John 16:33 to ground the command to 'take heart' in Christ's completed victory over all enemies

So we ought not to be shocked. We must not also despair. The greatest opposition one could imagine, the murder of the innocent Son of God. If you, you were there that day and saw Jesus crucified, dead, and buried, you go to bed that night thinking, where is the hope here? And yet Sunday morning comes. It became the means— that death, that highest form of opposition, becomes the means through which God accomplished the greatest victory imaginable, the, the salvation of sinners like you and me, and the resurrection triumph of Christ the King. Remember that Jesus said to his disciples just before he goes to the cross in John 16, I have said these things to you, that in me you may have peace. In the world you will have tribulation. You will face opposition. There will be enemies without and within. But take heart, I have overcome the world. In this world, Jesus says, you will experience all of this. But our Lord Jesus commands you to take heart. Why? Because he and he alone has overcome the world. At the cross of Christ, he, he crushed the head of the dragon, that great enemy that seeks to devour us. He dealt decisively with that enemy that still is within you, our sin and our guilt, and he conquered our final enemy, death itself. So what, my friends, what can actually separate you from the love of Christ? Nothing.

24 · Applies the theological claim directly to believers currently facing opposition, offering the promise of peace grounded in Christ's victory and the assurance that neither life nor death can separate them from Christ

Take heart, take heart, for our Lord Jesus has overcome the world. And do you see why Jesus did that for you? To what end? That you might have peace. So Christian, if you, if you're in the throes of opposition today, you can have peace knowing that for you to live is Christ, and to die is gain.

25 · Concludes the sermon with 2 Corinthians 4:16-18, reframing present opposition as 'light and momentary' in light of eternal glory, and calling the congregation to fix their eyes on the unseen eternal realities rather than present suffering

So we do not lose heart. Though our outer self is wasting away, our inner self is being renewed day by day. For this light, momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison, as we look not to the things that are seen but to the things that are unseen. For the things that are seen are transient, but the things that are unseen are eternal.

26 · Closes with pastoral prayer asking God to make Christ visible to the congregation as their hope and peace in the midst of opposition, emphasizing the congregation's continual need for God's presence

Let's pray. Oh Lord, thank you that you have overcome all opposition. Thank you that in you we can have peace. In you we can have hope no matter what we are facing this morning, no matter what trials or oppositions, enemies without, and our own sinful heart. Oh God, these things are no obstacle for you. So God, I pray for, for us this morning. Would you make Christ Oh, hide Him not from us. Make Christ so visible to us, His glory, that He is our hope, that He is good, that You are good to us. God, we need that this morning. We need that tomorrow morning. We need that Thursday morning. We need that every hour, O God. We need You. So meet us, O God. Thank you that you have declared to us all that we need, that you are with us because of Christ. It's in his name we pray. Amen. Please stand.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace opposition's origin, its dual assault on the church, and the gospel's promise that God's purposes cannot be thwarted.

Monday Ephesians 6:12

Paul reminds us that our struggle is not against flesh and blood but against spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms. The opposition the returned exiles faced—initially appearing as political and social resistance—reveals a deeper reality: Satan's active hostility against God's redemptive work. We face the same enemy today, though often disguised in temporal forms.

Tuesday 1 Peter 5:8

Peter describes the devil as a roaring lion seeking someone to devour, while our own flesh works in alliance with external temptation. The Ezra 4 adversaries exploited both external pressure and internal fear in the remnant community. We must remain vigilant to how external enemies and our own vulnerabilities conspire against us—this dual threat is why opposition cuts so deeply.

Wednesday Galatians 5:17

Paul teaches that the flesh and Spirit are in constant conflict within the regenerate heart. The adversaries in Ezra succeeded partly because they appealed to the fears and compromises already present within God's people. Our redemption in Christ does not eliminate this internal struggle; rather, grace empowers us to recognize and resist the flesh's capitulation to external pressure.

Thursday 2 Corinthians 4:4

The god of this age blinds the minds of unbelievers to prevent them from seeing the light of the gospel of Christ's glory. The Ezra 4 adversaries sought to integrate themselves into temple worship, diluting its exclusivity. Today's opposition often comes dressed as tolerance and inclusion, tempting us to blur the boundary between Christ and competing religious claims. We must cherish and defend the exclusivity of Christ's saving work.

Friday John 16:33

Jesus tells His disciples that in the world they will have tribulation, yet He has overcome the world. The cross—history's greatest opposition to God's redemptive plan—became the instrument of our salvation and Satan's defeat. When opposition confronts us, we remember that Christ has already secured the victory; our present struggles are not defeats but confirmations that we belong to the One who has already won.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. When the adversaries approached the returned exiles in Ezra 4:2, they presented themselves as allies who wanted to help rebuild the temple. What made their offer deceptive, and what does this reveal about how opposition often disguises itself in our own lives?
    Ezra 4:2
    → Can you think of a time when something that looked like help or unity actually threatened what you believed or valued?
  2. The exiles had to draw a clear line and say no to the adversaries' participation (Ezra 4:3). What was really at stake for them in maintaining that distinction, and why couldn't they simply compromise and include everyone in the work?
    Ezra 4:3; unit #18
    → How does this connect to what the sermon identified as the deeper issue—the exclusivity of worship and the distinctiveness of Israel's faith?
  3. The sermon taught that opposition reveals two enemies: one from without and one from within (Galatians 5:17). As the exiles faced external adversaries seeking to stop the work, what internal vulnerabilities might those adversaries have exploited—and what do you observe about your own heart in times of external pressure?
    Galatians 5:17; 1 Peter 5:8
  4. How does the sermon's claim that 'opposition brings clarity' help us understand why the exiles had to define so sharply what they believed about their calling and their God? What clarity did opposition force them to articulate?
    → What convictions about Christ and His kingdom does opposition tend to clarify or sharpen in our own lives as believers?
  5. The sermon presents the cross of Christ as the supreme victory accomplished *through* the very opposition meant to destroy God's purposes (John 16:33). How does this reality reshape the way we should think about opposition we face today—not as accidents or defeats, but as the arena where God works?
    John 16:33; 2 Corinthians 4:16-18
    → When you're facing opposition in your faith or witness this week, how might meditating on Christ's victory change the way you respond?
  6. The exiles experienced a painful season of opposition and delay, yet the sermon emphasizes that opposition is 'not eternal'—God's purposes will ultimately prevail. What does it mean for us, corporately as a church, to hold both the reality of present pain and the certainty of God's final victory at the same time?
    1 Peter 4:12
