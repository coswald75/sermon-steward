Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through the Jerusalem Council's defense of the gospel—from the peril of doctrinal error, through the apostolic testimony that faith alone saves, to the joyful proclamation that Jesus is enough.

Monday Acts 2

The Spirit's outpouring at Pentecost established the church's foundation: the proclamation that Jesus alone is Lord and Savior (Acts 2:36). Acts 15 defends this same gospel against those who would add circumcision and the law—a seemingly small addition that would have fractured the church and robbed believers of the assurance that Christ's work is complete. We see how doctrine matters most when the stakes are highest.

Tuesday Acts 13-14

Paul and Barnabas's missionary journeys (Acts 13-14) brought Gentiles into faith in Christ without the law—yet false teachers demanded circumcision anyway. This confrontation forced the church to answer: Does ethnicity, ritual, or obedience to Moses' law define God's people, or does faith in Christ alone? The council's answer clarified that the church is not a reformed Judaism but a new covenant people, united not by bloodline or legal observance but by grace.

Wednesday 1 Corinthians 15:7

Paul lists James as a witness to the risen Christ, establishing his authority in the Jerusalem Council (1 Corinthians 15:7). James's own testimony upheld the council's decision: salvation comes through faith in Christ, not through the works of the law. We see in the apostolic witness across cultures and backgrounds the same conviction—that the risen Jesus, not human achievement or ancestry, is the foundation of our belonging to God.

Thursday Amos 9

James quoted Amos 9 to show that God's prophetic word always intended the inclusion of the Gentiles in His covenant people—not as subordinates keeping the law, but as coequal heirs of the promise. When the false teachers demanded circumcision, they contradicted God's own revealed purpose and treated Christ's finished work as insufficient. Every attempt to add human works to the gospel denies the sufficiency of what Jesus accomplished on the cross.

Friday Acts 16

Acts 16 shows the gospel freed from the burden of the law spreading freely to Asia and Europe, with Paul baptizing believers into Christ alone without any demand for circumcision or legal observance. The joy that fills Acts 16—the jailer's household saved, the slave girl liberated, believers rejoicing—flows directly from the Jerusalem Council's affirmation that faith in Christ is all God requires. We, too, are invited into this freedom: to rest in his finished work and to proclaim the same liberating truth to all nations.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What was the specific controversy that forced the Jerusalem Council to convene, and why did the false teachers believe their requirement was necessary for salvation?
    Acts 15:1-5
    → How might their insistence on circumcision and the law have sounded reasonable to Jewish believers at the time?
  2. Peter, Paul, and Barnabas all testified that God makes no distinction between Jews and Gentiles in salvation. What does this reveal about how the gospel fundamentally reshapes our understanding of who belongs to God's people?
    Acts 15:7-9
    → How might this have challenged the assumptions of Jewish believers in ways similar to how the gospel challenges our own assumptions today?
  3. According to the sermon and the council's decision, what happens theologically when we add anything—even good things like obedience to the law—to faith in Christ as a requirement for salvation?
    Acts 15:10-11
    → Can you think of ways this same error shows up in our own thinking about what God requires from us?
  4. James quoted Amos 9 to show that God's plan all along was to include Gentiles in His people through faith alone. What does this reveal about the continuity of God's purposes from the Old Testament through the gospel?
    Acts 15:13-18
    → How does knowing that salvation by grace alone was always God's plan affect the way you think about your own acceptance with Him?
  5. The sermon emphasizes that the application of this passage is simply to rejoice that Jesus is enough. What makes it difficult for us to rest in the sufficiency of Christ's finished work, and what tends to tempt us to add our own effort or obedience as part of our standing before God?
    → Where do you find yourself most inclined to believe that Christ plus something else is what God requires?
  6. How should the gospel truth that Christ was 'pierced, bloodied, and made unclean' on our behalf reshape the way we approach worship together this week, particularly as we gather around the table for communion?
    → What would it look like for you to respond with genuine gratitude rather than mere obligation?
