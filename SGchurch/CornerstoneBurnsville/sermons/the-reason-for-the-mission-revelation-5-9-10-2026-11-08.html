Don't try to find your identity or your sense of worth horizontally. In Christ, you are a beloved child of God. You are more loved than you'll ever know, and that will never change.

40 · The preacher turns to the scope of the ransom, expositing the phrase 'from every tribe and language and people and nation' to show the universal reach of Christ's atonement

And now, as the text continues, we need to ask this question: Who are these people who were ransomed for God? The text tells us, you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation. No, Christ's sacrifice was not just for one nation or for a few tribes. No, by his blood he ransomed a multitude from every people group on earth.

41 · The preacher articulates the ecclesiological implication of the multi-ethnic ransom: Christ is forming diverse peoples into one body, reversing the division that sin created

And all this diverse group of people, he's actually forming them and making them into one body, his church. And that's what makes the church so unique and so beautiful. You know, where sin brought division because of differences in language, Christ now fully brings unity by his blood. Where sin brought war, hatred, murder because of differences in ethnicity, Christ now brings true peace, reconciliation, and forgiveness because of his sacrificial death on the cross on our behalf.

42 · The preacher applies the doctrine of the multi-ethnic church to concrete church relationships, addressing prejudice and unforgiveness

And now this has significant implications for how we live as a church and how we relate to one another as a community of brothers and sisters in Christ. I mean, there is no room to look down upon anyone just because they are different from us. You know, if God has accepted them in Christ, we dare not do anything less. And even when a brother or a sister sins against us, which will happen, if God has forgiven them in Christ, then we dare not refuse to extend the same grace and forgiveness that we have also received in Christ.

43 · The preacher articulates the doctrine of definite atonement (limited atonement), arguing from the past-tense language of the text that Christ's death effectively ransomed a specific people, not merely made salvation possible

And furthermore, when we consider the sacrificial death of Christ, we know that Christ will have This great multitude of people from every tribe and language and people and nation, because his death was effective. Notice again, the text doesn't say that he potentially ransomed a people for God. No, it doesn't say that. He actually did ransom them. He fully and effectively paid the ransom price with his own blood. And dear church, believe you me, Christ will have what he paid for in full.

Just for a moment, let's, let's listen to John 10:14-16. Jesus spoke these words. He said, I am the Good Shepherd, and I know my own, and my own know me, even as the Father knows me and I know the Father. And I lay down my life for the sheep. Church, that is why Christ is worthy to take the scroll and to open its seals. He is the only one able to execute or to carry out God's plan, not only for judgment, But even more so for redemption, because His death was effective to ransom a people for Himself. Christ will receive the full reward for His suffering. He will have every single sheep He died to save, that He died to ransom.

45 · The preacher applies the doctrine of definite atonement to evangelism, showing that while we don't know who the elect are, we can evangelize with confidence knowing that Christ's sheep will respond to the gospel when they hear it

Now, dear saints, that is why we evangelize. I mean, we do not know who will receive the gospel message and who will reject it. But what we do know is that if we go out and share this glorious news of Jesus with our neighbors, our coworkers, our family members, his sheep will sooner or later hear his voice and they will follow him.

And saints, that is why we plant churches. That is why my family and I and the Leslies and the Geletas from Ethiopia. That is why we moved to Nairobi to plant a church. I mean, we have no power to save anyone. We have no power to rescue people from sin and hell. We have no power to deliver people from darkness and to bring them into God's light. We have no power to change people's hearts or transform their lives so that they stop living for themselves and start living for the glory of God. We cannot do any of those things.

But what we do know is that this gospel is the power of God for salvation. We know that Christ has ransomed people for God from every tribe and tongue and people and nation, and he will have them, every single one of them, into his fold. And we know that he is pleased to use weak powerless redeemed sinners like you and me to go out and to get those sheep through the preaching of the gospel. And so now we go and we evangelize for them. We plant churches for them. We engage in the mission for them. I mean, we don't know who they all are, but Christ knows.

And when you pray, even pray for us in Nairobi. Pray that the word of Christ will speed ahead. And be glorified through the salvation of many souls. Pray for us too, that we will not put our confidence in ourselves, our wisdom, our strategies, our gifting, but rather place our confidence in the Christ who was slain and his gospel, who alone is worthy to take the scroll and able to bring to pass God's full plan of redemption. Because He died and rose again to save sinners.

49 · The preacher signals the final movement of the sermon, introducing the eschatological hope contained in Revelation 5:10

Finally, we see that Christ not only died to ransom us, but He also made us a kingdom and priests to our God, and we shall reign on the earth.

50 · The preacher traces the creation mandate to rule back to Genesis, showing that humanity's original purpose was to exercise dominion under God's authority, but that sin corrupted this purpose

I mean, when God created humanity in Genesis 1, He created us in His image. And part of what, part of what that means is that He made us to rule over His creation in submission to Him as Supreme King. However, when Adam and Eve fell, when they sinned, humanity was unable to rule as God intended. I mean, any rule that we exercise even right now is tainted by sin, corrupted by sin, and not done for the glory of God but for the glory of self.

51 · The preacher identifies Christ as the last Adam who perfectly fulfilled the human vocation, receiving dominion and a kingdom

But Christ, the last Adam, he perfectly obeyed his Father. And in, in Daniel chapter 7, we are told that he received dominion, glory, and a kingdom. We just read Psalm 8 this morning talking about man. You have made him a little lower than the angels, and you have crowned him with glory and honor, that God has placed all things under his feet. Yes, when Adam failed and the rest of us failed, guess who fulfilled that promise? Christ Himself. Christ, the last Adam.

52 · The preacher expounds the incarnation and exaltation of Christ, emphasizing the stunning reality that a human being now sits at God's right hand

We are told in Hebrews that He was made a little lower than the angels, and He did that because of the suffering of death, crowned with glory and honor so that He might taste death for everyone. So He became man. So that He could die as a man in our place. But now He rose again from the dead. He was exalted to the right hand of God the Father. And just imagine for a moment. Yes, this is Christ Himself seated at the right hand of God the Father. Yes, He is God from all eternity, the second person of the Trinity. But don't forget this. That Christ also became man, that He is the Son of Man. And the one who is seated at the right hand of God is actually one of us.

53 · The preacher articulates the eschatological hope: believers will share in Christ's rule and have priestly access to God

He, He sits at the highest position in the entire universe, reigning and ruling over all creation. And now we who belong to Christ, we who belong to Him, we get to share in His rule. We get to share in His kingdom. He has made us a kingdom and we shall reign with Him. Not only that, He also made us priests to God. And that means that we have full access to our God through Christ Jesus.

Now, dear saints, when we think about the mission of the Church, you know, this passage in Revelation 5, it gives us great reason to give ourselves fully to the mission. Christ was found worthy to take the scroll, and therefore he has all authority to bring to pass all of God's promises, all of his purposes, and all of his plans for judgment and final redemption and for the renewal of creation. And this same Christ, he is the one who ransomed a people for God from every tribe and tongue and people and nation. And now those 2 realities, they guarantee the success of the mission. They guarantee that our prayers for the mission will be heard and answered because ultimately this is His mission. He is more invested in the mission than any one of us will ever be.

55 · The preacher concludes by inviting the congregation to join the heavenly worship depicted in the passage, turning the exposition into doxology

And now we can also join the 4 living creatures, the 24 elders, the angels, and all of creation in worshipping the one who sits on the throne and the Lamb and declare that he alone is worthy.

Let us pray. Indeed, Father, we thank you for your truth and for your word this morning, that Christ indeed is worthy to take the scroll and he is the one who was slain. Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Revelation 5:1-5, John weeps because no one is found worthy to open the scroll. What do you think the scroll represents, and why does its remaining sealed matter so much—not just for heaven, but for our lives right now?
    Revelation 5:1-5; Daniel 12:4
    → How would our understanding of suffering, prayer, or God's promises change if we believed no one could open it?
  2. The sermon identifies several catastrophic consequences if the scroll remained sealed—no final judgment, no resurrection, no new creation, Christian hope limited to this life. Which of these hits you most personally, and why?
    Revelation 5:1-14
  3. When John is told that 'the Lion of the tribe of Judah' has overcome and is worthy to open the scroll, the reader expects a warrior. Instead, he sees a Lamb 'standing as if slain.' What does this juxtaposition—Lion and Lamb, strength and sacrifice—tell us about the nature of Christ's victory?
    Revelation 5:5-6
    → How does this reshape what 'power' or 'worthiness' actually means in God's economy?
  4. The sermon emphasizes that Christ's ransom was 'effective'—that through His death He has already purchased a people from every tribe, language, people, and nation. What does it mean practically that this isn't a future promise but an accomplished fact?
    Revelation 5:9-10; Romans 8:28
    → How should that change the way we approach evangelism and church planting—are we creating the outcome, or announcing one that's already secured?
  5. The sermon names a fallen condition: we can drift into thinking that strategic planning, demographic analysis, or human effort is what makes the mission succeed. How does understanding Christ's worthiness and the effectiveness of His ransom free us from that subtle burden?
    Revelation 5:9; 2 Corinthians 5:21
    → What might change in how we pray, plan, or labor if we're resting on Christ's finished work rather than our own performance?
  6. Because Christ has ransomed a people and will certainly receive the full reward for His suffering, the sermon says we can 'give ourselves fully to mission with confidence.' How does the gospel of Christ's atoning death—His bearing our penalty and transferring our ownership from sin to God—become the actual fuel for missionary courage and generosity?
    Revelation 5:9-10; Hebrews 2:9
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace Christ's worthiness to execute God's redemptive plan from the cosmic significance of the sealed scroll through the efficacy of His atoning death to the guaranteed gathering of His ransomed people, grounding our confidence in mission.

Monday Daniel 7:13-14

Daniel's vision of the Son of Man receiving dominion, glory, and a kingdom that will never pass away establishes the cosmic scope of Christ's authority—the very authority needed to open the sealed scroll and govern all history toward redemption. As we see Christ receiving this kingdom from the Ancient of Days, we recognize that His worthiness flows not from human achievement but from His eternal station as God's Son, ruling over all nations and peoples.

Tuesday 2 Corinthians 5:21

Paul's declaration that Christ became sin for us and bore God's judgment as our substitute reveals the specific, substitutionary nature of Christ's atoning work—the very act that qualifies Him as worthy to open the scroll. In His sinless life becoming our sin-bearer, Christ satisfied divine justice and opened the way for us to be counted righteous, demonstrating that His ransom was not symbolic but efficacious, purchased with His own blood.

Wednesday John 10:14-16

Christ's declaration that He knows His sheep and will gather them from other folds into one flock under one Shepherd shows that His atoning work produces a specific, unified result—not scattered individuals but a people belonging to one shepherd across all nations and languages. The gospel does not leave us fragmented; it draws us together as Christ's corporate possession, a living sign that His ransom transcends every human barrier.

Thursday Romans 8:28

Paul's affirmation that God works all things together for good for those who love Him depends entirely on the truth that the scroll has been opened and a sovereign ruler governs history for the church's ultimate benefit. Without Christ's worthiness and His effective authority over creation, suffering becomes arbitrary chaos rather than a thread in God's redemptive tapestry—which is why the Lamb who was slain is the answer to every aching heart.

Friday Hebrews 2:9

The writer of Hebrews shows us Jesus crowned with glory and honor because of the suffering of death, seated now in a position where His dominion cannot be reversed or thwarted—a reality that frees us to pour ourselves into evangelism, church planting, and mission without anxiety about failure or fruitlessness. Because Christ's triumph is accomplished and guaranteed, we engage in the work He calls us to with the certainty that He will gather His ransomed people from every nation, and our labor in the gospel is never in vain.
