Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace God's sovereign providence through three conversions that shattered social categories and established Europe's first church, learning that nothing—not demons, injustice, nor human indifference—can thwart his redemptive purposes.

Monday Isaiah 46:9-10

Isaiah declares that God 'makes known the end from the beginning, and from ancient times, what is still to come'—a vision of absolute divine governance over all history. The God who spoke these words is the same God orchestrating Lydia's journey to the riverside, the demon-possessed girl's encounter with Paul, and the jailer's midnight earthquake. In meditating on these verses, we grasp that God's providence is not reactive but intentional: he accomplishes his purposes with the certainty of one who has already seen the end.

Tuesday Acts 10 (Cornelius's household)

In Acts 10, God uses a vision, an angel, and a rooftop prayer to bring Cornelius—a Gentile centurion—and Peter together so that the gospel might leap the barrier of Jewish law. Similarly, in Acts 16, God employs a wealthy businesswoman's prayer gathering, a demon's testimony, and a midnight earthquake to draw three people from utterly different social worlds into his kingdom. The pattern reveals that God's redemptive artistry is infinite: he meets each person where they are, using circumstances and agents suited precisely to draw that person's heart to Christ.

Wednesday 2 Corinthians 11:25

Paul himself would endure imprisonments, beatings, and perils—yet each suffering became a conduit for gospel advance rather than an obstacle to it. His jailing in Philippi appears, at first, to be a catastrophic reversal; yet it becomes the very means by which the jailer and his household hear the gospel and are saved. As we consider Paul's lifelong pattern of suffering and its fruit, we are struck with awe: God's purposes cannot be imprisoned, and our afflictions—however unjust—are held within his larger redemptive designs.

Thursday Acts 20 (implied reference to the same journey taking 5 days)

The very circumstances that seemed to derail Paul's mission—the greed of the slave girl's owners, the magistrates' injustice, the Roman imprisonment—were permitted by God's sovereign will precisely because they served his redemptive ends. God did not cause or endorse the sin; he permitted it and worked through it to accomplish what no smooth, unimpeded journey could have achieved. This mystery humbles us: our God is great enough to use human wickedness as a servant to his gracious purposes, while remaining entirely holy and just.

Friday Acts 18 (Crispus's household, anticipated)

Lydia the merchant, the enslaved oracle, and the jailer—together with their households—form the nucleus of Europe's first church: a society that transcends the hierarchies of gender, economics, and status that ruled the pagan world. This pattern of God gathering the 'most unlikely' people into his redeemed family (as seen again in Corinth and throughout Scripture) is not incidental to the gospel's power but central to its proof. When we see the body of Christ united across lines that human sinfulness would keep sundered, we witness the only community on earth worthy of worship—and we are emboldened to declare that this Christ alone is Lord.
