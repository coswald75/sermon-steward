Theological claims· 13
  1. The book of Ruth answers the question posed by Judges: will a worthy, sacrificial king emerge to redeem God's people from their brokenness? unit #7
  2. God answers Israel's need for a redeemer through Boaz, who pictures the worthy king displaying steadfast love and provision. unit #8
  3. Mr. So-and-so refused redemption because it required radical personal cost without personal benefit—he chose self-interest over sacrificial love. unit #14
  4. Boaz's redemption is costly and selfless grace enacted—he sacrifices his own security and wealth to redeem another's future without personal benefit. unit #16
  5. Boaz answers the need for a redeemer-king from Judges, but he points beyond himself to David and ultimately to Jesus, the complete fulfillment. unit #17
  6. Jesus is the greater Boaz who paid the ultimate redemptive price by emptying himself completely to secure true redemption for his people through his death and resurrection. unit #18
  7. Christ's costly redemption is powerful enough to transform any past and invite anyone stuck in bondage into new identity through his precious blood. unit #20
  8. Like Ruth, strangers to the covenant have been brought near by Christ's blood and are invited to fall into his grace. unit #21
  9. Christ was cut off and blotted out in our place so that we would never be cut off, securing for us the great reward of eternal inheritance with him. unit #26
  10. The three-part blessing pronounced over Boaz and Ruth finds complete fulfillment in Jesus Christ and his Church. unit #28
  11. The blessing fulfilled in Christ now unfolds upon believers as accomplished and secured redemption—giving present fellowship and future inheritance based on Christ's work, not our performance. unit #29
  12. Believers live in tension between tasting redemption now in part and waiting for full future realization—maintaining this exile mindset prevents both worldly attachment and hopeless despair. unit #30
  13. Christ's costly, sacrificial love as the greater Boaz is the foundation of Christian hope. unit #31

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace redemption's pattern from Israel's need through Boaz's costly sacrifice to Christ's ultimate work, learning what true redemption costs and what it secures for us.

Monday Judges (referenced throughout sermon as establishing Israel's need for a redeemer-king)

Judges ends in moral chaos—"everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (21:25)—leaving God's people desperate for a leader of integrity and steadfast love. As we enter Ruth's narrative this week, we see that God does not leave His people without hope; He raises up Boaz as a picture of the redemptive king who will eventually come in Christ. This pattern teaches us that our deepest need is not primarily material but spiritual: we need a Redeemer whose character is trustworthy and whose love is sacrificial.

Tuesday Ruth 2 (Boaz's initial display of steadfast love and provision)

In Ruth 2, Boaz notices Ruth gleaning in his field and moves with intentional kindness—commanding his workers to leave extra grain and ensuring her safety without drawing attention to his generosity. This is the character of a true redeemer: he notices the vulnerable, acts with mercy, and provides without demanding recognition or return. We see in Boaz the portrait of a leader shaped by covenant love, prefiguring Christ, who sees our spiritual poverty and provides redemption with extravagant grace.

Wednesday Philippians 2 (Christ's self-emptying sacrifice as the pattern of redemptive love)

Paul writes that Christ "emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" and "humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross" (Philippians 2:7-8). Where Boaz's redemption cost him land and security, Christ's redemption cost Him His divine prerogatives, His comfort, and ultimately His life—yet He paid this price willingly to bring strangers and outcasts into His family. The gospel humbles us as we grasp that our Redeemer's love refused no sacrifice to make us His own.

Thursday Ephesians 2 (strangers brought near by Christ's blood into the covenant family)

Ephesians 2:13 declares, "But now in Christ Jesus you who once were far off have been brought near by the blood of Christ." Ruth, a Moabite outsider, enters Israel's covenant through Boaz's redemptive act; we, who were spiritually alienated and powerless, are grafted into God's family through Christ's redemptive work. The barrier that once separated us from God's people and God's promises is torn down, not by our merit but by our Redeemer's blood—we are no longer strangers but beloved family, inheritors of blessing.

Friday Revelation 3:5 (Christ's promise to the overcomer: never to be blotted out, eternal security in Him)

Jesus promises, "The one who conquers will be clothed thus in white garments, and I will never blot his name out of the book of life" (Revelation 3:5). Boaz secured Ruth's future by stepping into the kinsman's place; Christ secured our eternal future by being cut off in our place—bearing the judgment we deserved so we would never face separation from God. This security, purchased at infinite cost, invites us to live this week with grateful confidence: our names are written in heaven, our inheritance is guaranteed, and our Redeemer's love will never fail us.
