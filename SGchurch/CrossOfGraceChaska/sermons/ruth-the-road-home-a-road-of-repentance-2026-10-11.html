Theological claims· 10
  1. The author's purpose is not to establish a direct connection between compromise and consequence, but to reveal God's longing for Naomi to return home. unit #4
  2. God gave us biblical narrative to shape our hearts to long after what his heart longs for, and Ruth's story should provoke us to want Naomi to return home. unit #6
  3. Naomi's geographical return to Bethlehem is simultaneously a spiritual return to God, making her journey the road of repentance. unit #10
  4. Repentance is not only how we come to faith in Christ but the ongoing road we walk throughout our entire Christian life. unit #11
  5. God's goodness and covenantal faithfulness move him to teach sinners the way back home at the cost of himself. unit #14
  6. Repentance is not about perfection but about direction — it is a messy process where we take steps toward God while our hearts and minds are not yet fully settled. unit #19
  7. Ruth's costly personal decision to trust Yahweh fulfills Jesus's promise in Mark 10 that those who lose everything for the gospel will be exalted, as God canonized her story and placed her in the Messianic line. unit #25
  8. God exalts the marginalized who make a personal risky decision to trust him, and no one can ride the coattails of another's repentance. unit #26
  9. Naomi's wrestling with her grief by invoking God's names El Shaddai and Yahweh shows she is fighting to reconcile God's power and goodness with her suffering, and God will prove himself faithful through the grief. unit #33
  10. Jesus is the friend who sticks closer than a brother and will not give up on us until he has brought us safely home, ensuring that repentance is never walked alone. unit #37

That is, while you see that Naomi, she is struggling front and center in the story, you can't help but see these little bits within the story that is to cue us off to the fact that God is working behind the scenes to prove his faithfulness. It's subtle. It's a subtle cue, but it should start creating expectation in the heart of the reader.

36 · Expounds how Ruth (whose name means friend) embodies and reflects God's covenant faithfulness to Naomi, showing that God's provision of Ruth is both literal companionship and a demonstration of God's character

And here one aspect of Naomi's return home that's sometimes even missed is the fact that Naomi is not alone. Verse 22, so Naomi returned. She shooed, if you remember. She shooed and Ruth the Moabite daughter-in-law with her. You see, even for the journey back home God grants Naomi a friend in Ruth. The Hebrew Ruth actually means friend. And so, here Ruth is a new convert and she is standing with Naomi center stage in this particular story, but there she stands, if you will, embodying and reflecting the faithfulness of God. God is working behind the scenes certainly, but what you see is Ruth and Naomi and what Ruth is reflecting and portraying is something of God's own character to Naomi. Naomi is struggling with God and, if you will, something of God's own covenantal love has just scooted right up next to Naomi. There is Ruth being a faithful friend. A beautiful demonstration of God's own kindness and faithfulness to Naomi.

37 · Applies the principle of Ruth as friend to Jesus as the ultimate friend who accompanies believers through repentance, synthesizing the sermon's argument that God never abandons us on the journey home

Folks, the same is true for you. You have a friend, a friend who sticks closer than a brother. You have a friend who is not ashamed to call you brother or sister. He's not too busy. He's not too preoccupied. He's never on vacation. For those in Christ, Jesus declares over us, I no longer call you servants, but I call you friends. Folks, repentance is never walked alone. You have a friend. His name is El Shaddai. His name is Yahweh. And he will not give up on you until he has brought you safely home.

38 · Summarizes the five observations about repentance and concludes with the hope that believers never walk the road of repentance alone because Jesus is their friend

So, yes, repentance begins with a realization of God's goodness. It will be a messy process. It must be a personal decision. It will be a fight of faith, but you can take hope. We will never walk this road alone. Why? Because we have a true friend in Jesus.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Ruth 1:1-5, we see Naomi's family leave Bethlehem during a famine and settle in Moab. What does the text reveal about how this decision unfolds, and what might the author want us to notice about the way compromise often begins—not with a dramatic rebellion, but with what seems like a reasonable step?
    Ruth 1:1-5
    → Can you think of a time when you rationalized a decision to move away from God's design, telling yourself it was temporary or justified by circumstances?
  2. When Naomi hears that the Lord has visited Bethlehem with bread (Ruth 1:6), she decides to return home. What does it mean that God's *goodness*—his provision for his people—is what initiates her repentance, and how does this shape our understanding of what moves us toward returning to God?
    Ruth 1:6; Romans 2:4
  3. Naomi urges Ruth and Orpah to stay in Moab and find security in new husbands (Ruth 1:11-13), but Ruth clings to Naomi and refuses to leave. What is Ruth actually choosing when she says, 'Your people will be my people, and your God my God' (Ruth 1:16-17), and what does her costly personal decision reveal about what repentance sometimes requires of us?
    Ruth 1:16-17; Mark 10:29-31
    → Why do you think the sermon emphasizes that 'no one can ride the coattails of another's repentance'—what does that mean for our own journey home to God?
  4. After Naomi returns to Bethlehem, she tells the women, 'The Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me. I went away full, and the Lord has brought me back empty' (Ruth 1:20-21). How is Naomi's wrestling with God's character—invoking his names El Shaddai and Yahweh even in her grief—itself an act of faith, and what does the sermon suggest God will do through her pain?
    Ruth 1:20-21; Psalm 25:8
  5. The sermon teaches that 'repentance is not about perfection but about direction—it is a messy process where we take steps toward God while our hearts and minds are not yet fully settled.' Where are you currently taking steps toward God even though your emotions or circumstances are not yet resolved, and what would it mean to keep walking that road of repentance?
    Ruth 1:6-22; Colossians 2:6
  6. Throughout Naomi's journey, God works behind the scenes—orchestrating her return, bringing Ruth into her life, and eventually placing Ruth in the Messianic line. How does the truth that 'Jesus is the friend who sticks closer than a brother and will not give up on us until he has brought us safely home' transform the way you view your own road of repentance?
    Ruth 1:1-22; Proverbs 18:24; Hebrews 2:11
    → What difference does it make to know that you are never walking this road alone?
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the road of repentance through five claims: God's longing initiates our return, repentance reshapes our entire Christian life, his covenantal faithfulness moves him to costly grace, our return demands personal risky decision, and Christ himself ensures we never walk alone.

Monday Romans 2:4

Paul reminds us that God's kindness—not his judgment—is what leads us to repentance. Just as the Spirit moved Naomi's heart toward home through the recognition of God's provision during famine, so the gospel operates: it is the sheer goodness of God revealed in Christ's death and resurrection that awakens our hearts to long for restoration. We are compelled homeward not by fear alone, but by the overwhelming grace that says, 'I have paid the price to bring you back.'

Tuesday Colossians 2:6

Paul's exhortation to walk in Christ just as we received him establishes that the same stance of trust that inaugurates our salvation sustains it throughout our days. Repentance is not a one-time event at conversion but the posture we maintain: turning from our own understanding, returning to Christ's lordship, beginning again. The road Naomi walked is the road every believer walks; we are always either drifting toward compromise or turning toward home.

Wednesday Mark 10:29-31

Ruth's abandonment of security, family, and homeland to follow Naomi's God demonstrates the radical trust Jesus later promised would not go unrewarded. Her willingness to lose everything for the gospel—though she did not yet know it was the gospel—positioned her in the very lineage of the Messiah himself. This teaches us that personal, costly decisions to follow Christ, however risky they appear in the moment, are received by a God who exalts the faithful and weaves their stories into his eternal purposes.

Thursday Psalm 25:8

The psalmist declares that God instructs sinners in his way, not because they arrive perfected, but because they are willing to be taught. Naomi's wrestling with God—calling herself 'Mara' even as she returns home—shows us that repentance accommodates grief, doubt, and unresolved pain. We need not wait until our hearts are clean or our minds fully convinced; God meets us in the mess of our turning and teaches us as we move. Each step toward him, however halting, is a step on the road home.

Friday Proverbs 18:24

The proverb about friendship beyond kinship finds its supreme fulfillment in Christ, who proved his fidelity by dying for us while we were yet sinners. Just as Ruth's covenant loyalty to Naomi meant she would not abandon her on the road home, so Jesus walks our repentance with us—not leaving us to navigate compromise and return in isolation. The most liberating truth of the gospel is that we never repent alone; the One who loved us enough to die for us now loves us enough to stay with us until we are safely home.

Sunday-evening family table

The Road Home Looks Different Than We Expected

For the parent

This prompt anchors on Naomi's surprising return to Bethlehem — she went away full and came back empty, yet God was working. Help your family see that repentance often feels like loss before it feels like homecoming, and that's when we need to trust God most. Listen for whether anyone recognizes a time when 'going backward' to God actually felt like moving forward in a new way.

Naomi left her home in Bethlehem because things were hard, and she thought going to Moab would be better. But when she decided to go back home, she said she was returning 'empty' — she'd lost her husband and sons. If you were Naomi, would going back home have felt like a good thing or a scary thing? Why? And what do you think made her decide to go back anyway, even though it was hard?
works for ages 7+ — younger children grasp the emotional journey of Naomi leaving and returning; older kids and teens can engage with the paradox of 'going backward' as spiritual progress
