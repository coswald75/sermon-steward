Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace God's sovereign goodness through pain, from questioning his providence to becoming vessels of his grace—moving from grief to generosity.

Monday Job 2:10

Job's wife urges him to curse God and die—to treat his suffering as evidence of divine abandonment. Job refuses, asking 'Shall we accept good from God, and not trouble?' This cuts to the heart of Naomi's struggle: she has accepted terrible loss from the Lord's hand, yet cannot accept that his goodness continues working beneath the surface. Like Job, we are called to a faith that holds both truths simultaneously—real sorrow and real divine kindness—without letting grief convince us that God has ceased to be good.

Tuesday Psalm 126:5

The psalmist declares, 'Those who sow in tears will reap with songs of joy.' Ruth's gleaning—her humble, faithful work in Boaz's field—occurs in the season of tears, yet becomes the instrument of God's providential care. We tend to expect God's goodness to arrive suddenly and visibly, yet the Scripture shows us that his sovereignty often works quietly through our faithful obedience and through the kindness of his people, gradually transforming our circumstances and our hearts.

Wednesday Deuteronomy 24:19-22

God's gleaning laws were not mere social welfare; they were Israel's liturgy of remembrance, their lived confession that they too were slaves redeemed by God's hand. Boaz's extraordinary kindness to Ruth flows from this deep well: he sees in her the vulnerability God himself had protected in Israel. When we grasp how completely undeserving we are of God's grace, we cannot help but overflow with generosity toward those in need. Our kindness becomes our testimony to the gospel that has saved us.

Thursday Micah 6:8

The prophet calls us to act justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with God—and Boaz is the embodiment of this trio. His generosity to Ruth springs not from duty or social obligation, but from a heart shaped by communion with God. He goes beyond the law's requirements, offering Ruth not mere scraps but a place at his table, water, and protection. This is what a heart captivated by God's grace looks like: it cannot help but overflow toward others, especially those whom the world overlooks.

Friday Luke 12:48

Jesus teaches that 'from everyone who has been given much, much will be demanded.' Boaz's generosity flows from recognizing how much he has been given; Ruth's willingness to glean and receive kindness flows from the same humility—she has nothing to claim and everything to receive. In the gospel we have been given immeasurably more than we deserve, and this transforms us into both joyful givers and grateful receivers. Stinginess and proud self-sufficiency both vanish when we truly comprehend our standing before God's mercy.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What does Naomi mean when she tells the women of Bethlehem, 'I went away full, and the Lord has brought me back empty' (Ruth 1:21)? What is she really saying about how she experiences God's goodness at this moment?
    Ruth 1:20-21
    → How does her statement help us understand why simply telling someone 'God is good' can feel hollow when they're grieving real loss?
  2. Walk through what Ruth actually does in Ruth 2:3-13. What specific choices does she make, and what does her behavior reveal about what kind of faith looks like when you're vulnerable and uncertain about provision?
    Ruth 2:3-13
  3. Boaz tells Ruth, 'May the Lord repay you for what you have done. May you be richly rewarded by the Lord, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge' (Ruth 2:12). What is Boaz actually doing in that moment—not just with his words, but with his actions toward a vulnerable outsider?
    Ruth 2:12
    → How does his kindness toward Ruth connect to what God has done for us in the gospel, and what should that stir in us?
  4. The sermon emphasizes that Israel's gleaning laws (Deuteronomy 24:19-22) were meant to flow from their memory of God's deliverance in the Exodus. Why would remembering what God has done for you produce generosity toward the vulnerable, rather than just produce gratitude?
    Deuteronomy 24:19-22
  5. The sermon identifies a 'fallen condition' in our hearts: stinginess and prideful self-sufficiency both keep us from experiencing and extending grace. Which of those two—refusing to give generously, or refusing to receive kindness humbly—is more difficult for you to recognize in yourself, and why?
    → What would it look like this week to let God's generosity toward you reshape either your giving or your receiving?
  6. Ruth ends by telling Naomi, 'This man is a close relative of ours' (Ruth 3:2, anticipating what we learn later). But before that revelation, Ruth simply receives Boaz's kindness with gratitude and humility. What does Ruth's willingness to receive—without yet knowing the full provision God has planned—teach us about what trusting God's goodness actually requires of us?
    Ruth 2:13
    → How does that kind of trust reshape the way you might grieve a loss or sit with uncertainty in your own life?
Sunday-evening family table

When Hard Things Lead to Kindness

For the parent

This card invites your family to notice how Boaz's kindness toward Ruth flowed from his own understanding of God's goodness. Help your kids see that the hard things we experience—loss, need, struggle—can either make us bitter or generous, depending on whether we trust God's care.

Boaz had suffered loss too, but instead of becoming hard-hearted, he became incredibly kind to Ruth. Think about a time when someone showed you kindness when you were hurting or scared. What do you think made that person kind instead of mean? And how did their kindness change how you felt about God or about being generous to others?
Works for ages 7+. Younger children can share simple examples with parent help; older kids and teens can reflect more deeply on the connection between receiving grace and giving it.
Memory verse this week

Ruth 2:12

The LORD repay you for what you have done. May you be richly rewarded by the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge.

Why this verse: This verse crystallizes the sermon's central claim: God's sovereign goodness works through his people's generous kindness, and those who receive grace learn to extend grace to others. Boaz's blessing over Ruth—invoking God's protection and reward—models the grateful, generous response that flows from understanding God's own overwhelming kindness to the undeserving.
