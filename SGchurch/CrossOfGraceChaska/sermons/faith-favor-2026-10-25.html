Theological claims· 5
  1. Biblical faith avoids both fatalism (God's sovereignty makes human action irrelevant) and transactionalism (faith obligates God), instead affirming that God sovereignly works through and rewards active faith. unit #3
  2. Christ is the ultimate worthy man who surpasses Boaz by shielding believers from God's wrath, securing eternal favor, and granting the new identity of son or daughter through his cross-work. unit #13
  3. Boaz's radical table invitation to Ruth, removing her Gentile shame and providing abundant satisfaction, typologically points to Christ inviting sinners to his table with overflowing spiritual satisfaction. unit #14
  4. Jesus instituted communion as a repeated personal invitation to his table, declaring acceptance and satisfaction to believers who need to hear it again and again. unit #16
  5. Christ secured a world of eschatological potential for believers — an existence free from sin, death, and grief — while presently demonstrating faithfulness by making his power perfect in our weakness. unit #20

3 · Establishes the sermon's theological framework by defining two errors to avoid (fatalism and transactionalism) and anchoring the correct view in Hebrews 11:6

And there are 2 ditches we need to avoid. Number one is this: it's fatalism. It's the idea that God is sovereign, so what I do really doesn't matter. It doesn't matter that I act. Because God has already determined what is going to happen, and so I don't need to be involved in that when He has already determined everything. That is not biblical faith, right? It's the idea that if I act in faith, well, now God is obligated to work on my behalf. I did this, God, why aren't you doing something? Have you been there before? Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how God's sovereign love rewards active faith through protection, provision, and Christ's ultimate invitation to his table.

Monday Hebrews 11:6

The Hebrews passage declares that without faith it is impossible to please God, for those who draw near must believe he *exists* and *rewards* those who seek him. This is the theological heartbeat of Ruth's gleaning: she acts in vulnerability, not because the system guarantees reward, but because she trusts in a God who delights to bless the faithful risk-taker. We too are invited into this same posture—not passivity masked as piety, nor striving that binds God's hand, but active trust in his faithful character.

Tuesday Leviticus 19

When Ruth gleans according to the law, she is not begging at Boaz's mercy—she is claiming what God himself legislated as belonging to the poor and foreigner (Leviticus 19:9-10). Her risky faith is faith *in God's justice*, not mere chance. This transforms our understanding: God's sovereign protection of the vulnerable is not occasional rescue but embedded in the very fabric of his covenant order. When we act justly toward the marginalized among us, we participate in God's own revealed character and his delight in lifting up the needy.

Wednesday Ephesians 2

Paul writes that we were dead, objects of wrath, but God made us alive in Christ, seating us in heavenly places (Ephesians 2:4-6). Where Boaz showed Naomi and Ruth covenant kindness within the bounds of earthly law, Christ demolished the barrier between Jew and Gentile, absorbing the wrath we deserved and clothing us in his righteousness. The favor Ruth tasted—protection, provision, belonging—is eclipsed by the cosmic reversal Christ achieved: we are no longer slaves or foreigners but beloved children, eternally secured in the Father's affection through the blood of the Son.

Thursday John 6:35

When Jesus declares, "I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger," he echoes and transcends Boaz's act of calling Ruth to eat and drink at harvest. Christ's table is not a temporary provision for a day's work but an eternal feast that satisfies the deepest hunger of our souls—the hunger for acceptance, purpose, and communion with God. Every time we gather at his table in communion, we hear him whisper afresh to our shame-bound hearts: *You belong. You are fed. You are mine.*

Friday John 7

In John 7, Jesus cries out on the feast day: "If anyone thirsts, let him come to me and drink." This is not a one-time offer but an open, persistent invitation to a thirsty people. We return to the table week after week not because we have finally achieved worthiness, but because we remain those who need reminding that we are welcomed, that our shame has been removed, that Christ's feast is inexhaustible. The gospel tells us that every time we come broken and hungry, we find Christ saying what Boaz said to Ruth: *Come. Eat. Be satisfied. You are safe here.*

Sunday-evening family table

When You're Brave Enough to Ask

For the parent

This prompt draws from the moment Ruth decides to glean — a risky choice for a foreigner with no safety net. Use it to help your family think about what it looks like to trust God when we're scared or when we don't have everything we need. Listen for how they connect their own fears to Ruth's courage.

In the sermon, Ruth made a brave choice to go gleaning even though she was poor and didn't know if anyone would help her. Can you think of a time when you had to do something brave or ask for help even though you were a little scared? What made you brave enough to try?

Faith & Favor in Our Marriage

  1. What did you hear about God's character in this sermon, and how did it stir your own heart toward greater trust in Him?
  2. Where in our marriage do we tend toward either fatalism ("God will work it out, so we don't need to try") or transactionalism ("If we do enough, God owes us") — and how might God be calling us to active, risky faith together?
