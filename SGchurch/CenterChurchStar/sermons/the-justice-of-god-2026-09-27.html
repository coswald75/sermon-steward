Theological claims· 5
  1. God's true people are marked by the divine work of heart circumcision, not by race, tradition, or ceremony—and this was always the covenant standard. unit #4
  2. God has proven Himself to be beyond faithful to His covenant people throughout the Old Testament, patiently enduring their constant rebellion when He would have been perfectly just to destroy them. unit #11
  3. The prophetic rebukes in Scripture are not God being cruel but God being kind—He was never required to call His people to repentance, but His love compelled Him to warn them away from destruction. unit #13

Absolutely, we'll take all your advice, we'll do all of these things. But then what happens? The grease piles up, the chicken goes rotten, nothing changes, and all they're left with is a new coat of paint. The process of confronting sin is not meant to lead us to accept condemnation and assume our hearts will not change. That's not the point. Confronting sin guides us to see our need for help. And that is what Paul is doing. Not humiliating anyone, not burning down things just for the sake of lighting fires. He is tearing down our hiding places so that the only place left to run is grace. The question we have to answer when we read Romans, when we hear the Scripture, is can we really see our need for help? Or are we just throwing a jersey on? Do we need our hearts to be changed? And realize we can't do it. When we see our true need for help, the Bible, the Word of God, invites us to turn to the one who does change hearts, who promises to change hearts. And this is picked up by Paul when he speaks of believers those who have had their hearts changed, who are in the process of having their hearts changed. This very language is picked up by Paul in his letter to the Colossian church, where he describes the change we need. He says, in him, meaning Jesus, also you were circumcised with a circumcision made without hands. By putting off the body of flesh by the circumcision of Christ, having been buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him through faith in the powerful working of God who raised him from the dead. And you who were dead in your trespasses and the uncircumcision of your flesh, God, made alive together with him, having forgiven us all our trespasses by canceling the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands. This he set aside, nailing it to the cross. If you know you need help, if you know you need your heart to be changed and you can't do it, Know that you can call out to the one who has nailed all our infractions, all of our insecurities, everything to the cross, and he will make you new. You can, you can, you can hope in that, and you can know you will receive help in that, but you must know first you need help.

20 · The pastor concludes by reframing the sermon's central argument: the justice of God is not the final word—the gospel is that the God who judges is the God who helps, and Scripture exists not to condemn but to lead us to rest in Christ

Far more than the news of God's judgment, this is the story of how we can stop fighting, stop pretending there's nothing wrong, stop pretending it's not our fault, and rest. Isn't that what hope brings? It allows us to rest. We can rest knowing that we have help. We can rest knowing that the one who changes hearts has paid our debt. What God tells his people to do, what is required, he does it for them. He helps us. The call this morning is not to merely know the justice of God, but to know that the same God who judges is the God who helps. And he is presented to you on the pages of Scripture. This was written, this passage, these Scriptures were written so that you and I would believe. So stop listening to the lies that these pages are just here to embarrass you, to discourage you, to make you feel crummy, or to convict you just for the sake of being high and mighty. These Scriptures, the words of God, are delivered and preserved for you today so you can live, so we can be changed, so we can be counted as the true people of God, circumcised of the heart, loving God with all our mind, all our soul, and all our strength. For the first time even, genuinely.

21 · The pastor closes with a pastoral prayer asking the Holy Spirit to help the congregation see conviction as kindness, overcome the fear of exposure, and receive the help God offers

Let's pray. Spirit of God, we ask that you, in your kindness, would show us where we need to repent, Lord, that maybe even for the first time, we would see and receive that conviction as your loving kindness, Lord, your commitment to rescue a people for yourself. We probably need help, Lord. We probably need— I know there are probably some of us that are so embarrassed, so terrified at the idea of something being exposed, Lord, and it is that fear that keeps us from opening our arms to you and saying, yes, help me, Lord. I pray, Spirit of God, that you who know the hearts, you who judge men and the secrets of men, Lord, I pray that you would show us the exposure of those things would be love and kindness and goodness to us that we may receive your help. May you be honored in our repentance, and may we receive rest and joy in the transformation of our hearts. Do this in your people. In Jesus' name, amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace God's covenant faithfulness through five cross-references, moving from the heart-work that marks true covenant identity, through God's patient endurance of rebellion, to the exposure of sin as an act of mercy that drives us to Christ.

Monday Deuteronomy 10:14-16

Moses commands Israel to circumcise not flesh but heart—to love the Lord their God with undivided affection. This ancient word exposes a timeless truth: God has never been satisfied with outward conformity. The same God who called Abraham's descendants to covenant demanded the transformation of the inner person, proving that His covenant standard has always required the work of grace, not the security of heritage.

Tuesday Jeremiah 9:25-26

Jeremiah's word echoes Deuteronomy's requirement: circumcision of heart matters infinitely more than circumcision of flesh. Yet Israel resisted for centuries, and God did not abandon them. His forbearance in the face of persistent unfaithfulness reveals the depth of His covenant love—He was never obligated to spare them, yet He endured their rebellion, calling them back again and again through prophets they rejected.

Wednesday Psalm 51

David's broken confession, 'Create in me a clean heart, O God,' reveals that God's confrontation of sin is redemptive, not punitive. God could have destroyed David for his adultery and murder, yet instead He sent Nathan with a word that crushed and healed the king simultaneously. This is the pattern of divine rebuke: exposure of failure designed to awaken the sinner to his desperate need for God's mercy, not to seal his condemnation.

Thursday Deuteronomy 4:5-8

Moses reminds Israel that their statutes and decrees will display their wisdom and understanding among the nations—they exist as a testimony to God's proximity and justice. Yet we know that Israel repeatedly failed to keep covenant, and by every measure of justice they should have ceased to exist. Their survival across millennia, despite their constant unfaithfulness, is not accident but proof of God's resolute covenant love enduring what they deserved.

Friday Colossians 2:11-14

Paul unveils the answer to Israel's failure and ours: in Christ we are 'circumcised with a circumcision made without hands'—the Spirit's work that no ceremony can replicate. More, Christ nailed our debt to the cross, canceling the written code against us. God's exposure of our sin throughout Scripture was never meant to destroy us but to drive us to this one place: the cross where our unfaithfulness meets His justice, and His grace wins.

Sunday-evening family table

When God Tells Us We're Wrong

For the parent

This prompt invites kids to think about why God exposes our sin in Scripture—not to shame us, but to help us. Listen for whether they see God's rebuke as kindness rather than cruelty, and gently guide them toward understanding that exposure always points us toward Christ's help.

In the sermon, we heard that when God told His people through the prophets, 'You have broken your promise to me,' He wasn't being mean—He was being kind, like a parent warning a child away from danger. Can you think of a time when someone you love told you that you did something wrong? How did that feel? And why do you think they told you?

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. Paul opens this passage by asking whether there is any advantage to being Jewish, and then answers his own question. What does he identify as the genuine advantage, and how does his answer surprise us if we've been thinking about Jewish identity primarily in terms of ancestry or religious ritual?
    Romans 3:1-2
    → What might this tell us about how easily we can mistake external markers of faith—church membership, family heritage, regular attendance—for the actual work of God in our hearts?
  2. In Romans 3:3, Paul asks: 'What if some were unfaithful?' and then declares that God's faithfulness does not depend on human faithfulness. Why does Paul need to make this distinction, and what objection is he trying to head off before his readers raise it?
    Romans 3:3
  3. The passage reveals a posture of defensiveness in verses 5-8: the objector essentially argues that his unfaithfulness actually showcases God's justice, so why should he be condemned? What is this person refusing to do, and what does that refusal tell us about how sin operates in the human heart?
    Romans 3:5-8
    → Where do you see this same objection alive in our culture today—people claiming their choices are justified by circumstances beyond their control?
  4. Paul insists that God is 'true' even when every human is found to be a liar (Romans 3:4). What does it mean for God to be 'true,' and how is His truthfulness connected to His justice in condemning sin?
    Romans 3:4
  5. The sermon emphasizes that God's covenant standard was always internal—a 'circumcision of the heart'—not merely external ceremony or bloodline (see Deuteronomy 10:14-16). How does this reshape the conversation about who truly belongs to God's people, and what does it expose about our own tendency to measure faithfulness?
    Deuteronomy 10:14-16; Romans 2:28-29
    → If the covenant always required a work of God on the heart, not just our own effort, what does that suggest about where our hope must rest?
  6. The sermon teaches that Scripture's exposure of our sin and failure is not meant to condemn us but to reveal our desperate need for help that only Christ provides. In light of Colossians 2:11-14, which speaks of Christ accomplishing the circumcision of the heart and canceling our debt, how should the experience of being confronted by God's justice actually move us—what should it stir in us, and why?
    Colossians 2:11-14
    → What changes in how you read Scripture's rebukes and calls to repentance if you understand them as acts of God's kindness rather than cruelty?
