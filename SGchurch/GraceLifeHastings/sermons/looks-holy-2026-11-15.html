And notice the list here. He talks about food and drink, festivals, new moons, Sabbaths. Those are those yearly, monthly, weekly patterns that appeared all throughout the Old Testament as part of Israel's calendar. God had given Israel these feasts and these ceremonies, what we call the ceremonial law, and he gave them to Israel for a lot of good reasons. It was intentional that God gave them these things.

So the question isn't whether these old— the Old Testament was right in commanding these festivals and feasts and these calendars. Of course it was right in commanding this of Israel. 29 · Develops the shadow metaphor: shadows are real, truthful, and revelatory, not deceptive or worthless

Now that we live in post-arrival of the thing that those feasts and festivals and ceremonial law was pointing toward, how then should we live? Or I may not have said that last week. I think I said it in Table and Truth a couple of weeks ago. But a shadow doesn't lie. It tells us something real about the thing that is casting a shadow.

30 · Illustrates the shadow-substance relationship through the analogy of waiting for a friend: the shadow announces arrival but is not the object of affection once the friend appears

That happens. The shadow tells you that, guess what, the person is approaching. They're coming. They're here. But when your friend walks into the room, we don't then run and embrace the shadow. We don't fall down in admiration of the shadow. We don't weep over the goodness of the shadow that's now entered the room. The shadow did its work. The shadow showed us that our friend was entering the room. But now, the substance is here. Our friend has arrived. The one we love is here.

31 · Applies the illustration: Old Covenant institutions (Passover, sacrifices, priesthood) were shadows pointing to Christ as the fulfillment

And like that shadow, these were the things of the Old Covenant. The Passover was pointing us to the Lamb of God. The sacrifices pointed to the one sacrifice that would actually take away sin. They were shadows that were declaring to us something was coming, but they were never meant to be the destination. Christ is the reality that all of those shadows were pointing toward.

So, what Paul is doing here is he is not just announcing that now we are free from all of those things, he is telling us why our freedom from those things actually makes sense. The calendar was never an end in itself. The feast was never an end in itself. The festivals were never ends in themselves. They were signs, they were road signs that were showing us the way.

I'm in a superior class of holiness here.

54 · Introduces Paul's fundamental verdict on the false teachers: they have lost connection to Christ the head

And verse 19 encourages us otherwise because he condemns them by saying they are not holding fast to the head.

55 · Explains the subtlety of the false teaching: respectful Jesus-talk coexists with redirecting attention to something 'higher' than Christ

That's Paul's diagnosis of those people who insist on these practices and experiences and identify themselves as superior. What are they? They are not holding fast to the head. They may not stand up and announce that Jesus is unimportant. They might speak about Jesus respectfully, but what are they directing your attention to? They are directing your attention to something that is supposedly higher.

56 · Contrasts the false promise ('higher than Christ') with the gospel truth: Christ is the terminus, not the starting point

They are not saying Christ isn't enough. They just promise you ways to go higher than Christ. Oh, I can get you beyond that. We can go further than that. But guess what, church? In Christ, there is nowhere further to go. There is nothing higher. There is nothing deeper. There is nothing beyond him. There is nothing superior.

57 · Describes the characteristic pattern of false teaching: Christ-affirming language used to market experiences or practices that position themselves as necessary for Christ's effectiveness

And this is what makes this warning so important. False teaching doesn't arrive and just say, well, guess what, Grace Life Church? Jesus is false. Christianity isn't true. No, false teaching shows up and says things like, Jesus is so wonderful, and guess what? If you want to make him really effective in your life, I've got this experience that will help. That's what false teaching does. Here's an experience outside of Christ that will make the wonderfulness of Christ really effective for you. That is what false teaching will do.

58 · Identifies the structural move in false teaching: Christ demoted from destination to doorway, with something else becoming the true goal

An extra practice, a special experience, an advanced teaching where Christ, he is just treated as the beginning of our walk, but something else becomes the goal. An experience. A practice, a learning, a therapy, that becomes the destination. And God's word will have none of it.

59 · Highlights the tragic irony: those claiming superior access to life have severed connection to the source of life

And what a funny irony here. The people claiming that they're offering some form of something higher, they have lost hold of really the only thing that gives spiritual life. In their pursuit of life, they've lost the thing that actually gives life— Christ.

60 · Unpacks the threefold work of Christ the head: nourishment, unity, and God-given growth of the body

I mean, think about what Paul says the head does. The whole body— it's from Christ that the whole body is nourished. It's from Christ that the body is knit together. It's from Christ that the grows with the growth that comes from God.

61 · Contrasts two growth models: secret discovery versus organic life from the living head who supplies all that the body needs

The church doesn't grow because somebody discovers a secret path to growth. It grows, the church grows because our living head gives us what we need to grow. Our head nourishes us. Our head supplies us. Our head builds us up.

62 · Prescribes the antidote to false teaching: not competing experiences but return to Christ the head

And in contrast to these false teachers, we don't go looking for even a different, more impressive, though parallel experience to keep with false teachers. If we want to resist false teaching, what we don't do is double down. By saying, oh yeah, you've got that experience? Well, I've got a better experience over here. What do we do? We go back to the head. We just keep looking at the head.

63 · Reiterates the Christ-centered growth principle negatively: maturity does not come from drifting away from Christ or his church in pursuit of spiritual secrets

From Christ, the body is nourished and held together. We grow. We don't become more mature by drifting away from Christ. We don't become more mature by drifting away from Christ's Church in pursuit of some spiritual insight or secret.

64 · Identifies the ordinary means of grace through which Christ nourishes his people, contrasting their ordinariness with the extraordinary Savior at work through them

Jesus feeds His people through His Word, through prayer, the fellowship of the saints, and the sacraments He's given to us. They may look ordinary, but guess what? A full, extraordinary Savior is at work in all of these ordinary means.

65 · Provides a diagnostic test for evaluating claims to spiritual secrets: does the teaching lead to holding fast to Christ or make Christ a doorway to something else?

Of course, the Holy Spirit may work in remarkable ways, but no experience will ever give someone the authority to move beyond Christ. If someone tells you that ordinary Christians are missing a real secret, ask yourself, well, does their teaching help me hold fast to Christ? Or are they making Christ the doorway into something else, some method, some secret.

66 · Contextualizes the false teaching warning to the congregation's particular temptation: boasting in theological knowledge rather than visions

You know, we're not, as a church, we're not, I don't think, tempted to boast about visions. But we do boast about perhaps what we know, our theological distinctions or whatnot, and those are good. Truth is important. But we could explain our theological distinctions while being unloving and ungracious. So perhaps knowledge is our trap, if that knowledge makes us despise others who haven't studied as much as we have.

67 · Identifies the proper end of theological knowledge: humility, deeper knowledge of Christ, and love for Christ and others, not superiority

Now, if that's the case, if we despise people, well then something has gone wrong. In our pursuit. Maybe it's not an experience, maybe it's knowledge, because knowledge is meant to humble us. And the goal of sound doctrine is not that we as a church would somehow enter into some circle of superiority. No, we study sound doctrine so that we would know Christ know Him more deeply, and love Him and one another faithfully.

68 · States the proper orientation of learning: toward Christ the head, not self-admiration or mutual admiration

What we learn should draw us toward the head of Christ's body, not make us admire ourselves or even admire each other.

69 · Introduces the third major point with crisp parallel phrasing

The third thing that Paul says, is don't confuse self-denial with sanctification. Don't confuse self-denial with sanctification.

70 · Reads verses 20-22, establishing Paul's question: why submit to worldly regulations after dying with Christ to the elemental spirits?

If with Christ you died to the elemental spirits of the world, why, as if you were still alive in the world, do you submit to regulations: do not handle, do not taste, do not touch, referring to the things that all perish as they are used according to human precepts and traditions?

71 · Unpacks the if-clause: union with Christ by grace through faith means co-death with Christ to the old order's dominion

And then Paul, he's pressing the matter here deeply. If you died with Christ, if you have been united with Christ, if that has happened by faith, by grace, through the powerful working of the Holy Spirit when he regenerated your heart and made you alive together with Christ, raising your dead soul from the grave. If you have been united with Christ, then when Christ died, you died with him to the dominion of the old order.

72 · Identifies the contradiction: living under the old order's rules after being freed from its dominion through union with Christ

Why then do we live as though that old order has the ability to dictate how we gain access to God? I mean, those elemental spirits of the world, that's that enslaving order that Paul has already warned us about. Believers no longer belong to its rule.

73 · Characterizes the regulations: impressive-looking prohibitions focused on perishable things, but ultimately just human teachings

And sure, that rule has its slogans: do not handle, do not taste, do not touch. Those regulations, they focus on things that die when they're used. They look impressive because they require restraint, but Paul says these are just human teachings.

74 · Contrasts the false promise (regulations to reach fullness) with the gospel reality (fullness already possessed in Christ)

They're being offered in a way to reach some spiritual fullness, but guess what? You already have that spiritual fullness. You have it in Christ.

75 · Reads verse 23, establishing the verdict: regulations have appearance of wisdom but zero actual value in restraining fleshly indulgence

Verse 23, these things have an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body. But they are of no value, no value, no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.

76 · Restates the verdict: outward impressiveness and perceived holiness do not equal actual power to restrain sin

They may have an appearance of wisdom. It may look and sound impressive. A man may fast, deny himself, and arrange his life by all sorts of rules, and everybody around him may look and see that this person is really holy. But Paul says that that kind of self-made religion, it has no power to restrain the flesh.

77 · States the fundamental incapacity of man-made religion: it cannot produce the new life that comes from Christ

And man-made religious severity, it cannot give the new life that comes from Christ.

78 · Illustrates the difference between external restraint and heart transformation through the example of a man struggling with drunkenness

I mean, think of a man who may struggle with drunkenness. He removes alcohol from his house, he avoids places where he is tempted, and he asks somebody to hold him accountable. Those are all very wise steps and they are necessary steps, but can't a man do all of that while still loving drunkenness in his heart?

79 · Applies the illustration: restraints limit opportunity but cannot produce the new affections necessary for holiness

Restraints might limit opportunities to sin, but what they cannot do is give someone a new love for holiness, a new love for righteousness.

80 · Second brief illustration: phone restrictions as helpful tools that cannot create love for purity

Or somebody who, who puts restrictions on their phone— those are good, we should use them, but those restrictions can't make a person love purity.

81 · Generalizes the principle: rules restrain but cannot regenerate; fences around sin cannot produce new hearts

Rules can restrain behavior, but they cannot create spiritual life. This is the old problem of the heart. We can build restraints around sinful desires, and those are wise restraints, but a fence is not a new heart. It doesn't make us love what is good and true and beautiful.

82 · Identifies the true source of sanctifying power: the Spirit unites believers to Christ, from whom all spiritual life is drawn

That work is a work of the Spirit of God. He unites us to Christ, and we as branches now united to the vine draw all of our life from Him.

83 · Distinguishes support structures (trellis, restraints, cuffs) from the life source (the vine, the head), affirming the former's usefulness while denying them regenerative power

The trellis that helps us grow is not the place where our life is drawn from. Our life comes from the vine. The restraints over our hands that keep us from reaching and grabbing for things that are sinful, yes, they are good and they are wise at times, but they are not where we get our life from. The cuffs don't give us life, our head does.

84 · Qualifies the polemic against self-denial to prevent antinomian misunderstanding: Christian discipline remains necessary and good

So it's— this is not an argument to step away from Christian discipline. We should deny ourselves. We put to death sin. We resist temptation. We establish godly habits. We obey commands.

85 · States the critical distinction: discipline is necessary but not the source of spiritual life or means of union with Christ

But we don't treat those things as the source of spiritual life. You cannot discipline yourself into union with Christ.

86 · Identifies the gospel's enduring role in sanctification: Christ is not left behind after justification but remains the sanctifying agent

That's why the gospel is not just the doorway into Christianity. Something that we leave behind when it comes time to mature. Jesus justifies us and He sanctifies us.

87 · Applies the sermon diagnostically, asking where listeners look for spiritual fullness and whether they measure themselves or are judged by non-biblical standards

So where are you looking for spiritual fullness? Maybe you've been measuring your faithfulness by a private set of rules, or you've been made to feel like a second-class Christian because you don't do or you haven't experienced someone else's rules or experiences?

88 · Delivers the concluding exhortation with concrete practices framed by identity in Christ

Hear the answer from Colossians 2: Hold fast to the Head. Open the Scriptures, pray, gather with the church, Confess your sins, receive correction, fight sin, but do all of it as a man or woman who belongs to Christ.

89 · Pivots from sermon proper to the Lord's Supper with minimal connective tissue

Which brings us to the Lord's Table.

90 · Establishes the sacramental logic: the Lord's Supper is not supplementary sacrifice but nourishment from Christ the head

Paul says that the head nourishes his body, and Christ has given His church this meal. Not because His sacrifice needs to be added to, but because at this meal we get strengthened by our Head.

91 · Calls the congregation to remember the sermon's threefold warning as they approach the table

So before we take, remember Paul's warning: don't let anybody judge you by the shadows. Don't let anybody disqualify you with claims of spiritual superiority, and don't let your conscience be surrendered to rules that cannot conquer sin.

92 · Summarizes the sermon's core affirmations as preparation for participation in the sacrament

Remember, Christ is the substance. Christ is the head. You have died with Christ and your life is in Him. Hold fast to Him.

93 · Closing prayer thanking God for sufficiency in Christ and asking for help to trust him alone

Let's pray. God, thank You. That in Christ we have all that we need. Would you help us to trust and look to him alone for all that we need, believing that he will supply it for our good and for your glory? In Jesus' name, amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through five theological claims that expose the emptiness of appearance-based spirituality and anchor our hearts in Christ alone as the sole source of holiness and life.

Monday Romans 8:1

Paul's declaration—that there is now no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus—cuts to the foundation of all false religion: the fear that we remain guilty before God and must earn his favor through practices and severity. When we grasp that our Judge has declared us righteous in Christ, we stop trading the substance of Christ for shadows. The gospel's power frees us from the endless treadmill of self-made rules.

Tuesday Hebrews 10:1

The Hebrews passage illuminates why the Colossian false teachers were so deceptive: they were urging believers to cling to real shadows—genuine institutions God had ordained—but as if those shadows still held power after the substance had arrived. Shadows are not lies; they reveal something true about what casts them. But obsessing over shadows after receiving Christ is odd ingratitude and a refusal to acknowledge that the ceremonial law was never an end in itself but a sign pointing to him.

Wednesday Psalm 99:1-3

Psalm 99 exalts the true ground of holiness: God himself, seated on his throne, transcendent and awesome. The psalmist's call to exalt and worship at God's footstool reminds us that authentic holiness means drawing near to God's actual presence, not to the appearance of devotion. Self-made regulations that separate us from Christ's fellowship masquerade as paths to holiness while they actually distance us from the only source of true sanctification—holding fast to Christ the head.

Thursday Colossians 2:19

This verse anchors the body of Christ in its Head in a way that exposes all spirituality that drifts from him as sterile, regardless of how impressive it appears. Spiritual life flows from Christ alone through the ordinary means of grace in the local church—Word, prayer, fellowship, sacraments—not through hidden knowledge or ascetic experiences. We are fed by Christ through these means, and when we abandon them in pursuit of higher experiences, we ironically sever ourselves from the source of actual spiritual nourishment.

Friday Romans 8:1

As we return to Romans 8:1 at week's end, we see the practical answer to all false spirituality: the freedom and life we have in Christ through his finished work cannot be improved upon or supplemented by our effort. Those tempted to judge themselves or others by diet, calendar, or severity ought to remember that the verdict has already been rendered—no condemnation in Christ—and that frees us to grow not through self-imposed asceticism but through deeper union with him and faithful discipline within the community he nourishes.
