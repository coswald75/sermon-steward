5 · This unit addresses a potential misreading of 'firstborn of all creation' by tracing the biblical-theological meaning of 'firstborn' as a term of rank and inheritance rather than chronological order

He is the image of the invisible God and He's the firstborn over all creation. Now, this phrase, firstborn over all creation, has caused trouble in the church for centuries because if we just take that verse or that idea, firstborn of all creation, it can sound like Paul is saying that Jesus was the first creature that God made, that God made Jesus but Don't worry, Jesus is important, he was made first. But he isn't made. Because firstborn in scripture doesn't just refer to the first thing to happen, it also refers to rank or inheritance. In fact, in Psalm 89, God says of David, I will make him, David, the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth. But David wasn't Jesse's firstborn. In fact, David was the youngest, but what did God do? God gave him the position of the firstborn. He gave him the position as heir, as king, and that's Paul's point. Christ stands over creation, not as one of theirs, not as one of the created things made by God, but Christ stands over creation as creation's heir, as creation's Lord.

6 · This unit establishes Christ's deity and eternality by appealing to verse 16 (all things were created by Him) and the Nicene Creed's confession against Arianism

And if there were any doubt, verse 16 tells us, for by him all things were created in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible. Jesus is not in the category of created things because Paul says created things came from Jesus. He's the one that made it all. And the church for centuries has fought over this. In the 4th century, a man named Arius taught that the Son was not eternal God in the same sense as the Father. Arius, he taught really highly of Jesus, but he still said that Jesus was created. So church leaders gathered at Nicaea in 325 and they wrote what we call the Nicene Creed, and in it they confessed Jesus Christ as God of God, Light of Light, Very God of very God, begotten, not made, being of one substance with the Father, and then by whom all things were made. In 3:25, these leaders, they were not inventing a new idea about Jesus. They were guarding and protecting and standing on what Paul says right here in Colossians Christ is not part of creation, Christ is the Creator. And that's why this creed has served the church for centuries, not because it improves on Scripture, but because it confesses what Scripture teaches and it rejects Arius, which Scripture denies. So if you've been in a church that's confessed the Nicene Creed, all it's doing is confessing what Christ is preaching, or what Paul is preaching right here of Christ in Colossians chapter 1.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through five portraits of Christ's sufficiency—His divine nature, His creative power, His headship, His resurrection triumph, and His reconciling work—each one answering the lie that anything can be added to Him.

Monday Hebrews 1:3

Hebrews clarifies what Colossians declares: Christ is not a pale reflection of God's character but the radiance of His glory—the very light of divinity made visible. When we see Jesus, we see God Himself. This is the foundation of everything else Paul will say about Christ's sufficiency; if all of God's glory and being rests in Him, there is literally nothing else to seek.

Tuesday Colossians 2:9

Paul repeats this staggering claim with intensity: the complete, undiminished fullness of God—not a portion, not an echo—inhabits Christ in bodily form. This is not a theological abstraction but a concrete historical reality. The Colossians were being tempted to supplement Christ with secret knowledge and spiritual experiences, but Paul shows them that they already possess everything; Christ contains it all.

Wednesday 1 Corinthians 15:20

Christ's resurrection is not one miracle among many—it is the hinge on which creation's redemption turns. As the firstfruits, He guarantees the harvest; His rising from death declares that He alone holds power over the grave and that all things will be restored through Him. This is why nothing can be added to Him; He has conquered what we cannot and made a way none of us could make.

Thursday Romans 5:1

Paul's logic is relentless: our reconciliation is not something we construct through works, deeper experiences, or hidden knowledge—it is something Christ purchased with His own blood and offers to us with empty hands. We come as receivers, not achievers. The peace we desperately seek is already complete in His finished work; we need only receive it in faith.

Friday Colossians 2:6-7

If Christ is enough—the foundation of all things, the head of the church, the source of peace and redemption—then our entire lives belong to Him. We cannot compartmentalize Jesus to the spiritual realm while keeping our work, relationships, and daily decisions separate. The natural response to such fullness is to let Him reign over all we are and do, together walking in the reality that everything we touch is His.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Colossians 1:15, Paul describes Jesus as 'the image of the invisible God.' What does it mean that Jesus is the image of God, and how does this claim address the Colossians' pressure to seek hidden knowledge or deeper spiritual experiences elsewhere?
    John 14:9; Hebrews 1:3
    → How does understanding Jesus as God's image change the way you think about knowing God—is there any knowledge of God available to us that isn't revealed in Jesus?
  2. According to Colossians 1:16-17, what is Christ's relationship to creation, and what does it mean practically that 'all things hold together in him'?
    Colossians 1:16-17
    → If all things—including your work, your relationships, your Monday morning—hold together in Christ, what changes about how you approach those things this week?
  3. The sermon identifies a 'Jesus-plus' temptation in the Colossian church—the impulse to add special knowledge, rules, or experiences to their faith in Christ. What forms does this same temptation take in our own lives and churches today?
    → What makes these additions feel necessary or compelling to us, even when we know Christ is sufficient?
  4. Colossians 1:19-20 states that 'all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell in him, and through him to reconcile to himself all things, whether on earth or in heaven, making peace by the blood of his cross.' How does Christ's fullness and His reconciling work through His blood address our deepest need—the broken relationship between ourselves and God?
    Colossians 1:14; Colossians 1:21; Romans 5:1
    → When you come to worship or to the Lord's Table, do you come believing you have something to contribute to your peace with God, or do you come with empty hands to receive what Christ has already made?
  5. The sermon teaches that because Christ made everything and sustains everything, 'Christianity cannot be confined to one corner of life—our Mondays belong to Christ just as much as our Sundays.' What would it look like to live Monday through Friday as if Christ is truly Lord of all things, not just of religious activities?
    → Where do you find yourself compartmentalizing your faith—treating some areas of life as belonging fully to Christ and others as more negotiable?
  6. If all the fullness of God dwells in Christ and nothing can be added to Him, what does it mean for our lives that He chooses to give Himself to us? How does His sufficiency and His self-giving reshape the way we approach our relationship with Him?
    Colossians 2:9
    → How might grasping that Christ needs nothing from us actually free us to serve Him and others more joyfully?

For the parent

This card invites kids to think about why people try to add things to Jesus—extra rules, special knowledge, or practices they think will make them "more Christian." Listen for whether they grasp that Jesus is already completely full and complete; the goal is to help them see that following Jesus doesn't require anything extra.

Pastor Chad said that some people in the church at Colossae thought they needed to add special rules or secret knowledge to Jesus to really know God. If Jesus is God Himself—if all of God lives in Him—why would we ever need to add anything to Him? What might we try to add to Jesus today, and why do you think we do that?
works for ages 8+
Couples · three questions over coffee

Christ is Enough

  1. What part of Christ's fullness—His power as Creator, His headship over the church, His reconciliation through His blood—most humbled or amazed you as you heard it preached?
  2. Where are we tempted, together or individually, to add something to Christ—a spiritual practice, a standard, a relationship, a pursuit—as if He were not fully sufficient for our peace and wholeness?
  3. How can we pray for one another this week to rest more deeply in the finished work of Christ and to release whatever we've been trying to add to Him?
Memory verse this week

Colossians 1:19

For in him all the fullness of God was pleased to dwell.

Why this verse: This verse crystallizes the sermon's central claim that nothing can be added to Christ because all the fullness of God dwells in Him alone. It is the theological foundation upon which the entire defense against the Jesus-plus mentality rests.
