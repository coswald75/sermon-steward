Illustrations· 5
  1. personal story · unit #4 — The preacher begins an extended illustration of two houses facing a storm, embedding personal experience with different natural disasters to build relatability and anticipation. The story will serve as the controlling metaphor for the sermon's central contrast between two foundations.
  2. analogy · unit #5 — The illustration now unfolds in full narrative detail: two houses, two foundations, identical external circumstances, radically different outcomes. The storm does not distinguish between the houses—it reveals what was already true about their foundations. This sets up the sermon's central distinction between appearance and reality, between what is seen and what is hidden.
  3. personal story · unit #14 — The preacher illustrates the danger of trusting human counsel with a personal wedding example: well-meaning people give advice, but not all advice is godly. The illustration makes the abstract point concrete—trusting in man begins with believing worldly wisdom without subjecting it to Scripture.
  4. analogy · unit #16 — The preacher provides a memorable illustration of meditation as rumination—chewing the cud like a cow. The image is visceral and unforgettable, teaching the congregation that biblical meditation is not passive reading but active, repeated engagement with Scripture until understanding and application are internalized.
  5. cultural reference · unit #27 — The preacher provides a contemporary illustration of worship using social media influencers. The illustration captures the dynamics of worship—captivity, attention, delight, imitation. The point is that worship is demonstrated by what captures our attention and shapes our behavior. The cursed person gives that attention to human voices; the blessed person gives it to God.
Theological claims· 2
  1. The fundamental choice in life is not about avoiding storms but about what foundation you build on—yourself or God. unit #6
  2. The blessed life is the only rational choice—why would you trust in man (the lesser) when you could trust in God (the infinitely greater)? unit #34

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the roots of trust—from the Fall's fracture of human confidence, through the emptiness of worldly securities, to the unshakable strength found only in knowing God as our Father.

Monday Genesis 3:8-19

Here we see the moment trust fractured: Adam and Eve hid from the God who made them, attempting to cover their shame with fig leaves rather than running to His mercy. Their choice to trust their own judgment over God's word—to eat from the tree despite His warning—inaugurated the pattern every human heart repeats: we naturally believe we know better, that our strength and wisdom are sufficient. This is the root of the cursed life Jeremiah describes, and it runs through us all.

Tuesday Ecclesiastes 1-2

Solomon, the wisest and richest of all men, exhausts himself chasing pleasure, accomplishment, and accumulation—only to discover it all is vanity, a grasping at wind. His testimony exposes the bankruptcy of the cursed life: even when we achieve everything we set out to achieve, the soul remains hollow because it was never built on God. We see here the futility of the five categories the sermon identified—we cannot build a blessed life on anything less than the eternal God Himself.

Wednesday Romans 1:18-25

Paul reveals the architecture of the cursed life: we know God exists and displays His power, yet we suppress that truth and pour our trust into created things instead of the Creator. This is not ignorance but rebellion—we choose the lesser because we want to be our own gods. The blessed life becomes visible only when we stop this exchange and direct our trust toward the one true God whose glory infinitely surpasses anything we could manufacture or possess.

Thursday 1 John 3:1; 1 John 4:17-18

John cuts to the heart of what makes trust rational and unshakable: we are loved children of the living God, adopted into His family at infinite cost through Christ's blood. When we grasp that reality—when we know we are His—fear and anxiety lose their grip because we are secure not in the stability of our circumstances but in the unchanging heart of our Father. Love casts out fear; sonship casts out the need to trust in ourselves.

Friday Philippians 4:4-9

Paul gives us the grammar of a blessed life lived in real time: rejoice always, refuse anxiety through prayer and thanksgiving, and deliberately fill your mind with what is true and worthy of praise. These are not burdensome commands but the natural overflow of a heart that has chosen to trust God; they are the disciplines that keep us tethered to that trust when circumstances try to shake it. In practicing these things together, we strengthen one another's confidence that our Father is faithful, wise, and worthy of our full reliance.

Blessed is the man who trusts in the Lord, whose trust is the Lord. He is like a tree planted by water, that sends out its roots by the stream, and does not fear when heat comes, for its leaves remain green, and is not anxious in the year of drought, for it does not cease to bear fruit.

Why this verse: This verse encapsulates the sermon's central thesis: the blessed life flows from active trust in God rather than trust in human strength, and this trust produces spiritual fruitfulness and peace even amid affliction. It is the climactic promise of the passage and the foundation for the entire argument about where our security truly rests.
