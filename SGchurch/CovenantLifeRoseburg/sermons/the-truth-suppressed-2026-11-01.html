0 · Opens the sermon by positioning the text within the Romans series and preparing the congregation for difficult material by characterizing it as sobering

This morning we continue our journey through Paul's letter to the Romans, and we come to one of the most sobering passages in all of Scripture. Romans chapter 1, verses 18 through 23. This is the word of God.

1 · Reads the primary text in full, establishing the complete argument Paul makes about God's wrath, human knowledge of God through creation, and the exchange of glory for idolatry

The wrath of God is being revealed from heaven against all the godlessness and wickedness of people, who suppress the truth by their wickedness, since what may be known about God is plain to them, because God has made it plain to them. For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities—his eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly seen, being understood from what has been made, so that people are without excuse. For although they knew God, they neither glorified him as God nor gave thanks to him, but their thinking became futile and their foolish hearts were darkened. Although they claimed to be wise, they became fools and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images made to look like a mortal human being and birds and animals and reptiles.

2 · Establishes the controlling interpretive claim that human unbelief is active suppression rather than passive ignorance, focusing on the present-tense verb to show the ongoing nature of this rebellion

Paul begins with this stunning declaration: 'The wrath of God is being revealed from heaven against all the godlessness and wickedness of people, who suppress the truth by their wickedness.' Notice that word 'suppress.' It's not that people are ignorant of the truth. It's not that they haven't been given enough evidence. It's that they are actively, presently, continuously suppressing what they know to be true.

3 · Connects the wrath passage to the preceding gospel declaration, showing that understanding God's wrath is necessary context for appreciating salvation, and emphasizes the present-tense revelation of wrath

Now this follows immediately after Paul's declaration that the gospel reveals the righteousness of God. In verses 16 and 17, Paul told us that the gospel is the power of God for salvation, that in it the righteousness of God is revealed. But now, in verse 18, he introduces the dark backdrop against which the gospel shines so brilliantly. The wrath of God is also being revealed. Not will be revealed in some future judgment—though that's true—but is being revealed right now.

4 · Defends the doctrine of God's wrath against modern resistance by defining it as the necessary expression of God's holiness and justice, distinguishing divine wrath from human vindictiveness

The wrath of God. This is a difficult doctrine for our modern sensibilities. We want a God of love, a God of grace, a God of acceptance. And He is all those things. But He is also holy. He is also just. And His holiness cannot tolerate sin. His justice cannot overlook rebellion. The wrath of God is not some primitive, vindictive rage. It is the settled, righteous opposition of a holy God to everything that destroys His creation and defies His character.

5 · Expounds the universal scope of condemnation by showing that all people, regardless of moral performance, suppress truth that God has made plain, preparing for the argument about natural revelation

And against what is this wrath revealed? Against all godlessness and wickedness of people who suppress the truth by their wickedness. Paul is building a case here. He's establishing that every person—not just the obviously immoral, not just the violent criminal, not just the pagan idolater, but every person—stands condemned before God. Why? Because everyone suppresses the truth. Verse 19: 'what may be known about God is plain to them, because God has made it plain to them.'

6 · Establishes the theological category of natural revelation, explaining how creation itself makes God's invisible attributes visible and accessible to all people, rendering them without excuse for unbelief

This is the doctrine of general revelation, or natural revelation. Verse 20: 'For since the creation of the world God's invisible qualities—his eternal power and divine nature—have been clearly seen, being understood from what has been made, so that people are without excuse.' Think about what Paul is saying. He's saying that from the very beginning, from the moment God spoke creation into existence, He has been revealing Himself. The heavens declare the glory of God, as the psalmist says. The intricacy of a flower, the vastness of the cosmos, the moral law written on the human heart—all of it points to a Creator. All of it reveals God's power and divine nature.

7 · Uses the analogy of a painting requiring a painter to make the argument for natural revelation accessible and intuitive, showing that denying a Creator is as absurd as denying a painting has an artist

Let me illustrate this. Imagine you walked into a building and saw a magnificent painting on the wall—intricate detail, perfect composition, stunning use of color. And someone said to you, 'That painting just appeared. No one painted it. The paint just happened to fall on the canvas in exactly that way.' You would rightly think that person was either joking or delusional. Why? Because effect requires a cause. Design requires a designer. The painting demands a painter. In the same way, creation demands a Creator. The complexity and order of the universe don't just happen. They point to God.

8 · Reinforces the clarity and universality of natural revelation to eliminate any appeal to ignorance as excuse, and supports this with parallel passages from Paul's missionary preaching to Gentile audiences

Paul says this revelation has been 'clearly seen.' It's not hidden. It's not obscure. It's not reserved for the intellectual elite. God has made it plain. And notice the phrase 'so that people are without excuse.' This is critical. Paul is establishing that no one will stand before God on the day of judgment and say, 'I didn't know. You didn't give me enough evidence. I would have believed if You had just shown me more.' No. The evidence has been given. The truth has been made plain. And when people reject God, they do so not out of ignorance but out of willful suppression of what they know to be true. This is what Paul taught everywhere he went. When he was in Lystra, he told the pagans that God 'has not left himself without testimony' but has shown kindness through creation. When he was in Athens, he declared that God made the world and everything in it, that He determines the times and places where people live 'so that they would seek him and perhaps reach out for him and find him, though he is not far from any one of us.'

9 · Unpacks the grammar and logic of verse 21 to show that human rebellion is not against unknown truth but against known truth, demonstrated by the refusal to glorify and thank God despite knowledge of Him

But here's where it gets even more devastating. Verse 21: 'For although they knew God, they neither glorified him as God nor gave thanks to him, but their thinking became futile and their foolish hearts were darkened.' Did you catch that? 'Although they knew God.' Not 'although they suspected God might exist' or 'although they wondered about God.' They knew God. Past tense. They had knowledge of God through His creation. And what did they do with that knowledge? They refused to glorify Him. They refused to give Him thanks. They refused to submit to Him as God.

10 · Traces the downward trajectory from knowledge of God to futile thinking to foolish self-deception to idolatry, showing that the exchange of God's glory for created images is the inevitable result of refusing to glorify Him

And what was the result? Their thinking became futile. Their hearts were darkened. Verse 22: 'Although they claimed to be wise, they became fools.' This is the great irony of human rebellion against God. We think we're being sophisticated when we deny Him. We think we're being intellectually superior when we explain away His existence. But Paul says we're being fools. And then the ultimate tragedy in verse 23: 'and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images made to look like a mortal human being and birds and animals and reptiles.' They exchanged the Creator for the creature. They traded the infinite for the finite. They gave up the glory of God for worthless idols.

11 · Applies the ancient idolatry described by Paul to the modern context by showing that contemporary forms of worship—money, success, comfort, relationships—constitute the same exchange of God's glory for created things

Now when Paul wrote this, his original audience would have immediately thought of the pagan world around them—the temples to Zeus and Apollo, the statues of Roman emperors demanding worship, the Egyptian gods with animal heads. But Paul's indictment goes deeper than that. This isn't just about ancient paganism. This is about the human heart. Every time Israel made a golden calf, they were doing this. Every time we make anything in creation more important than the Creator, we're doing this. When we worship money, we've exchanged God's glory for images made to look like mortal things. When we worship success, fame, comfort, pleasure, even family—when any created thing takes the place that belongs to God alone, we have become idolaters. We have exchanged the glory of the immortal God for a cheap substitute.

12 · Pivots from diagnosis of the human problem to the question of God's response, preparing the congregation for the exposition of divine wrath as abandonment

So where does this leave us? What is God's response to this universal rebellion, this wholesale suppression of truth, this tragic exchange of glory for shame?

13 · Defines God's wrath as His decision to abandon people to the consequences of their rebellion by giving them over to the sins they choose, previewing the threefold 'God gave them over' pattern in the following verses

This is where the wrath of God comes in. But notice how God's wrath is revealed. It's not primarily through fire from heaven or immediate judgment, though those things are real and will come. The wrath of God revealed in our present time is this: God gives people over. Three times in the verses that follow our text, Paul will say 'God gave them over'—to sexual impurity, to shameful lusts, to a depraved mind. God's wrath is His decision to let people have what they want. If you want to live without God, He will let you. If you want to worship the creature rather than the Creator, He will give you over to the consequences of that choice. And those consequences are devastating. When God removes His restraining hand, when He stops holding back the full force of sin's destructive power, humanity descends into chaos.

14 · Shifts from exposition to direct pastoral address, applying the weight of the text to the congregation's immediate context and heightening the urgency of evangelism by personalizing the stakes

Church, do you see how serious this is? Do you see how urgent the gospel is? Every person in this room, every person you work with, every person in your family who doesn't know Christ—they are under the wrath of God right now. Not because they haven't been given enough evidence. Not because God hasn't made Himself known. But because they are actively, continuously suppressing the truth in unrighteousness. They know God exists, but they refuse to glorify Him or give Him thanks. And God's wrath is being revealed as He gives them over to the consequences of that rebellion.

15 · Issues the first application by correcting false anthropology and calling the congregation to adopt the biblical view of humanity as rebellious suppressors of truth rather than innocent seekers

This passage demands several responses from us. First, we must abandon any notion that people are basically good, basically innocent, basically sincere seekers who just need a little more information. No. The Bible is clear. All people know God through creation, and all people suppress that truth. The unbeliever is not ignorant; he is in rebellion. She is not searching; she is running. And until we understand this, we will never understand the depth of our own sin or the glory of God's grace in saving us.

16 · Issues the second application by calling the congregation to self-examination regarding idolatry, warning that even believers are tempted to exchange God's glory for created things

Second, we must guard our own hearts against idolatry. The same impulse that drove ancient pagans to bow before statues lives in us. We are constantly tempted to exchange God's glory for something less. What are the idols in your life? What created thing are you tempted to worship? Is it your career? Your reputation? Your comfort? Your children? Even good things become idols when they take God's place. And when we give ultimate allegiance to anything other than God, we participate in the same suppression of truth that Paul condemns here. We must ruthlessly identify and destroy the idols in our hearts.

17 · Issues the third application by calling for evangelism that combines urgency about the lost with humility about the Christian's own former rebellion, preventing self-righteousness

Third, this text should drive us to evangelism with both urgency and humility. Urgency, because people are perishing under God's wrath. They need the gospel. They need rescue. They need Jesus. We cannot be casual about this. We cannot be silent. But also humility, because we were once suppressors of truth ourselves. We were once in rebellion against God. We were once fools who exchanged His glory for worthless idols. It was only by grace—sheer, undeserved grace—that we came to know Christ. So we evangelize not as those who are morally superior, but as beggars telling other beggars where to find bread.

18 · Issues the fourth application by moving from the darkness of condemnation to the glory of the gospel, showing how Christ absorbed the wrath described in the passage and secured righteousness for believers

Finally, this passage should fill us with wonder at the gospel. If this is the human condition—universal knowledge of God, universal suppression of that knowledge, universal condemnation, universal descent into idolatry and its consequences—then how can anyone be saved? The answer is Jesus Christ. The gospel is the power of God for salvation to everyone who believes. While we were still suppressors of truth, still rebels, still idolaters, Christ died for us. He took the wrath we deserved. He satisfied God's justice on our behalf. And now, by faith in Him, we are declared righteous. We are reconciled to God. We are rescued from the consequences of our suppression and rebellion. The same wrath Paul describes here—the wrath that should fall on us—fell instead on Jesus at the cross.

19 · Brings the sermon to closure by reiterating the stakes (a world under wrath), the solution (the gospel), and the congregation's responsibility (bold proclamation and faithful living), ending with a benedictory prayer

Brothers and sisters, we live in a world that suppresses the truth about God. A world that exchanges His glory for worthless substitutes. A world under His wrath. But we have been given a message of rescue. We have been entrusted with the gospel—the only power that can save. Let us proclaim it with boldness. Let us live it with integrity. And let us never forget what we have been saved from and what we have been saved for. May God use this text to deepen our gratitude for the gospel and sharpen our urgency for the lost. Amen.

20 · Closes with pastoral prayer that rehearses the sermon's key themes—God's revelation, human suppression, Christ's rescue—and asks for grace to treasure and proclaim the gospel

Let's pray. Father, we thank You that You have not left us in the dark. You have made Yourself known through creation. You have revealed Your glory. And yet we confess that apart from Your grace, we would be suppressors of truth like everyone else. We would be rebels, idolaters, fools. Thank You for sending Jesus to rescue us from Your wrath. Thank You for the gospel. Help us to treasure it, to proclaim it, and to live in the light of it every day. In Jesus' name, Amen.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. Paul writes that God's wrath is revealed against those who suppress the truth in unrighteousness (Romans 1:18). What does it mean that wrath is *revealed* — how is this different from wrath that simply comes in the future?
    Romans 1:18, Romans 1:24-32
    → Can you think of a concrete example from Scripture or observation where you've seen people abandoned to the destructive consequences of their chosen idolatry?
  2. According to Romans 1:20, what has God made *plain* to all people, and through what means does He communicate it?
    Romans 1:20, Psalm 19:1
  3. The sermon teaches that human ignorance of God is not innocent but *culpable* — rooted in willful rejection rather than lack of evidence. How does this reshape the way you think about unbelievers you know or encounter?
    → Does accepting this biblical diagnosis make you more or less compassionate toward them, and why?
  4. Romans 1:21-23 describes a progression: people knew God, but they did not glorify Him or give thanks, and their thinking became futile, and they exchanged His glory for idols. What does this sequence suggest about how idolatry begins in the human heart?
    Romans 1:21-23, Isaiah 44:9-20
    → Where do you see this same pattern playing out in contemporary culture — the suppression of truth leading to the exchange of God's glory for something else?
  5. The sermon applies Romans 1:18-23 by saying that *we* must actively identify and destroy the idols in our own hearts, recognizing that even good things become sinful when they take God's place. What might this look like practically — not in theory, but in your actual life this week?
    → What good thing — a relationship, a career ambition, a possession, a reputation — are you most tempted to elevate above loyalty to God?
  6. Given that all people are in active rebellion against known truth about God and therefore stand under His wrath, how should this shape both the *urgency* and the *humility* with which we evangelize?
    Romans 1:16-17
    → How does remembering that *we* were once suppressors of truth, saved only by grace, protect us from spiritual arrogance as we witness?
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how humanity's suppression of God's known truth leads to justly deserved condemnation—and how this diagnosis humbles us and shapes our witness.

Monday Psalm 19:1

The heavens declare God's glory with such clarity that David calls it unceasing speech—not a whisper, but proclamation. This is the foundation of Romans 1:18-23: there is no neutral ground, no excuse of ignorance. When we grasp that creation itself is God's relentless testimony to every person, we see why the suppression of this truth is willful rebellion, not innocent confusion.

Tuesday Isaiah 44:9-20

Isaiah's scathing account of idol-making—a craftsman carving a god from the same wood he burns for warmth—exposes the absurdity at the heart of idolatry. Yet this passage cuts deeper: it shows us that idolatry is not crude paganism alone but the perversion of our reason and will. We know better, yet we bow. This mirrors Paul's diagnosis: we exchange the glory of the immortal God for images, choosing the lie over the truth we have plainly seen.

Wednesday Acts 17:24-28

Paul's speech in Athens declares that God is not distant or obscure but the very ground of our existence—"in Him we live and move and have our being." This proximity to God is not a comfort to the suppressor but a condemnation: there is nowhere to hide from truth so intimate and encompassing. Our rejection of God is not a failure to find Him but a refusal of the God who holds us in existence moment by moment.

Thursday Romans 1:24-32

The threefold refrain "God gave them over" reveals that divine wrath is not arbitrary punishment but the righteous removal of His hand. When we refuse to honor God or give Him thanks, He allows us to experience the full weight of our rebellion—broken relationships, corrupted desires, enslaving passions. This is terrifying mercy: God respects our choice to reject Him by letting us experience its true fruit. It should drive us to evangelize with urgency and humility.

Friday Exodus 32

Israel's golden calf—fashioned while Moses received the very law that forbade it—shows that idolatry lives in the hearts of God's own people. We too exchange the glory of Christ for the security of visible, controllable substitutes: wealth, reputation, comfort, relationships. Identifying and destroying these idols in ourselves is not a project of self-improvement but a response of gratitude to grace that has already saved us from the condemnation we deserve. What idol are you exchanging for God's glory this week?
