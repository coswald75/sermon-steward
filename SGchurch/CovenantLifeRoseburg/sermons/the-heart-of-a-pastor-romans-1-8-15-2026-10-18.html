Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace Paul's five expressions of pastoral love—thanksgiving, prayer, longing, mutual encouragement, and gospel proclamation—to discover that the heart of a pastor is the template for how all Christians should love one another in the body of Christ.

Monday 1 Thessalonians 5:11

Paul exhorts the Thessalonians to encourage one another and build one another up, a practice inseparable from constant intercession. When we prioritize prayer for others above our tendency to offer advice or mere presence, we demonstrate that we truly believe God's work in their lives matters more than our input. This is the apostolic pattern: prayer first, because it reveals where our trust actually lies.

Tuesday Ephesians 1:3-14

Paul's thanksgiving for the Ephesians is saturated with spiritual blessings—chosen, redeemed, forgiven—all grounded in God's work, not their achievement. Our gratitude as a church deepens when we learn to see every good quality in one another not as human virtue alone but as the evidence of God's grace at work in that person. This vertical lens transforms how we give thanks: we become people who recognize and celebrate God's activity in each other.

Wednesday Colossians 1:24-29

Paul labors and strains with all the energy God supplies in him, working toward one end: every person presented mature in Christ. This is pastoral love expressed as spiritual formation—not mere information transfer but the costly, persistent work of seeing believers grow into the fullness of Christ. We see here that Paul's longing for the Romans is not sentimental attachment but purposeful investment in their spiritual maturity.

Thursday Romans 15:7

Paul calls the church to welcome one another just as Christ welcomed us—a reciprocal embrace, not a one-directional flow from pastor to congregation. This implies that the pastor himself is built up, corrected, and encouraged by those he shepherds; no Christian is too advanced to need others. In our corporate life, we must resist the false hierarchy that makes pastors passive recipients and congregants active givers, embracing instead the reality that we all need each other.

Friday 2 Corinthians 5:18-20

Paul describes himself as an ambassador for Christ, appealing to people to be reconciled to God—a message not reserved for unbelievers alone but the animating center of all apostolic ministry. The gospel of reconciliation through Christ's death and resurrection is not a threshold we cross once but the perpetual lens through which we view all of life, all growth, all encouragement. When we minister to one another, we are not moving beyond the gospel; we are living ever more deeply within it.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. Paul opens this passage by thanking God for the Romans' faith and their reputation throughout the world. What is the difference between simply noticing that someone has genuine faith and what Paul does here—tracing that faith vertically to God's grace?
    Romans 1:8
    → How might this shift in how we think about gratitude change the way we encourage one another in our small group or church?
  2. Paul says he constantly remembers the Romans in his prayers and makes requests for them. Given that he has never visited them, what does the frequency and specificity of his prayer reveal about the nature of pastoral love?
    Romans 1:9-10
    → What might it mean for us that if Paul—an apostle—prioritized prayer for people he had never met, prayer itself may be one of the greatest measures of our love for someone?
  3. Paul expresses a longing to visit the Romans in person, and he has been hindered from doing so. Why would Paul emphasize this longing rather than simply being satisfied with his letters to them, and what does this tell us about the limits of mediated communication?
    Romans 1:10-11
    → In our own time, with all our technologies for staying connected, what is it about face-to-face presence that cannot be fully replaced?
  4. Paul says he wants to come to the Romans so that he might impart some spiritual gift and that they might be established. But then he adds something surprising: 'that is, that we may be mutually encouraged by each other's faith, both yours and mine.' What does Paul's expectation of being encouraged by people he has never met tell us about the nature of Christian community?
    Romans 1:11-12
    → How does this challenge any tendency we might have to think that spiritual maturity means we no longer need others or that younger believers have nothing to offer us?
  5. Paul describes himself as 'debtor both to Greeks and to barbarians, both to the wise and to the foolish.' How does this language of being a debtor shape Paul's motivation for gospel proclamation, and what does it suggest about how every Christian should view their obligation to share the gospel?
    Romans 1:14
    → What specific relationship or situation is the Spirit bringing to mind as you think about what you owe to someone through the grace that has been given to you?
  6. Throughout this passage, Paul's actions—thanksgiving, prayer, desire for presence, mutual encouragement, and gospel proclamation—all center on the gospel. Why is it essential that the gospel not merely be the entry point into Christian faith but the framework for everything we do in our relationships and witness?
    Romans 1:8-15

5 · Identifies the first pastoral expression—thanksgiving—and immediately notes the vertical dimension: Paul's gratitude for people is expressed as gratitude to God through Christ

6 · Distinguishes Christian gratitude from common gratitude: both recognize good in others, but Christian gratitude traces that good vertically to God's grace

You don't have to be a Christian to be grateful for things that you see in other people. And it's not like you can't ever say thank you and that you always have to say, I thank God for you. 7 · Identifies what Paul is not thankful for (worldly impressiveness, power, influence) and what he is thankful for (faith)
