Repent of your sins and place your faith and hope in King Jesus. And know he will take your hard and impenitent heart, this heart of stone, and he will give you a new living heart. A heart that can truly follow after him. Him.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we examine how the moralist's self-deception blinds them to their own sin, how God's judgment exposes that blindness, how Christ alone satisfies God's justice while offering salvation, and how the gospel transforms our hearts to genuinely obey.

Monday Matthew 7:1-5

Jesus exposes the spiritual hypocrisy that lies at the heart of moral judgment: we see the speck in our brother's eye while a plank obscures our own vision. The moralist in Romans 2, like the Pharisee in Matthew, mistakes moral superiority for righteousness, never grasping that sin blinds us to sin. We are called to examine ourselves by God's Word, not to construct a hierarchy of virtue based on comparing ourselves to others.

Tuesday Revelation 20:11-15

John's vision of the great white throne reveals the terror that awaits those whose names are not written in the Lamb's book of life—all found guilty according to their deeds. This is the judgment Paul warns the moralist faces: not a gentle correction, but the solemn, impartial verdict of the all-knowing God before whom all are exposed. The moralist who presumes they will escape this day because of their moral record or religious status faces a catastrophic awakening.

Wednesday 2 Corinthians 5:21

The exchange at the heart of the gospel shatters every moralist's delusion: Christ, the sinless one, was made sin for us so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God. This is not earned by our moral effort or religious knowledge—it is credited to us through faith alone. The Judge who will condemn all who refuse His salvation willingly bore the condemnation Himself, transforming judgment into mercy for all who believe.

Thursday Ezekiel 36:25-27

Ezekiel's prophecy of the new covenant reveals what the gospel accomplishes: God does not merely demand obedience from a hard heart, but removes it and replaces it with a heart of flesh—tender, responsive, alive to His Spirit. This transformation is not our work but God's sovereign grace, the same grace that breathed life into dry bones. The moralist's problem is not merely wrong behavior but a dead, resistant heart; the gospel's power is not moral instruction but regeneration itself.

Friday Ecclesiastes 8:11

The Preacher's observation rings true in every generation: "Because the sentence against an evil deed is not executed speedily, the heart of the sons of men is fully set to do evil." God's patience is routinely mistaken for permission, His kindness for weakness. But we must remember that every day of delay is an act of mercy calling us to turn—not an invitation to pile more wood on our account of judgment. The only right response to God's forbearance is not presumption but grateful repentance.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. Paul opens Romans 2 by addressing someone who judges others. What does he say this person is doing in verses 1-3, and what makes their position so contradictory?
    Romans 2:1-3
    → Can you think of a specific area where you might be tempted to judge another believer for something you yourself struggle with?
  2. In verse 2, Paul reminds us that God's judgment against those who practice sin is 'according to truth.' What does it mean that God judges by truth rather than by our self-assessment or our comparison to others?
    Romans 2:2
  3. Paul asks in verse 3, 'Do you suppose, O man, that you will escape the judgment of God?' Why does Paul believe the moralist has deceived themselves into thinking they might escape God's judgment?
    → What does it tell us about the human heart that we can convince ourselves we're exempt from God's righteous judgment?
  4. In verses 3-4, Paul asks another devastating question: 'Do you presume on the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience?' How does the moralist misunderstand God's kindness, and what does Paul say God's kindness is actually meant to produce?
    Romans 2:3-4
    → When you experience God's mercy or when judgment is delayed, what is your instinctive response — repentance, or continued sin?
  5. The sermon emphasizes that the moralist's 'hard and impenitent heart' stores up wrath for the day of judgment (verse 5). What's the difference between regret about our sin and genuine repentance, and why does that distinction matter for our standing before God?
    Romans 2:5
  6. The gospel announces that Christ took the wrath we deserved and gave us His righteousness (2 Corinthians 5:21), and that God gives believers a new heart (Ezekiel 36:25-27). How does the good news of Christ's substitution and the gift of a new heart directly answer the moralist's predicament in Romans 2?
    → If Christ has given you a new heart, what should be changing in how you relate to others and how you respond to God's kindness?
Sunday-evening family table

The Judge Who Became Our Savior

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to grapple with the shift from judgment to grace—the heart of Paul's message and the gospel. Listen for how your children understand that Jesus doesn't just judge us; He took our judgment so we could be saved.

In the sermon, Pastor Andrew said that Jesus is both the Judge (who sees all our sin) and our Savior (who took the punishment we deserved). If you knew Jesus was coming to judge you tomorrow, what would you be most worried about Him seeing? And now that you know He's also your Savior who took that punishment, how does that change the way you feel about Him?
works for ages 8+ — younger children can listen and respond with parent help; teens and adults engage at deeper levels of personal conviction and gospel comfort
Couples · three questions over coffee

Grace, Not Comparison
