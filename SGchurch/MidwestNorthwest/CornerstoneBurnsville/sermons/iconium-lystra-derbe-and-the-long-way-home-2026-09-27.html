Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc of the gospel's power through resistance: from inevitable division, through suffering's redemptive purpose, to the church's formation as Christ's enduring witness.

Monday Matthew 10:34

Jesus declares that He comes not to bring peace but a sword—the gospel's divisiveness is not a failure but its essential nature. When we proclaim that Christ alone deserves our worship and allegiance, we threaten every competing idol people have constructed, and that threat provokes opposition. This is not a sign the gospel is failing; it is proof the gospel is doing precisely what it was sent to do.

Tuesday John 15:18-19

The world's hatred of believers flows not from our failure but from our calling—we belong to Christ and bear witness against the world's idols. We were headed to deserved damnation, but God's undeserved grace in Christ has placed us on the narrow road, and that very rescue positions us as aliens to a world still enslaved by its loves. Our suffering, then, is not punishment but the expected cost of our chosen identity in Him.

Wednesday 2 Timothy 3:11-12

Paul reminds Timothy that persecution followed him in Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe—yet the Lord rescued him from all of it, proving that tribulation does not mean abandonment. Paul's assurance that "all who desire to live godly in Christ Jesus will be persecuted" is not a threat but a pattern, a confirmation that we walk the same road as the apostles and the cloud of witnesses before us. God's faithfulness to Paul in those cities is the pledge of His faithfulness to us when opposition comes.

Thursday Galatians 6:17

Paul bore the marks of Jesus in his body—scars from Lystra and other sufferings that testified to his union with Christ's suffering. These marks were not shameful badges but visible proofs that he had been bought at infinite cost and that his life was no longer his own but Christ's. When we embrace suffering as purposeful rather than accidental, we join Paul in carrying our crosses as trophies of grace, signs that we truly have died and our life is hidden with Christ in God.

Friday 1 Peter 2:19-20

Peter writes that suffering for doing good finds favor with God—a grace, not a curse. Paul and Barnabas demonstrated this priority when they retraced hostile territory not for comfort but to strengthen disciples and appoint elders, showing that evangelism is incomplete without forming believers into local churches under shepherd authority. Our suffering, when borne in submission to Christ and in service to His people, becomes the very instrument through which God shapes us and advances His kingdom in community, making us more like Jesus and securing our eternal weight of glory.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What pattern do you observe in Acts 14 about how people responded to Paul and Barnabas' proclamation of the gospel across the three cities—Iconium, Lystra, and Derbe? What does this tell us about what we should expect when the gospel is faithfully declared today?
    Acts 14:1-7; 2 Timothy 3:12
    → How does knowing this pattern ahead of time change the way you think about opposition or rejection you might face in sharing your faith?
  2. In Lystra, Paul and Barnabas are first treated as gods and then nearly killed by the same crowd. What does this dramatic reversal reveal about what the gospel actually threatens in people's hearts?
    Acts 14:11-20
    → Can you think of an area of your own life where you've sensed the gospel challenging something you were tempted to trust in or worship?
  3. After being stoned and left for dead in Lystra, Paul and Barnabas deliberately retrace their steps through the same hostile territory instead of taking the shorter route home. What does this choice—to strengthen, encourage, and appoint elders in the churches that had just rejected them—tell us about what Paul understood the mission to be?
    Acts 14:21-23
  4. The sermon emphasizes that Paul and Barnabas taught the new disciples 'through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God' (Acts 14:22). Why would this be a necessary and encouraging word for young believers who had just witnessed their apostles beaten and stoned? How does this differ from what many churches promise today?
    Acts 14:22
    → What would change in your own faith if you embraced this same teaching as true about your own Christian life?
  5. Looking at the pattern of the gospel in Acts 14—that it both converts people and divides families and communities—what does this tell us about attempts to soften or reshape the gospel to make it more palatable or culturally acceptable?
    Matthew 10:34; John 15:18-19
  6. The sermon points out that we were 'headed to deserved damnation' but God rescued us through undeserved grace in Christ. In light of this gospel reality, how should that shape the way we respond when we encounter hostility, misunderstanding, or rejection as we witness to others?
    Acts 14:15-17; 1 Peter 2:19-20
    → What would it look like this week to let your gratitude for undeserved grace compel you toward someone who needs to hear the gospel, regardless of whether you expect rejection?
