Instead of every family sending their firstborn son to serve in the temple, the Levites are going to be a substitute for all of you and they will serve. So when you meet the Levites, just be reminded they are substitutes taking your place, serving as mediators for you. But as a nation, all of Israel was meant to represent God to the whole world. These are the people who have the, the very presence of God dwelling in their midst, in the tabernacle and in the temple. These are the people who have God's revelation. So they, they know God's ways. And when they live them out, Deuteronomy says, all the nations are going to see you walking in God's ways. And they're going to think, what a wise people to have such wonderful laws as this. We want to know their God. We want to go to them so that we can meet this God also. That was the purpose for which they existed.

32 · Connects the return from exile to Isaiah's prophecy, showing that God's purpose in restoring his people was always doxological—they exist for his glory and praise

And Ezra's making it clear. The whole point of this return is to restore that priestly purpose. In Isaiah 43, there's a prophecy that God would restore his scattered people. His purpose is explicit in doing this. Bring my sons from afar and my daughters from the end of the earth, everyone who is called by my name. That's the return of exiles. Why? For what purpose? Whom I created for my glory. People created for the glory of God. That's their purpose. That's who they are. They exist to glorify God. Further down, Isaiah 43:20-21, my chosen people, the people whom I formed for myself, that they might declare my praise. This is why God undertook to have a people, people who exist for his own praise.

33 · Unpacks what it means to glorify God using Piper's telescope analogy: living in a way that brings God's greatness into view for others, making them desire to know him

God's people exist for the glory of God. What does it mean to glorify God? I can't improve on the definition I first came across. I think in my early 20s from John Piper. This helped me so much. To glorify God is a term we hear all the time. And Piper said helpfully to me, to glorify is like to magnify. Think about what it means to magnify something. A microscope magnifies something tiny and makes it bigger. So you get a sense of what it actually is, but that's not it. We're not trying to blow up God and enlarge him and make him look bigger than he actually is. To magnify could also be used in the sense of a telescope. A telescope takes things that are massive beyond our comprehension. And beautiful and glorious, but distant. And they appear to us like a tiny pinprick of light. And a telescope brings it into view. If you've ever seen pictures from the Hubble Space Telescope and you go, that's what a Crab Nebula is? That's what the Milky Way galaxy looks like? And just takes your breath away to think that's what's out there. To glorify God is to live your life like a telescope. Where through your life, people who have little to no concept of God at all begin to get some sense of the greatness and the glory and the goodness and the grace of God through you. Because they see in you that you treasure this God and they think this must be a good God. They see that you trust him, that you don't have any fear because you trust in God. That he's freed you from all of your guilt and sin and shame because you trust in this God. And they want that too. And through your life, they begin to see there is no greater good in all the world than this God himself, to know him, to trust him, to live for him. That's what it means to glorify God. That's what priestly people do. That's God's purpose for his people.

34 · Applies Israel's priestly identity to the New Testament church using 1 Peter 2 and Revelation 5, showing that believers today are also a royal priesthood existing to proclaim God's excellencies and exhibit his grace

It was his purpose for his people then, and it's his purpose for you today. 1 Peter 2 says, You— this is New Testament, Peter writing to an audience that includes Gentiles, not ethnic Jews— you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for his own possession, that you may proclaim the excellencies of him who called you out of darkness and into his marvelous light. Or Revelation 5, they sing this song to Jesus, the Lamb who was slain. They sang a new song saying, Worthy are you to take the scroll and to open its seals, for you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God from every tribe and language and people and nation. Why? To what end? You have made them a kingdom and priests to our God, and they shall reign on the earth. This is who you are if you are in Christ. You belong to God. Jesus shed his blood to own you and to make you into a kingdom and priests and to reign on the earth and through you to exhibit the glory of his grace. This is the, the purpose of your life.

35 · Applies the call to glorify God by confronting idolatry: whatever you delight in most is what you glorify, calling for repentance from lesser things and turning to Christ alone

Whatever you desire the most, whatever you most delight in, that's what you glorify. Just take a moment to, to consider what would your kids or your spouse, what would your co-workers or your friends, what would they say that you delight in the most? What do you live for? What consumes your purpose and your passion? Do you find your satisfaction and your security in your phone? In your money? In your comfort? In your job performance? Whatever you delight in the most, you say to people, this is the thing that satisfies me and secures me the most. And I pray that for all of us who are by nature sinful idolaters, that we would turn in repentance from every lesser thing that competes for our satisfaction and security and turn to Jesus Christ alone and find in him the satisfaction of our deepest desires and our greatest longing for joy.

36 · Introduces the third main point—God's people are planted—by highlighting the bookend verses emphasizing place and return to 'their towns

Finally, God's people are a planted people. The first and last verse in Ezra 2 function like bookends on this whole roster. Verse 1, these were the people of the province who came up out of the captivity. They returned to Jerusalem and Judah, each to his own town. Verse 70, now the priests, the Levites, some of the people, the singers, the gatekeepers, the temple servants lived in their towns, and all the rest of Israel in their towns. These verses would be like most of this chapter, easy to overlook, but the repetition and the placement at the beginning, at the end, like a bookmark, meant to grab our attention. There's an emphasis on place in this text.

37 · Traces God's promise of land from Abraham through Joshua, showing that God's promises are not just about people but about place—land that God gives and fulfills

God had made a promise to Abraham, not just about his descendants, but about place. Genesis 12:7, to your offspring I will give this land. God said that about the land of Canaan when Abraham was wandering there. Then he repeated that promise over and over. He repeated it to Isaac and to Jacob and to Moses and to Joshua. And God fulfilled that promise when Joshua led the people into the Promised Land. And he said, every place that you touch with the sole of your foot, I'm going to give give you that land and God did it. He promised the land and then he gave the land.

38 · Describes the fulfillment of God's land promise in Solomon's reign—peace and security throughout the land, each person settled under their own vine and fig tree

Fast forward through the story, you know that in the days of the judges there were crazy times and wars and chaos and sin. Saul and David, they fought against foreign armies and giants but finally in Solomon's day there was peace in the land and listen to how that's described in 1 Kings 4:25. And Judah and Israel lived in safety from Dan even to Beersheba, from the north to the south. It's like from the far north of Minnesota all the way down to the tip of Florida. Every man under his vine and under his fig tree all the days of Solomon. Every man under his vine and his fig tree.

39 · Connects Ezra 2's 'each to his own town' back to the promised land imagery and forward to Jeremiah's prophecy of God planting his people in the land again

That description here in Ezra 2 of returning exiles, each to his own town, is meant to evoke for us these memories of God's favor and his blessing and to stir hope. God's going to plant his people in this place. God has not given up on this messed up world. Just the opposite. God is keeping his very specific promise to return his people here in Ezra back to that land, just like he promised through Jeremiah. Listen to Jeremiah 24:6. I will set my eyes on them for good, and I will bring them back to this land. I will build them up and not tear them down. I will plant them and not pluck them up. To be planted by God. That's repeated in Jeremiah 32 and 42 and Isaiah 60, to be planted by God. God plants his people in a place.

40 · Establishes the theological pattern: God reveals himself as the one who plants people in place—from Adam in the garden to Israel in the land—intending his blessing to expand outward

One of the ways that God reveals himself, not just through speaking but through acting, God reveals himself as the God who parts seas and the God who provides manna from heaven and water out of rocks. And in this particular act of redemption, God is revealing himself as the God who plants people in a place. As Psalm 68:6 says, God settles the solitary in a home. God settles the solitary in a home. In the beginning, God planted a garden and he put Adam in that garden to work it and keep it. And then he told him, fill the earth and subdue it and take dominion. And so the, the picture is that Adam was supposed to extend that garden outward. Everything outside the garden was wilderness. But he was supposed to subdue the wilderness and exercise dominion by cultivating it. And then God planted Israel in a place and he meant for Israel to extend and expand his blessings outward to the rest of the nations.

41 · Articulates the eschatological vision: the promised land was never meant to stay small but to expand until the whole earth is filled with God's dominion and worship

So Psalm 2:8, God can say to his Son, ask of me and I will make the nations your heritage and the ends of the earth your possession. Or Psalm 72:8, praying this blessing on God's anointed king, may he have dominion from sea to sea and from the river to the ends of the earth. The vision is for this promised land, not to just be a little sliver of the earth, but to extend outward until the whole world is filled. Because remember, God made his people to be priestly people for his praise. And so God's plan is to fill the whole world with worship.

42 · Grounds the vision of worldwide worship in Habakkuk and Malachi's prophecies that the earth will be filled with the knowledge of God's glory and his name will be great among all nations

The prophet Habakkuk says, the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord as the waters cover the sea. I point this out every time I read that verse. Not just filled with the glory of God the way that the heavens display the glory of God, but filled with the knowledge of the glory of the Lord. That means people who know God and love him and delight in him. God's going to fill the world with people who worship him. Or Malachi 1:11, from the rising of the sun to its setting, my name will be great among the nations. From east to west, everywhere the sun shines, The name of God is going to be great and greatly praised.

43 · Applies the theology of place directly to the congregation: Sioux Falls matters, their gathering to worship is a fulfillment of prophecy about God filling the world with worship

So what does that mean for you? It means the place where you live really matters. God so loved the world that he gave his own Son. Sioux Falls matters. God has planted us as a congregation here on South Koval in Sioux Falls. How kind of God. When we gather to worship God in this city today, this is a place where, well, First Baptist started 150 years ago, 200 years ago, nobody was gathering and worshiping the name of Jesus in this place. So our being here and worshiping Jesus is a fulfillment of prophecies in Habakkuk and Malachi. God's filling the world with worship.

44 · Addresses a theological error—Gnosticism's devaluation of the physical—and reasserts Christian belief in the goodness of creation, the incarnation, and bodily resurrection

There's a Gnostic temptation to downplay the significance of physical place in the physical world and the importance of geography. But we're not Gnostics, we're Christians. We believe that God made the world and he made it good and he made it to be inhabited and he made it to be filled with his praise. And we believe that Jesus took on flesh not to deny the importance of flesh but to redeem our flesh and to guarantee that he will raise us from the dead with bodies like his.

45 · Applies the principle of being planted by calling the congregation to put down roots in a place with a people, acknowledging this doesn't have to be at his church but must be somewhere for the sake of their souls and fruitfulness

So let me just encourage you, don't underestimate the long-term impact beyond what you will see in your lifetime of putting down roots in a place with a people. Transient people are always moving from one place to the next, one thing to the next. And transient people can't bear a whole lot of load because they themselves are not very stable. But bearing the heavy weight of responsibility requires some stability, and stability comes with time. So if you are unsettled or non-committal, I want to encourage you to pray about putting down roots somewhere. I want to be clear, that doesn't have to be here. Maybe you're a visitor, maybe you've been worshiping with us for a few months. It does not have to be here, but it is significant to find a people with whom you can worship, to whom you can commit your life and belong and participate in life with in gospel community. Wherever you are, make sure that there is a congregation of Christians worshiping Jesus every week for the sake of your soul. And then ask God to establish you and to increase your load-bearing capacity, to make you a blessing to others.

46 · Draws the Christological conclusion: if Ezra 2 gave the exiles confidence in God's land promise, believers today have even greater confidence that the whole world belongs to Jesus

Ezra 2, it gave these returning exiles confidence that they had a claim to this land because this was the land God had promised to their forefathers and not just promised it but delivered it. How much more confidence should we have today knowing that this whole world belongs to Jesus who conquered sin and rose from the dead and sat down at the right hand of God the Father on high? From where he now rules over the nations and he is coming again. He bought this world with his blood and he deserves the worship of all people.

47 · Returns to the opening identity crisis problem and offers the solution—returning to God through Christ—then uses a rhetorical question about ancestral pride to set up the final application

The only way out of our present identity crisis, whether you personally don't know who you are or you realize as a nation we don't know who we are, the only way out of an identity crisis is to return to God through Jesus Christ. In repentance and faith. That's the answer for every people everywhere. At 1 Ezra 2, it looks irrelevant, but can you imagine if the name of one of your ancestors was listed here? You read the book today, would it make a difference to you that not much was said about them? Wouldn't that be an incredible privilege just to know right there, that's it, I come from Hodaviah and he was named in Scripture, Spirit-inspired Word of God? Wouldn't that Wouldn't that mean something to you?

48 · Elevates from Ezra 2's earthly roster to the ultimate roster—the Book of Life—warning of eternal consequences for those whose names are not found there

I want to end with this. The Bible says that there, there's another book called the Book of Life and it has names written in it. This is what Revelation 20 says about that book. I saw the dead, great and small, standing before the throne and books were opened. Then another book was opened which is the Book of Life and the dead were judged by what was written in the books. According to what they had done. And if anyone's name was not found written in the Book of Life, he was thrown into the lake of fire. It'd be one thing to have your name written in Spirit-inspired Scripture, but to have your name written in the Book of Life, there is nothing greater, nothing sweeter, nothing more comforting than that.

49 · Gives the gospel assurance: how to know your name is in the Book of Life—through faith in Christ alone—and issues urgent pastoral invitation for those uncertain to seek help immediately

How do you know? If you have repented of your sins, and turn to Jesus Christ alone to forgive you, trusting in his shed blood, his death on the cross, his resurrection from the dead as sufficient sacrifice to pay the debt that you could never pay, then your sins are forgiven and you can know that your name is written in the book of life. That when you stand before God, you will not hear words of condemnation. You won't be cast into the lake of fire, but you will be welcomed in to enjoy God and his presence with his people forever. There's nothing more important for you than to know that Jesus is your Savior and that your name is in that book. Do not leave here this morning without knowing that. If you're not sure, please come forward and talk to me, any of the prayer team afterwards, any of our pastors. Don't leave here without knowing that your name is written in that book.

50 · Final charge to believers: leave with full assurance of your identity as God's people, preserved for his praise

And if you know through Christ and the Spirit testifies with your spirit that you are God's children, then As you leave here, do so with full confidence and joy and assurance that you are the people of God, a people for His own possession whom God will preserve for the praise of His name to the ends of the earth.

Let's pray. Oh God, all Your works are wondrous works. Who could number all your ways? We think about them, we're overwhelmed and we're awed. But God, of all your works, there is none as great and glorious as the work that you did when you sent your Son Jesus to become a man and to die in the place of sinners. And what a joy it is for us week after week to gather to magnify Jesus' name. We pray that you'd be pleased to, to make our lives like telescopes that would bring your greatness and your glory into focus for people around us. We know that all over this city where you've planted us are people who don't know you at this moment, people who are not worshiping you, not walking with you. God, would you make our lives a witness? A testimony. Would you keep us and preserve us by your grace so that we might live and praise you? Establish us, O God. Establish the work of our hands. May the lives we live, the words we speak, the things that we give ourselves to in the lives that you've given us in our lifetime— may these things serve by your grace to further your kingdom on earth. In the glory of Jesus, until this world is filled with the knowledge of the Lord as the waters that cover the sea. Amen. Let's stand together.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace God's covenantal faithfulness through the preservation of his people: from the ancient promise of belonging, through Israel's priestly calling, to our present identity in Christ and our strategic placement as his witnesses.

Monday Exodus 6:7

This foundational covenant formula echoes throughout Scripture and establishes the exclusive, reciprocal bond that makes God's people *his* people. When we grasp that God has claimed us as his own possession, we understand why the genealogy in Ezra 2—with all its specific names and numbers—matters: each person listed belongs to the God who bound himself to them in covenant. This same God binds himself to us through Christ, making us irreversibly his.

Tuesday Genesis 3:15

The promise of the offspring who would crush the serpent's head was not abstract—it required the preservation of a specific people, a faithful lineage through which Christ would come. Ezra 2's roster, seemingly mundane, is actually the testimony that God protected that genealogical promise through exile and return. In the same way, God preserves us today not for our comfort but for his purposes: that through us, the gospel of Christ might advance and his name be glorified.

Wednesday Exodus 19:5-6

The priests and Levites listed in Ezra 2:36-40 were not merely functional clergy—they embodied Israel's corporate identity as a priesthood, mediating God's presence and teaching his ways. Their return to rebuild the temple was not just restoration; it was the renewal of Israel's priestly vocation in the world. We too are called to this same priestly function: to stand between heaven and earth, proclaiming God's excellencies and embodying his holiness in our workplaces, neighborhoods, and relationships.

Thursday 2 Samuel 7:12-16

David's dynasty was promised an everlasting throne, yet the monarchy fell and exile seemed to end that hope. The return of the exiles, cataloged in Ezra 2, proves that God's promise was not empty—he was preserving the line through which the true King would come. Every name in that roster carried forward the messianic hope; every family's restoration was an act of God's faithfulness to his word. In Christ, that promise reaches its fulfillment, and we inherit all the covenant blessings secured by his person and work.

Friday 1 Peter 2:9-10

We are not preserved by genealogy or ethnicity, but by union with Christ—the true seed of Abraham in whom all God's promises are yes and amen. Like the exiles who returned to rebuild the temple and restore worship, we are strategically placed by God in our communities, our workplaces, and our families to display his glory and call others into his kingdom. Our identity is not earned by merit but granted by mercy; our purpose is not self-directed but oriented toward his praise; our placement is not accidental but providential.
