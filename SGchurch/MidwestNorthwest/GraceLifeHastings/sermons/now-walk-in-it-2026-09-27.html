Theological claims· 18
  1. When God saves you, He gives you all of Jesus—complete qualification, deliverance, transfer, redemption, and forgiveness—making these finished realities for everyone who is in Christ. unit #3
  2. We possess Christ completely and yet are not yet complete Christians—we have everything we need in Him and a lifetime of growth in front of us. unit #4
  3. Christ being completely sufficient does not mean we have nowhere left to grow—the difference is not between Christ being insufficient and sufficient, but between us being immature and mature. unit #7
  4. Christians often wrongly take the sufficiency of Christ and turn it into an excuse for a lack of growth, but sanctification and maturity are not optional—they are everything. unit #8
  5. The gospel that justifies you also changes you—grace doesn't just get you through the door, it teaches you how to walk. unit #9
  6. God wants the truth of His revealed will in our heads because He wants obedience in our hands and hearts—knowing God's will is not about information but about transformation. unit #13
  7. You can possess extensive biblical knowledge—regular Bible reading, catechism memorization, seminary training, theological precision—and still remain unbelievably immature in character and conduct. unit #14
  8. Doctrine and theological knowledge matter enormously—Paul is praying for knowledge, and Christianity does not become more spiritual by becoming less thoughtful. unit #15
  9. Knowledge of God's will must become wisdom—understanding how to live in God's world in ways that please God, shaping our decisions, relationships, work, speech, and instincts so that we become humble, holy, patient people rather than theologically informed people who are difficult to be around. unit #16
  10. Because Christ is enough—which is what Colossians is all about—He is Lord over all of life or not at all; His sufficiency means no area of our lives can be withheld from His direction. unit #18
  11. While works contribute nothing to justification and justification by faith alone is true, justified people do not remain unchanged—Christ justifies no one whom He does not at the same time sanctify, because the Christ who makes you righteous also makes you holy. unit #21
  12. The gospel is not just a one-time message for conversion—it keeps working continuously in believers, producing in us the character of what we have received (grace, forgiveness, mercy, service). unit #24
  13. Ordinary weekly Christianity—hearing the Word, praying, singing, receiving the Supper, confessing sins, forgiving, serving, suffering, and returning—matters enormously because through these repeated rhythms, grace upon grace, the gospel bears fruit over time, often in ways we don't immediately notice. unit #26
  14. While experiences never replace Scripture, there is a knowledge of God that deepens over time as His Word is believed, obeyed, and held onto—truths we can affirm at 20 we come to know existentially at 70 through our own pain, suffering, and endurance. unit #29
  15. Anyone can be patient for a short time, but enduring faithfully when the marriage remains difficult, prayers remain unanswered, circumstances remain unchanged, and pain persists—that requires God's power. unit #32
  16. God's power is displayed not only when He removes burdens and suffering—though He sometimes does—but also when He makes us strong enough to keep carrying them faithfully without bitterness or anger. unit #33
  17. Christianity is not a story of decent people getting better but a story of rescue—God delivering enslaved people who could not escape themselves from the dominion of darkness. unit #40
  18. We should live differently and pursue growth because we live in a different kingdom now with a different citizenship—grace produces obedience out of allegiance to the King we belong to. unit #42

0 · The pastor reads the primary text passage (Colossians 1:9-14) in full, establishing the foundation for the sermon's exposition

Asking that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding, so as to walk in a manner worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing to him, bearing fruit in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God, being strengthened with all power according to his glorious might for all endurance and patience with joy. 1 · Opens the sermon with a contemporary illustration about discovering untapped capabilities in a smartphone to establish the sermon's central analogy—that Christians possess Christ completely but often fail to understand the full extent of what they have in Him

You know, you can own something for a really long time before you figure out and understand what you really have. I think about my phone like that. Yeah, I can— I've had, you know, a smartphone for a few years now, and it never fails. Sometimes I may see a video online about something else that the iPhone can do, and I'm blown away. I think, I never knew that I could do that. It feels like it opens up this door of opportunity, a new task, a new skill. That was available to me this whole time and I never knew it.

2 · Extends the opening illustration with an example of infants who discover one function of a toy and fixate on it while remaining unaware of its fuller capabilities, reinforcing the pattern of possessing something without fully understanding it

I think children, infants particularly, can be like this. You know, they get something that maybe they've been wanting and they discover it can do this one thing and they get so enamored by this one thing they just keep doing it over and over again. And we as parents looking in saying, well, you know, it can do more than that. Like, it can do more than make noise when we're all trying to go to bed. It can do more than that.

3 · Transitions from illustration to theological assertion, establishing the core tension of the sermon: God gives us all of Christ in salvation (complete justification and belonging) rather than partial forgiveness or conditional salvation

There are things that you can own for a long time before we fully understand what's at our disposal. And the Christian life is sometimes just like that. When God saves you, He doesn't give you just part of Jesus. He doesn't forgive half of your sins and then leave you to see how you do with the rest. And what Paul is telling us this morning in Colossians 1 is that the Father, He has done several things for us. He has qualified us, He has delivered us, He has transferred us, and that He has done in His beloved Son. And not only that, in Him we have redemption, the forgiveness of our sins. These things are real realities for us. If you are in Christ, you really belong to Him.

4 · Establishes the sermon's controlling tension: complete possession of Christ coexists with Christian immaturity

And yet, even though you may have belonged to Him for several years now, you have barely begun to understand what that means and all that that entails. There is still so much to learn. There's so much sin to put to death. There's so much wisdom to grow in. So much fruit to bear in our lives. There are trials that are heading our way unexpectedly that we don't know about, that we're going to need endurance for, that we don't have right now. There's difficult people in our lives that we need to understand patience and grow in patience with. There's joy and thanksgiving that we have just barely begun to know. And that's one of the wonderful tensions in the Christian life. We possess Christ completely. You, as a Christian, you have Christ completely. And yet, you're not yet a complete Christian. Yes, you possess Christ completely. But you are not yet a complete Christian. You have everything you need in him and you have a lifetime of growth in front of you.

5 · Returns to the text to identify the addressees and purpose of Paul's prayer

That's the reality and tension of the Christian life and that's why Paul prays the way that he does in this passage. In this passage, he's not praying for non-Christians or unbelievers to become Christians. In this passage, he's praying for Christians, people who already have faith in Jesus, people who already love one another, people whose hope is already in heaven. And what does Paul want for these people who already belong to Jesus? What does he want? He wants them to grow. He wants them to mature.

6 · Exposits Colossians 1:9, tracing Paul's response sequence: the Colossians heard the gospel, it produced fruit, Paul heard about the fruit, and he began praying continuously for them

And so from the day we heard, we have not ceased to pray for you. Asking that you may be filled with the knowledge of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding. Colossians 1:9. And what Paul in this prayer does is he shows us what grace intends to grow in the Christian life. He shows us what grace is intending to grow in your life. Last week Paul told us in the first 8 verses of Colossians that the gospel had been bearing fruit among the Colossians since the day you heard of it. And now Paul is saying, from that day, from the day we heard about your faith, we haven't stopped praying for you. I have not stopped praying for you. They heard the gospel, it produced fruit, Paul heard about that fruit, And what did he start doing? He started praying for them. And what does he pray? That you may be filled with the knowledge of his will and all spiritual wisdom and understanding.

7 · Addresses a potential objection—if the Colossians already know God, why pray they be filled?—and uses it to drive toward a crucial doctrinal distinction: Christ's sufficiency does not eliminate the need for growth because the issue is not Christ's insufficiency but our immaturity

But wait a minute, these people already know God. They know him. They've believed the gospel. Paul's just given thanks to God for their faith in Jesus Christ, their love for the saints, their hope in heaven. He's already called them faithful brothers and now he's praying that they would be filled. Because evidently the idea that Christ is enough doesn't mean that you have nowhere left to grow. The truth that Christ is enough That you have all of Christ does not mean you have nowhere left to grow, that you can stop growing, or that there is not growth still in front of you. In fact, Christ being enough for us means exactly the opposite because there is a difference between saying Christ is insufficient and saying that you are immature. Christ is completely sufficient, and yet we are immature. We need to grow. Christ doesn't lack anything, but we do. I do. And it's not Christ who needs to grow, I do.

8 · Identifies and refutes a common theological error: using Christ's sufficiency as justification for spiritual passivity

And one of the mistakes that Christians often make is taking the sufficiency of Christ and turning it into an excuse for a lack of growth. I'm already saved by grace, Jesus paid it all, my righteousness is in Christ, so I don't need to be too concerned about this holiness and sanctification thing. I mean, when the judgment falls, I'm okay, I'm safe, so why do I need to be so concerned about this growth idea? Or this sanctification idea, about this putting to death sin business? Why— what exactly is all of this talk about maturity? Well, in short, it's everything.

9 · Establishes the connection between justification and sanctification through metaphor

Because the gospel that justifies you also changes you. And the Christ who forgives your sins doesn't just forgive your sins, He makes a claim on your life. Grace doesn't just get you through the door and then bring you into the household and leave you sitting in the foyer. That's not what grace does. It doesn't leave you on the porch. Grace teaches you. It teaches you how to walk.

10 · Returns to exposition of Colossians 1:9 with focus on Paul's prayer that believers be filled with knowledge of God's will

And that's exactly where Paul goes. Paul prays that they will be filled with the knowledge of His will, that they will be filled with the knowledge of His will. Now even as I say that, I realize I think that filled is one of those words that I can't shake my southern accent from, so if you hear it oddly, you know what I mean. F-I-L-L-E-D, not F-E-E-L-E-D, but that you would be filled Is that better? With the knowledge of his will in all spiritual wisdom and understanding, verse 9.

11 · Addresses a common misunderstanding about what 'God's will' means, contrasting the popular conception (discovering God's secret individual life plan through spiritual discernment) with what Paul actually means in this passage

And now again, we often hear God's will and our minds go to somewhere Paul isn't wanting us to go. We like to think, well, what's God's will for my life? Should I take this job or should I take that one? Should I move or should I not? Should I buy this house? Should I go to this college? And Christians spend enormous amount of time and energy trying to discover this secret plan that God may have for their lives hidden under a rock somewhere. We make God's will sound like it's this giant scavenger hunt that we go searching for, and if we're good enough and if we're spiritual enough, we will discern all the clues for God's will. But that's not the way that Paul is talking about God's will here.

12 · Provides the correct interpretation of 'God's will' in Colossians 1:9—not secret providence but revealed will that shapes thinking and living

He's not praying that the Colossians would become experts at deciphering God's secret providence. He's praying that they would know God's revealed will so much, so thoroughly, so deeply, that it shapes the way that they think and live in the world. It shapes the way they think about the things they encounter, the news they see on the television, the way that they live lives together, and it influences the way that they live. And we know that that's what he means here because he says, so as to walk in a manner worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing to him, in verse 10. This knowledge is intended to lead us somewhere.

13 · Establishes the relationship between knowledge and obedience, asserting that biblical knowledge exists for the purpose of producing submission of heart and hands

Paul, he wants the truth of God's word, the truth of God's revealed will in our heads because he wants obedience in our hands and in our hearts. He wants your head to be filled because he wants your heart and your hands to be submissive. That's what God wants. He wants spiritual wisdom and understanding, not just information. Knowing God's will is not about information, it's about transformation, and there's a difference.

14 · Illustrates the information-transformation distinction through a series of concrete examples showing how extensive biblical knowledge can coexist with profound immaturity

You can know an enormous amount about Christianity and stay unbelievably immature. You can do your Bible reading every year, you can attend all of the Bible studies, students, you can memorize your catechism questions, you can do your memory verses and still be unbelievably immature. You can go to seminary and still be rude to your wife. You can explain theology and debate it with the best of them and be dishonest at work. You can defend Calvinism with the best of them and be impatient with your children. With your children. You can have a settled doctrine and be very unpleasant to be around. I don't know if you knew that, but you can. I've seen it. I've been it.

15 · Corrects against a potential misreading of the previous unit—that if knowledge can coexist with immaturity, knowledge must not matter

Because knowing things, it matters. Doctrine matters enormously. Theology is critical. I mean, Paul's praying for it. Don't say knowledge doesn't matter. Paul's praying for knowledge here. To say that knowledge doesn't matter is to deny what Paul is praying for right here. Christianity, it doesn't become more spiritual by becoming less thoughtful. That doesn't happen.

16 · Defines wisdom as the proper end of knowledge—truth applied to living in God's world in ways that please God

But this knowledge of God's will is supposed to become wisdom. And wisdom is understanding the truth that we know how to live in God's world in ways that please God. That's what Paul wants. That's what God wants for your life. He wants Christians who know Him so well that His truth shapes our decisions, our marriages, our work, our money, our speech, our instincts. He wants knowledge that will make us humble. And holy and patient. He doesn't want us to have a kind of knowledge that makes us difficult to have dinner with. Paul wants us and God wants us to have knowledge that makes us wise.

17 · Demonstrates the comprehensive scope of Paul's prayer by listing its components and then applying the comprehensiveness principle: God does not want partial devotion or sectioned-off obedience but gospel influence throughout the entire life—thoughts, loves, habits, reactions, ambitions, work, marriage, speech, suffering, weekdays, and weekends

And notice as Paul is praying these things how comprehensive this prayer is. That we would be filled with all knowledge, walking worthy, fully pleasing to the Lord, bearing fruit in every good work, increasing in the knowledge of God, strengthened with power, enduring patiently, and giving thanks. It's pretty comprehensive, isn't it? Because God, in saving us and calling us and giving us knowledge and growing us, He is not after just our obedience and our submission and our devotion in one tiny corner of our lives. He wants the Gospel to work through your whole life. Every area of your life. Your thoughts. What you love. Your habits. How you react to people. What you're ambitious for. Your work. Your marriage. The way that you talk. How you suffer. He wants your Monday morning and your Sunday morning. He wants every room in your house to be influenced and directed by this gospel.

18 · Applies the comprehensiveness principle to the sermon's central theological theme (Christ's sufficiency), arguing that Christ being enough means no area of life is exempt from His lordship

That's why when we say Christ is enough, which is what Colossians is all about, Christ is enough, that should never produce lazy, stagnant Christians. If Christ is enough, there is nowhere in our lives we say, well, Jesus doesn't get this part. He doesn't get to speak into this part. He doesn't get to direct this area of my life. No, he is Lord over all or not at all. That is Christ being enough.

19 · Begins exposition of verse 10's call to walk worthy by first addressing the theological anxiety this language produces in evangelical listeners—concern that 'worthy' implies earning salvation

And the purpose of this knowledge is that we would walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing to Him in verse 10. Now, when we hear the word worthy, as good evangelicals, we get a little nervous, we start to squirm a little bit, and that's okay because we don't want to ever imagine that we somehow make ourselves worthy of salvation. We want to avoid that idea. Paul doesn't mean walk in a manner worthy of the Lord that Jesus does His part and now we live well enough to deserve the forgiveness that God gave to us. That's not what's being communicated here. What's not being communicated is that somehow you need to live in a way that has earned what Jesus did for you.

20 · Provides the positive interpretation of walking worthy—living in a way that fits the name we already bear through belonging to Christ

But it does mean walk in a manner worthy of the Lord. And what that means is that we live a life that fits the name that we belong to, the name that is over us. We fit who we belong to. Just like our children. Our children don't obey so that they earn the family name. They already have it. It belongs to them. It's on them. They bear the family name, and because they bear it, parents can look at their children and say, Live in a way that honors this name. Live in a way that brings honor to this name, not reproach to this name. That's what Paul is saying. He's saying Christ has received you and purchased you and transferred you. Now live like someone who belongs to it. Honor the name.

21 · Establishes the Reformed theological framework for the relationship between justification and sanctification

Our works certainly contribute nothing to our justification, but justification by faith alone doesn't mean that justified people remain unchanged. John Calvin said that Christ justifies no one whom He does not at the same time sanctify. Yes, justification and sanctification are worthy of being distinguished. We don't— but we also don't separate them because Christ doesn't separate them. The Christ who makes you righteous also makes you holy because you belong to Him.

22 · Addresses potential discomfort with the language of pleasing God by appealing to the parent-child analogy

And that's why Paul can say, fully pleasing to Him. We can live in ways that please God, and that shouldn't frighten us. It should delight us. I mean, moms and dads, you can understand this. Your child doesn't become your child by pleasing you. They are your children. But precisely because they're your children, their obedience can genuinely please you. Not to make them your children or to make them more of your children, but because they are. And children should want to please their parents. It should delight them to please their parents. And this is how we are.

23 · Exposits the content of the worthy walk (bearing fruit and increasing in knowledge) and establishes the verbal connection between verse 6 (gospel bears fruit and increases) and verse 10 (believers bear fruit and increase)

And what does this worthy walk look like? Bearing fruit and increasing in the knowledge of God, verse 10. Now, we heard those words last week in verse 6, the gospel is bearing fruit and increasing. And now again in verse 10, you are bearing fruit in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God. The Gospel, it bears fruit and increases. The people who believe the Gospel do what? They bear fruit and increase. The Gospel produces life in the people who believe it.

24 · Establishes the ongoing, continuous nature of the gospel's work through a series of metaphors (light, engine, seed) and then applies the principle concretely: grace received produces grace given, forgiveness received produces forgiveness given, mercy received produces mercy given, service received produces service given

That's why the Gospel isn't just a message that we believe once so that we can be forgiven and converted to Christianity. No, the gospel, it keeps working. It comes in, invades, and then continues to work. The light gets turned on and it never goes off. The engine gets started and never fails. It keeps running. The seed goes into the ground and then it starts producing fruit. Grace produces gracious people. Forgiveness produces people that forgive. Mercy makes us merciful people. The fact that we've been served by Christ makes us into people who are willing to serve others. The Gospel bears fruit.

25 · Illustrates the varied forms gospel fruit-bearing takes—sometimes dramatic and instantaneous (conversion of an addict, restoration of a marriage, Paul's conversion), sometimes quiet and incremental (slightly less anger, choosing forgiveness over scorekeeping, choosing honesty, learning generosity, practicing confession, enduring suffering without bitterness)

And sometimes it can be dramatic. A man is converted and his whole life changes in the blink of an eye. An addiction gets broken, a marriage gets restored, a person like the Apostle Paul who was hostile to Christianity suddenly becomes one of its greatest defenders and proponents. But sometimes it's less dramatic. It can be kind of quiet. It can look like a man who becomes slightly less angry than he used to be, a wife that learns to forgive instead of keeping score against her spouse, a teenager who chooses honesty when lying would get him out of trouble. It could be a Christian learning to give generously without needing praise for it. A husband that is willing to say, I was wrong, will you forgive me? It could be a family that suffers for years without becoming bitter toward God.

26 · Applies the quiet-growth principle to ordinary Christian life, arguing that weekly rhythms of Word, prayer, worship, Supper, confession, forgiveness, service, and suffering are the means of grace that produce fruit over time

That growth is not the kind of growth that makes headlines, But God notices it and God's producing it. This is one of the reasons why ordinary weekly Christianity matters so much. We hear the Word, we pray, we sing, we receive the Supper, we confess our sins, we forgive one another, we serve, we suffered, and we come back next Sunday. And we do it all again, week after week, year after year, grace upon grace. And along the way, sometimes we don't even notice it in ourselves, the gospel bears fruit.

27 · Illustrates the long-term fruit of ordinary Christianity by pointing to older believers in whom decades of gospel work have produced a settled character—reduced anxiety, quicker forgiveness, diminished appeal of bitterness

We see this particularly in older saints who've walked with the church for decades. When the gospel has been working in someone's life for 30, 40, 50 years, there's something settled there. Things that maybe make many of us anxious don't make those saints anxious anymore. Forgiveness can be offered more quickly because bitterness is just less appealing. It's fruit.

28 · Identifies the circular dynamic in Paul's prayer: knowledge leads to walking, and walking leads to increased knowledge

And that's what Paul's praying for. He begins by praying that they would be filled with the knowledge of God's will, and then as they walk and bear fruit, they are increasing in the knowledge of God. Notice that circle there. Knowing God teaches you how to walk, and walking with God teaches you to know Him.

29 · Addresses a potential theological error (elevating experience above Scripture) while affirming that there is a deepening, experiential knowledge of God that comes through believing, obeying, and holding onto Scripture over time

Not because experiences are better guides than Scripture. That's an error to be rejected. Experiences never replace Scripture, but there is a knowledge of God that deepens in us as His Word is believed and obeyed and held onto. Over time, you discover that God really will not leave me or forsake me. You really realize that God provides for me always. You see God's hand and you learn over time that He really can be trusted, that His grace really is sufficient. At 20 years old, we can affirm those things. A 70-year-old Christian often knows them, not just through affirmation, but knows them through their own pain and their own suffering, through their own endurance. This is what the gospel produces.

30 · Begins exposition of verse 11 by highlighting the language of divine power ('strengthened with all power according to His glorious might') and setting up an expectation: such grand language suggests some spectacular purpose is coming

And then Paul prays that they would be strengthened with all power, strength for ordinary faithfulness. According to His glorious might for all endurance and patience with joy. Now, this starts off like a big statement. Strengthened with all power according to His glorious might. I mean, when we hear those words, if it stopped there, we would presume that something enormous was coming right after that. That you would be strengthened with all power according to His glorious might. I mean, what is all this divine power for? I mean, we are given divine power to raise the dead, To make blind see, to make the deaf hear, to move mountains, we might presume?

31 · Subverts the expectation created in the previous unit by revealing what Paul actually prays divine power will accomplish: endurance, patience, and joy

Is it for— are we strengthened with all power for these extraordinary spiritual experiences that we hear about others having? What does Paul say? Strengthened with all power according to His glorious might for all endurance. And patience with joy. All the glorious might of God. And what does it look like in the Christian life? Sometimes it looks like just not giving up. Just keep on going. Just waking up and taking one more step. Endurance It means we just are willing to stay under the burden. Patient. How we bear under these difficult circumstances and difficult people. You need to endure. For all endurance and patience with joy You need the glorious might of God to do that.

32 · Applies the endurance principle by contrasting short-term patience (5 minutes, a difficult week) with long-term endurance (5 years, ongoing marriage difficulties, unanswered prayer, unchanged financial situations, persistent job burdens, unrelieved pain)

Anybody can be patient for 5 minutes. What's hard is to be patient and endure for 5 years. Anyone can go through a difficult week, but what about when the marriage remains difficult? What about the things that you've prayed for and they just haven't happened? Nothing has changed. What about when your financial position has not changed? When your job is still an enormous burden? When the pain is still there tomorrow? That is where we need power.

33 · Contrasts two manifestations of divine power: removal of burdens (healing, deliverance, answered prayer for relief) and strength to carry burdens faithfully (endurance without bitterness or anger)

We like to imagine that God's power is displayed when he takes those things away. The painful circumstances, the painful moments, the painful weeks, and sometimes it is. God heals, God removes, He changes, God answers prayers in that way, and we are grateful for them. But God's power isn't only displayed by taking away burdens and taking away suffering. Sometimes it's displayed by making you strong enough to keep carrying them faithfully, without bitterness, without anger.

34 · Shifts from teaching mode to direct pastoral address, honoring believers in the congregation who have demonstrated long-term endurance through terrible circumstances while continuing to worship, pray, and trust

Sometimes the enormous work of God, of His glorious might, is seen in that Christian who simply doesn't quit. They get up, they trust Christ, and they do the next faithful thing, and the next day they do it again. And there's people right here in this room that have done that, walked through the most terrible of circumstances and yet still worship, still pray, still trust. That is God's glorious might and power at work. Some of the strongest Christians you know will never look strong to other people because they're just— they're still here, still believing, still praying, still serving, still forgiving.

35 · Exposits the 'with joy' component of verse 11, distinguishing Christian joy from both stoicism (gritting teeth) and denial (pretending pain doesn't exist)

And we do this with joy. They do this with joy. I mean, different than just gritting your teeth until it's over. No, it's with joy. Not a joy that pretends it doesn't hurt. I mean, we know that the Bible doesn't ask us to pretend that things don't hurt. But this is a joy that runs deep. That says, this hurts, but God is still good. I don't understand this, but Christ is enough. I know He has not abandoned me. That is God's glorious might.

36 · Begins exposition of verse 12, connecting thanksgiving to maturity and understanding of God's work

And then Paul prays that they would give thanks to the Father. Verse 12, this is what it means to walk worthy. A mature Christian becomes increasingly thankful because they understand what God has done. Giving thanks to the Father who has qualified you to share in the inheritance of the saints in light.

37 · Emphasizes the completed nature of God's qualifying work through repetition and grammatical analysis

God has qualified you. Who qualified you? The Father did. He has done it. And that's past tense. It is done. It's not that you need to qualify yourself, it's that he has qualified you to share in the inheritance of the saints in light.

38 · Traces the biblical-theological trajectory of 'inheritance' from God's promise to Abraham, through the Exodus and wilderness wanderings, to the Colossian Gentiles who now share in that inheritance despite having no ethnic claim to it

Now, inheritance is a word that has a long history behind it. God promised Abraham a land. He brought Israel out of Egypt. He brought them through the wilderness. He gave his people their inheritance. And now Paul takes that word inheritance and applies it to these Christians in Colossae, many of whom were Gentiles. Their ancestors didn't go across the Red Sea. They can't trace their family line back to the 12 tribes. They had no familial claim on Israel's inheritance. And Paul says, but you have an inheritance among the saints because you belong to Christ. This is what God had promised Abraham from the beginning. In you, Abraham, all the families of the earth shall be blessed. And now here he says Gentiles in Colossae share in the inheritance of God's holy people because they are in Christ.

39 · Transitions from qualification (v

And it's not that they qualified for it, God qualified them. That is grace from start to finish. And Paul isn't finished. He says, He has delivered us from the domain of darkness. You, outside of Christ, were not just a confused person. You weren't just lacking some new information. Outside of Christ, we were all under dominion. We were in the realm of sin and death, blindness and rebellion, and God delivered us.

40 · Reframes the gospel as rescue narrative rather than self-improvement narrative

Christianity is not a story of decent people getting better. Christianity is a story of rescue, of God delivering us from a dominion. God came for a people who were enslaved and they could not escape themselves.

41 · Exposits the transfer from domain of darkness to kingdom of the beloved Son (v

He delivered us from the domain of darkness and transferred us to the kingdom of his beloved Son. He took us from one dominion to another, from one kingdom to another. God did not just rescue us out of this dominion and leave us standing in in neutral ground. No, He took us to Christ's kingdom, to His kingdom. God has changed our allegiances and now we have a King that we belong to. Our bodies belong to Him, our money belongs to Him, our calendar belongs to Him, and this is where Paul's prayer for a worthy walk makes sense.

42 · Applies the transfer theology to the sermon's call for growth, answering why believers should pursue transformation: because they now belong to a different kingdom with a different King

Why should you live differently? Why should you be concerned about growth? Because you live in a different kingdom now. You have a different citizenship. God has transferred you. So now walk like a citizen of that kingdom where you belong. Grace has done this. And this grace has produced, it produces this obedience out of this allegiance.

43 · Exposits verse 14's declaration of redemption and forgiveness, emphasizing the present-tense possession of both ('we have') rather than future hope or conditional offer

And then Paul finishes, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of our sins. We have redemption, not might have it, not working toward it, not that God will consider giving it to us if we make enough progress. We have it. Why? Because we have it in the beloved Son. You have redemption because you are in Christ. You have forgiveness because you're in Him.

44 · Applies the sermon's completed-work theology to anxious believers who doubt their standing with God or fear their performance affects their acceptance

So your growth is not about convincing God to accept you. You have been accepted. You're in. But because you're in, now grow. That is the Christian life. And some of us need to hear both sides of that. Some of you need to hear, you're in. Stop living as if God's acceptance somehow is going to rise or fall with your performance this week. Some of you need to hear that. You're in. Stop worrying about being in. Stop worrying about earning his love or earning your inheritance. Stop worrying about it. You have been qualified. You belong. You're in. And your performance this week isn't going to change that. You need to hear that. He has delivered you.

45 · Balances the previous unit by addressing believers tempted toward spiritual passivity who use grace as justification for lack of growth

But some of you need to hear the other side. Now grow. You're in, now grow. Don't take the grace of God and turn it into spiritual lethargy or laziness. Don't say, well, Christ is enough so I can make peace with the sin that He died to save me from. Don't spend the next 10 years of your life without growing in knowledge, wisdom, holiness, love, joy. Yes, Christ is enough, and because He is enough, there is a lifetime of growth ahead of you because the gospel doesn't leave you where it finds you. It forgives you there, and then grace begins to train you to walk.

46 · Issues concrete application to pray Paul's prayer personally throughout the coming week, providing believers with specific language adapted from the passage

So as you go through this week, pray this prayer. Pray this prayer with Paul. Fill me with the knowledge of your will. Give me wisdom. Teach me to walk in a way that pleases you because you have delivered me. Not so that I can get into the kingdom, but because you've already transferred me there. To transfer me to the kingdom of His beloved Son.

47 · Signals the end of this sermon and previews the next section of Colossians (the Christological hymn beginning in verse 15)

Christ is enough. And pretty soon Paul is about to show us why. Who is this King whose kingdom we have been brought into? Well, Paul is about to tell us He is the image of the invisible God.

48 · Provides the sermon's closing frame, synthesizing the sermon's central tension into a final charge

God. And when we see who He really is, the idea that you might need something beyond Him looks pretty foolish. You haven't outgrown the gospel. You never will. And you have a lifetime ahead of you of walking in what God has already given you in Christ. So the call this morning is that every one of us would rest in what God has done, and while resting, keep growing. Keep growing.

49 · Closing pastoral prayer that rehearses the sermon's core tension (complete in Christ yet called to grow), asks God for help embracing both realities, prays for fervency in growth, asks for ability to recognize growth in one another, gives thanks for visible evidence of God's power in the congregation, and asks for increased filling with knowledge that produces wisdom and corresponding life

Let's pray. God, would you please help us to embrace both of these realities, that in you we are complete, that in you where we have forgiveness of sins, full redemption, that we have acceptance and love and peace. But God, help us to not use that grace as an excuse for what Your grace intends to produce in our lives. Help us to pursue that growth with fervency, with joy, and humility, and help us to recognize it in one another, Lord, that we would see it as we behold each other loving patiently, forgiving humbly, enduring patiently with joy. God, I'm thankful for the power, for Your power on display among us, how we can look around this room and see that endurance, that joy, that growth among one another, but would you increase it more and more? Fill us with the knowledge of yourself that we would grow in wisdom and live accordingly. Through Jesus we pray. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week traces the arc from what God has already accomplished in Christ (our complete qualification and deliverance) through the call to grow in wisdom and knowledge, culminating in the grateful obedience that flows from belonging to a new kingdom.

Monday Colossians 1:12-14

Paul reminds us that we are already qualified to share in the inheritance of the saints in light, already transferred into the kingdom of Christ, already redeemed and forgiven. These are not future promises dangling before us—they are present realities for everyone in Christ. We rest this week not in our own spiritual achievement, but in the finished work that has already positioned us as beloved children of the Father.

Tuesday Colossians 1:6

The gospel 'is bearing fruit and growing in the whole world'—not just at conversion, but continually. This growth is not a sign that Christ was insufficient, but proof that His grace keeps working in us. We see in this passage that the same gospel that justified us yesterday continues to sanctify us today and will shape us tomorrow, bearing fruit in abundance throughout our lives.

Wednesday Colossians 1:9-10

Paul prays that we be filled with knowledge of God's will 'in all spiritual wisdom and understanding,' then immediately adds the goal: 'so that you may walk in a manner worthy of the Lord.' Knowledge is never meant to inflate us; it is always meant to direct us—shaping how we speak, serve, forgive, and relate. This week, let the truths you know challenge you to obey in ways that please the God who revealed them.

Thursday Colossians 1:11

Paul prays that we be 'strengthened with all power, according to his glorious might, for all endurance and patience.' Endurance is not passive waiting—it is active faithfulness when circumstances don't change. The power Paul describes is the supernatural ability to remain gentle, grateful, and at peace while carrying genuine hardship. This is the gospel at work in real time, producing character through the long obedience of faithfulness.

Friday Genesis 12:3

Abraham was called to leave his land and walk with God 'so that all the families of the earth shall be blessed' through him. Similarly, we have been transferred into Christ's kingdom and called to walk in a manner worthy of the Lord—not to earn our place, but because we belong to a King whose blessing extends through us to others. Our growth in holiness, wisdom, and fruitfulness is not private spiritual real estate; it is how we steward our allegiance to the One who redeemed us and display His character to a world watching how His people actually live.
