Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how God's sovereign hand, working through suffering and scattering, accomplishes gospel expansion that shatters human dividing lines and creates compelling unity in Christ.

Monday Acts 8

The persecution that scattered the Jerusalem church—beginning with Stephen's martyrdom in Acts 8—was not a detour in God's plan but its very instrument. As we read of the church's flight northward, we see that what felt like calamity to the disciples became the mechanism by which the gospel reached Antioch and ultimately the nations. The scattering reveals that God does not merely work around our suffering; He works *through* it to accomplish what comfortable, settled faith might never attempt.

Tuesday John 16

Christ's promise in John 16 that believers will face tribulation in this world—yet that He has overcome the world—echoes through the Antioch church's emergence from persecution. The scattered believers did not escape their suffering by moving north; rather, they discovered Jesus present with them in it, working His purposes through their very displacement. This reframing transforms suffering from meaningless loss into the context where God's faithfulness becomes most visible and His grace most efficacious.

Wednesday Galatians 3:28

Paul's declaration that in Christ there is neither Jew nor Greek finds its lived expression in Acts 11 when Gentiles in Antioch receive the gospel and when that Gentile church later sends relief to Jewish believers in Jerusalem. The cross does not merely soften ethnic boundaries—it annihilates them, making tribal identity irrelevant compared to union with Christ. We too are called to recognize that every dividing line we erect—cultural, economic, social—dissolves before the cross's obliterating power.

Thursday Acts 10

Barnabas's appearance in Acts 11 as the apostolic encourager sent to Antioch reflects the Spirit's consistent work throughout Acts 10 and beyond, breaking down barriers and affirming unlikely believers. His name means 'son of encouragement,' and Acts shows him living into that calling—not critiquing the Gentile believers but recognizing God's grace at work and strengthening their faith. Our own names and roles in the body of Christ similarly call us to particular expressions of grace that build up the church and affirm God's surprising work among His people.

Friday Acts 9:31 (from Acts 8-10 series context)

The Jerusalem and Antioch churches—separated by ethnicity, geography, and initial suspicion—became one fellowship when they discovered their common treasure in Christ. This unity was not manufactured by organizational effort but flowed from the gospel's power to make us one body, one bride, one family in a way that transcends what the world calls identity. As we face our own divisions and resist the notes God calls us to move toward, we are invited to trust that the unity He promises in Christ is far more precious than the tribal comfort we might sacrifice to find it.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Acts 11:19-21, the believers scattered by persecution end up preaching Christ to Gentiles in Antioch—a group they would never have intentionally sought out. What does this passage suggest about how God uses circumstances we would call 'bad' to accomplish His purposes?
    Acts 11:19-21
    → Can you think of a time when something difficult in your own life unexpectedly opened a door or changed your perspective in a way you didn't see coming?
  2. The sermon emphasizes that God's sovereignty doesn't minimize real suffering but reframes it by His accompanying presence. How does that distinction—sovereignty that includes presence—change the way you might pray through hardship?
  3. Barnabas arrives in Antioch and encourages the new believers rather than critiquing them for breaking Jewish tradition by accepting Gentiles. What does his response reveal about what it looks like to recognize God's grace at work in others, even when it surprises us?
    Acts 11:22-23
    → Where in your own life do you find it hardest to recognize God's grace working outside the boundaries you expected?
  4. According to the sermon, the cross has obliterated all human dividing lines—ethnic, social, cultural. Yet we live in a world deeply fractured by those very divisions. What does it mean for us as a church to live as though the gospel's power to unite is actually true?
    Galatians 3:28
  5. The passage culminates with the Gentile church sending famine relief to the Jewish believers who had initially excluded them (Acts 11:29-30). What does this act of generosity reveal about how the gospel transforms not just belief but our willingness to serve those who've hurt us?
    Acts 11:29-30
    → Is there a relationship in your life where the gospel is calling you to move toward someone rather than away, the way this Antioch church moved toward Jerusalem?
  6. The sermon describes suffering and scattering as God moving us from comfortable notes to new chords He's composing. Rather than praying for circumstances to change, what might it look like to ask God whether He's calling you to move to a different note this week—even if it feels dissonant?
