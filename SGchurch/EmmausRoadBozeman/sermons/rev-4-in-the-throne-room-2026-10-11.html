We entered the throne room of heaven with the apostle John, and we see this glimpse of God's overwhelming majesty. So the heavenly pattern of worship should be the pattern on which we base our earthly worship. And I'm— when I talk about that, I'm talking about this corporate worship setting. This worship, our worship as a church, is meant every Sunday It's meant to be a foretaste of that heavenly worship. So the heavenly worship is the worship by which we should assess our worship here. Don't you agree? That makes sense, right? So let's ask, how are we doing? Or it's probably better to ask, first of all, what is the pattern of worship that's most pleasing to God? 35 · Gamache's first application: worship that pleases God is thoroughly God-centered

So what worship is most pleasing to God? First, it's worship that is thoroughly God-centered. It centers on him, right? He's the center of our worship like his throne is the center of the universe. Now that seems obvious. But we have to say it. Worship is about God. Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God Almighty. And worship is to God. Worthy are You, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power. Worship is preoccupied with God. It's focused on who He is and what He's done. Worship is, like I said, the greatest end for which we were created. It is the great end. For which we were all made, which means that worship is the highest moral act in the universe. And you think about that on your way to church on Sunday. I'm about to gather with the saints, with brothers and sisters in Christ, and I am going to perform with them the highest moral act in the universe. And God is the audience. God is the one we're concerned with pleasing in worship.

36 · Gamache addresses a potential objection: isn't it egotistical for God to demand worship? He reframes the issue: God creating us for worship is supremely loving because worship focuses us on what brings ultimate joy — God Himself

Now, that's not to say that worship doesn't affect us. Being preoccupied with God, you better believe that's going to affect us. Creating us for worship is the most loving thing God can do. It's the most loving thing He could do because in the act of worship, we focus our attention on that which brings us the most joy and satisfaction, which is God Himself. But even so, we're not the audience. God is.

37 · Gamache applies the God-centered worship principle by critiquing the common post-service question: 'How did you like worship?' He reframes the question: 'Was God pleased with our worship?' This shift exposes self-centered worship patterns and calls the congregation to assess their worship by God's pleasure, not their own preferences

Now, I'm belaboring this point because I think we can subtly shift the focus. The main question after a Sunday morning is not, how did you like worship this morning? Which is really to ask— might be to ask, did it meet your preferences? Did you like the songs we sang? Did you like the style we sang them in? That just might betray a worship pattern preoccupied with self. The main question after a Sunday morning is, was God pleased with our worship this morning? And God is pleased by worship that is thoroughly centered on him, who he is, and what he's done. So how are we doing?

God is pleased with worship that's thoroughly God-centered And he's pleased by worship that is expressively passionate. God is pleased when we respond rightly to the one on whom we are centered. And we reflect the heavenly pattern of worship here on earth when we respond to who he is and what he's done with the expressive passion that he deserves. 39 · Gamache critiques spectator-style worship where the congregation watches a performance

Now, again, we are not the audience in worship. They— there is a lot of that in churches out there, and I'm talking outside Sovereign Grace family of churches. But you know what? God will not stand for that for long. We don't watch the show. Worship is not a spectator sport. God is the audience. We, the church, are the performers. And our emotions, our affections, our bodies, our singing, our clapping, our voices, our bowed knees, our dancing feet, our amens, right? Those are our instruments. We give all that we are in response to all that he is. And so I ask again, how are we doing? How does our earthly worship follow the heavenly pattern?

40 · Gamache's third application: worship that pleases God is ultimately corporate

God is pleased by worship that is preoccupied with him, that's passionately expressive, and finally, ultimately corporate. Now, I love private worship. We should be worshiping God over His Word and in prayer every day. But it's undeniable that worship in the Bible is a congregational activity. John was worshiping apparently alone. I mean, the guy's in exile. It's the Lord's Day, chapter 1, verse 10, and all this happened, right? He's alone. Private worship, but he was brought into the congregational worship of heaven.

41 · Gamache critiques the individualistic approach to worship ('my tank gets filled,' 'the mountains are my temple') and argues that God intends private worship to enhance corporate worship, not replace it

Now, this by no means undermines or devalues our private worship, but it does highlight the importance of what we do when we gather together as God's church. We participate in the heavenly worship together. I mean, we can tend to think so individualistically, right? Sunday worship, well, I'm— get my tank filled and that'll help me with my private worship through the week. And it certainly is that, but we can take that too far sometimes and think, well, you know, I don't really need to gather with the church today. I can stay home, worship God myself. I imagine in Bozeman it's a temptation to say, hey, the mountains are my temple. I'm just going to go out and worship God in the mountain. That's where I meet God. And we can tend to think that way. And you can, you can meet God in the mountains, but I'm contending that's not ultimately what God intends. He intends that our private worship enhance our experience of corporate worship so that it builds and strengthens the church. I mean, without the church, we're a detached body part, and that's weird. Right? Without the church, we're just an orphan, and that's sad. Without the church, we're just a single brick, and that's useless. That's not what God intends. He intends worship to be ultimately corporate, and we ultimately benefit because the best way to keep ourselves from false worship, from idolatry, is to gather with fellow believers to praise our indescribably glorious, inherently kingly, and infinitely holy God.

42 · Gamache synthesizes the three marks of worship that pleases God: God-centered, expressively passionate, and ultimately corporate

That's the worship that pleases God— worship thoroughly centered on him, worship that's expressively passionate, and worship that's ultimately corporate.

Now, almost done, but I have to admit it feels like— and I hope it does to you too— like something's missing. In chapter 4, we have God the Father. The throne. We have the Holy Spirit burning in his sevenfold fullness before the throne. But what about Jesus?

Well, remember, this is just part 1 of the scene, and my, oh my, do we see Jesus in chapter 5. But he's already here in chapter 4. Jesus— or John enters the open door to the throne room of heaven and by the summons of Jesus.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we step into the throne room alongside John, letting the vision of God's glory address every church problem, reshape our perception of earthly trials, and call us to the worship for which we were made.

Monday Revelation 2-3

John's seven letters to the churches catalog the real struggles of God's people: lost love, persecution, compromise, apathy, and spiritual death. The placement of the throne room vision immediately after these letters is not accident—it is divine strategy. When we behold the all-glorious God seated in sovereign majesty, every church problem finds its solution in renewed worship and gospel-centered perspective.

Tuesday Isaiah 6

Isaiah's vision of the Lord seated on a throne, high and lifted up, undoes his self-perception in an instant: 'Woe is me! I am ruined!' (Isaiah 6:5). The same transformative power operates in us when we truly behold God's indescribable glory—our earthly trials, fears, and discouragements are recontextualized in light of His supreme majesty. We see that we were made for something infinitely greater than our circumstances.

Wednesday Daniel 7

Daniel's vision reveals that though earthly kingdoms rise and fall—the lion, the bear, the leopard, the dreadful beast—there is One whose dominion is eternal and whose kingdom will never be destroyed (Daniel 7:14). In Revelation 4, we too are granted access to that same throne room, reminding us that God's sovereign rule over all history, all nations, and all circumstances is absolute. Nothing escapes His notice; nothing lies beyond His control.

Thursday Revelation 5

In Revelation 5, the vision expands: the Lamb who was slain sits with the One on the throne, and creation itself erupts in worship—'every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea' singing the new song (Revelation 5:13). This is not a temporary emotional response but the eternal vocation of heaven itself. We glimpse here the purpose for which we were made: to join that incessant, cosmic chorus of praise to the God who sits on the throne.

Friday Romans 8

Paul assures us that nothing—neither tribulation nor distress nor persecution nor famine nor nakedness nor danger nor sword—can separate us from the love of Christ (Romans 8:35-39). This confidence, born from knowing that the sovereign God reigns from the throne, frees us to worship with passion and abandon, not in isolation but alongside the whole church. Our worship becomes the natural overflow of a people who know they are loved by the One who controls all things, and we express that love together as His ransomed community.

In Revelation 4, John is invited into the throne room of heaven immediately after witnessing the risen Christ's letters to seven churches facing real problems — lost love, persecution, compromise, apathy. What do you notice about the placement of this vision? Why might God show John the throne room precisely at this moment in the narrative?
    Revelation 2-3; Revelation 4:1
    → How does beholding the glory and sovereignty of God in the throne room speak to a church struggling with one of those specific problems?
  2. Read Revelation 4:2-3 and 4:8. What specific attributes or qualities of God does John emphasize through his descriptions of the throne room — the One seated, the appearance, the endless declarations of the living creatures?
    Revelation 4:2-3, 4:8
  3. The sermon emphasizes that the throne of God is 'the control room of everything that exists.' When you face circumstances that feel chaotic, out of control, or unjust in your own life or church, how does the reality of God's sovereign throne in heaven reshape what you believe about those circumstances?
    Romans 8
    → What would it mean practically to live as though you actually believed that the invisible heavenly throne controls and determines the earthly visible realm?
  4. Revelation 4:11 declares that all things were created 'for your pleasure,' speaking to God as the audience and recipient of worship. How does centering worship on God's pleasure — rather than on what makes us feel good or fulfilled — change the nature of what we offer in corporate worship?
    Revelation 4:11
  5. The sermon claims that worship pleasing to God is 'thoroughly God-centered, expressively passionate, and ultimately corporate.' When you think about your own participation in worship — at church or in private — which of these three elements feels most natural to you, and which one challenges you most?
    Revelation 4:8-11
    → What would it look like to grow in the area where you feel least naturally inclined?
