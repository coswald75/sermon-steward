  1. personal story · unit #22 — Montegue uses a humorous personal story about emotional support animals on flights to illustrate the concept of 'dwelling richly.' We don't have to go there or anything. She just paid it off out of her generosity. Good old Aunt Bertha paid off your mortgage unexpectedly. You get that note in the mail. Wow. I mean, just imagine that, right? You get that kind of news. What would the conversation look like at the dinner table tomorrow evening? Well, kids, how was your day at school? Good. Did you get your homework done? Okay, let's get at that. Don't forget to pick up some eggs tomorrow on the way home from the office because we need them for tomorrow. My coworker is still doing that really weird clicking thing with his tongue. Finish your Brussels sprouts. That would not be the conversation at the dinner table, would it? No, the conversation— if that good news landed in the mail, the conversation of being mortgage-free would dwell in your family richly, right? You would explain it to your children, what this meant for your family. You would teach them about it. You would just— there's so much glorious news here. You would be singing. You would be composing epic hymns of praise to Aunt Bertha, right? You would be calling her and inviting her into your home, figuring out how do we, how do we just have her with us and thank her. How do we celebrate this? You would rename each of your daughters Bertha to honor the one who lavished such grace upon you. You would not be talking about Brussels sprouts and your coworker's tongue. The good news from Aunt Bertha would be dwelling in you richly. It's something to celebrate.

25 · Montegue applies the illustration to the gospel: the word of Christ—Christ's victory and salvation—is even better news than a paid-off mortgage, and therefore should naturally dwell richly in the church

And that's exactly what Paul is calling the church to do here. Let the word of Christ dwell in you, plural, richly. There's something glorious, even more glorious, believe it or not, for those of you that just bought a home, even more glorious than your mortgage being paid off. Christ has won and he has taken his throne and he reigns over all and he has saved his people from their sins. Let that word dwell in you. Dwell among you richly. Do you see how this is a pleasurable command? It's like, oh, we have to do that, we have to celebrate. No, I'm like, this is— there's nothing better. Let it dwell among you richly. There's not a lot of things you have to do other than just open your eyes to the glory of what Christ has done and just let it move in. Let it move in.

26 · Montegue applies the commands to specific contexts: the home, the car, individual life

So let me ask you, does your home let the peace of Christ reign? Does your home let the Word of Christ dwell in you richly? When you drive a long drive by yourself, is the Word of Christ dwelling in you individually richly? Is the peace of Christ reigning in your heart? Or do you see that person driving the way that you would prefer them not to drive, and other things begin to rule and reign in your hearts? Let the peace of Christ rule. Let the Word of Christ dwell in you.

27 · Montegue acknowledges the fuller ecclesiological application in verse 16 but declines to develop it, keeping focus on the main thread

And there's so much more you could talk about because Paul then talks about the life of the church: teaching and admonishing one another in all wisdom, singing psalms and hymns and spiritual songs. We won't get into that. Just that's what's supposed to dominate the church, the Word of Christ being celebrated and taught and counseled with, dwelling among us richly.

28 · Montegue identifies the second thanksgiving tagline and moves to the third command

And then Paul does it again. He adds that little tagline, with thankfulness in your hearts to God. First command had that tagline, be thankful, and now Paul makes sure to include thankfulness again. And once again, it's not, it's not just let your speech be thankful, it's let it dwell in your hearts, in the core of who you are. And we'll come back to that one theme as we finish here. But first, the final pleasurable command in verse 17, step 3: do everything in the Lord, in the name of the Lord Jesus. In Colossae, if someone wanted a successful harvest or a healthy delivery of a baby or a safe journey, they would invoke the name of various pagan gods, maybe offer a sacrifice at the temple. Jews, and likely some Christians in the church, would invoke the name of an angel sometimes in order to kind of try to curry favor. Paul wrestles with that, it seems, a little bit in this book, actually. And Paul, in a way, is saying, forget about all that rot. All you need here is Jesus. Do everything, word or deed, in his name. And once again, we understand the good news of the Pax Christiana, the peace of Christ. This is a joy and not a burden. It's definitely not a formulaic way or a mantra to kind of get God to do things for you. It's not that. It's to do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus who rules over all and who has saved you. But wait, there's one more thing. Paul says it again, giving thanks to God the Father through Jesus. 3 pleasurable commands: let Christ's peace reign in your hearts, let Christ's word dwell in you richly, and then the big comprehensive one, do everything in the name of the Lord Jesus. Paul subtly then attaches a critical piece to each of these commands, and it's a theme that he's been weaving throughout the book: be thankful. Be thankful.

29 · Montegue introduces the Thomas Watson quotation and pivots from its severity to the positive invitation Paul offers: gratitude produces a peaceful, rich life under Jesus' reign

If you scan the book of Colossians, you'll find that word just pop up over and over again. Here it pops up, you know, attached to all 3 commands. Puritan Thomas Watson once wrote about thankfulness, and he said this: an unthankful person is a monster in nature, a paradox in Christianity. The Puritans have a way of saying things. He says, he is the scorn of heaven and the plague of the earth. That's what an ungrateful person is. An ungrateful man never does well except in one thing, that is when he dies. Wow. Okay. Somebody might need a nap there a little bit, but there's something, maybe a positive side to what Watson is saying in a rather negative way. Because I think what Paul's saying here is it's an invitation to something beautiful. Gratitude results in a peaceful, rich life joyfully lived under Jesus' reign. And I know, I know I said that this wasn't just about you as an individual, but let me start there before we get to the final application. If you personally desire a truly rich life of inner peace— and by rich, I don't mean financially necessarily— if you desire a truly rich life of inner peace, it will only come when you learn to live with perpetual gratitude for what Jesus has accomplished. Learn to live with that. Let it move in. To your heart.

30 · Montegue cross-references Philippians 4:6-7 to reinforce the connection between thanksgiving and peace, then preempts the objection that some have little to be thankful for

Paul wrote a similar thing to the Philippian church. He said, don't be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus. How often do we attempt to eliminate anxiety by prayer and then forget the little phrase with thanksgiving. Sometimes the answer to our anxiety is to be thankful for what Christ has done and the so many other little blessings. There's a necessity to practicing gratitude if you want to richly experience God's grace. So be thankful to God the Father through the Lord Jesus Christ, and the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds in in Christ. And someone might be thinking, well, you don't know my life. It's rough right now. I don't, I don't have much to be thankful for. Well, we Midwestern Christians say to that, hogwash. Hogwash. And let me just list, as I conclude here, list from the book of Colossians what you have to be thankful for if you're in Christ. If you're in Christ, you have been delivered from the domain of darkness and transferred to the kingdom of Jesus. And we could just stop there and say, all right, well, that's good enough. But it goes on. And if you're in Christ, you have been redeemed and your sins are forgiven. If you're in Christ, you have been reconciled to God and are right now being presented holy and blameless to God the Father by Jesus. If you're in Christ, you died with Christ, you were buried with Christ, you rose with Christ, and you are alive with Christ who is victoriously seated at the right hand of God the Father. If you're in Christ, your debt of sin has been canceled and nailed to the cross. If you're in Christ, your enemies have been disarmed and put to shame by the triumph of your Lord and Savior. If you're in Christ, you are God's chosen, holy, and beloved child. If you're in Christ, when he returns in glory to rule and reign forever, you will be with him in pure eternal joy. If you're in Christ, you can let his peace reign in your heart If you're in Christ, you can let His Word dwell in you richly. If you're in Christ, doing everything in His name is a pleasurable command, a glorious reality. It is a delightful state of being. If you're in Christ, you have a new wardrobe. The filthy rags can be burnt and the wonderful love of Jesus can be worn. If you're in Christ Jesus, God the Son himself, through whom and for whom all things were created, he is your life. If you are in Christ, you have much to be thankful for.

31 · Montegue concludes by restating the thesis and moving from individual to corporate application

Gratitude results in a peaceful, rich life lived under Jesus' reign, and gratitude results in a peaceful rich church lived under Jesus' reign. May we all know and live out this peace so that a tumultuous world sees the glory and beauty of Jesus' reign and his pleasurable commands. Let the peace of Christ reign. Let the word of Christ dwell in you. Do everything in Jesus' name. Be thankful. We have much to be thankful for.

32 · Montegue closes with a pastoral prayer asking God to purge lesser things from the congregation's hearts and to open their eyes to the eternal glory of Christ, reinforcing the sermon's call to let Christ's peace and word dwell richly and to be a grateful people

Let's pray. Father, so often our eyes avert from the glory of who Jesus is and what Jesus has done and dwell on lesser things and let those lesser things move into our hearts and dominate who we are. Would you purge that from us, Lord? Would you open our eyes to the temporariness of so much of everything else and the glory, the eternal glory of being found in Christ? Lord, help us to be a peaceful, grateful people. And it's in Christ's name, for which we have much to be grateful for, in which we seek to do everything. It's in his name that we pray. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how Christ's peace, word, and lordship—each anchored in gratitude—reshape both individual Christian life and the corporate witness of the church.

Monday Colossians 1:13-14

Paul opens Colossians by reminding us that we have been rescued from darkness and transferred into Christ's kingdom—we possess redemption and forgiveness through His blood. This is the gospel fact that should compel perpetual gratitude in us; we are not our own, but belong to the One who ransomed us. When thanksgiving becomes our distinguishing mark, it testifies that we have truly grasped the magnitude of what Christ has done.

Tuesday John 14:27

Christ offers us a peace that the world cannot give and cannot take away—a peace radically different from political or circumstantial tranquility. This peace flows directly from His finished work at Calvary, where He accomplished our redemption and reconciliation with the Father. In a world of turmoil and competing loyalties, we are invited to rest in the objective, eternal reality of Christ's peace, which no earthly power can diminish.

Wednesday Colossians 1:20

Through Christ's blood shed on the cross, God has reconciled all things to Himself—peace has been made, not promised or hoped for, but accomplished. This cosmic peace is not dependent on our circumstances, our politics, or our feelings; it is rooted in the objective reality of Christ's substitutionary work. When we let this peace rule in our hearts and churches, we are submitting to what is already true, already won, already secured by our King.

Thursday Philippians 4:6-7

Paul teaches us that anxiety is answered not by changing our circumstances but by filling our minds with gratitude and the word of Christ; as His word dwells richly in us, the peace of God guards our hearts and minds. This is the inverse of the world's formula—we do not find peace by securing control, but by letting Christ's word and peace saturate our thoughts and decisions. When the gospel dwells richly among us, it naturally displaces the lesser anxieties and frustrations that would otherwise dominate our inner lives.

Friday Colossians 2:14-15

Christ has disarmed the powers and authorities, triumphing over them by the cross—He has freed us from the accusation and condemnation that once enslaved us. This victory is the foundation for a grateful, joyful life lived in full allegiance to His reign; we are no longer captive to fear, shame, or the tyranny of lesser rulers. As we corporately embrace this reality through thanksgiving and submission to Christ's lordship, we become a living witness to the world that true peace, true richness, and true freedom flow from Jesus alone.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Colossians 3:15-17, Paul issues three commands, each followed by a call to gratitude. What does the structure of these commands—pairing each with thanksgiving—suggest about how Paul understands the relationship between obedience and joy?
    Colossians 3:15-17
    → Can you think of a specific command from Scripture that you've experienced as burdensome rather than joyful? What might change if you saw gratitude as the gateway to experiencing that command as pleasurable?
  2. Paul tells us to let 'the peace of Christ' rule in our hearts as arbiters in disputes (3:15). How does understanding that Christ's peace is an objective reality secured at Calvary—rather than something we must manufacture through negotiation or compromise—reshape how we approach conflict in our church community?
    Colossians 3:15; Colossians 1:20
  3. The sermon contrasts Christ's peace with the Pax Romana—earthly political power offering false security. What competing 'peaces' are we tempted to trust in our current moment, and how does allegiance to Christ's kingdom challenge those temptations?
    Colossians 3:15; Colossians 1:13
    → Where do you find yourself most easily forgetting that your ultimate allegiance belongs to Christ rather than to earthly powers or circumstances?
  4. Paul commands us to let 'the word of Christ dwell in you richly' (3:16), and the sermon suggests the gospel is 'even better news than having your mortgage paid off.' What does it mean practically for Christ's word to dwell *richly* in your home, your car, your individual quiet moments—rather than being crowded out by lesser anxieties?
    Colossians 3:16
  5. The sermon identifies thanksgiving as 'a distinguishing mark of a Christian and of the Christian church.' Why would gratitude be so countercultural and remarkable as a corporate witness? What does a perpetually grateful church communicate about Jesus' reign that a comfortable or entitled church cannot?
    → When you look at our church community, where do you see genuine thanksgiving rising naturally from the gospel, and where might we be settling for mere compliance instead?
  6. In Colossians 2:14-15, Christ disarmed the powers at the cross; in 3:15-17, we're called to live in the reality of that victory through peace, word, and gratitude. How does understanding that Christ has *already* secured our peace and victory free us from the burden of fighting for control in our own lives and in our church?
    Colossians 2:14-15; Colossians 3:15-17
    → What would change in your daily decisions this week if you truly rested in the fact that Christ's reign is already accomplished rather than something you must earn or defend?
