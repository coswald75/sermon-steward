Theological claims· 14
  1. Despite Saul's absolute hostility to Christ and total lack of spiritual seeking, God had sovereignly determined a role for him in the kingdom. unit #5
  2. Though unique in its external circumstances, Saul's conversion reveals the pattern of all Christian conversion: Christ sovereignly seeks, confronts, transforms, and commissions those the Father has given him, regardless of their past condition. unit #6
  3. The first action in every conversion is Christ's confrontation: God sovereignly interrupts the sinner's trajectory toward destruction and forces them to reckon with who he is. unit #21
  4. A passive, non-confrontational model of salvation reduces Christ to a lottery ticket and eternal life to luck, contradicting Scripture's testimony of God's active pursuit of the lost. unit #22
  5. Scripture presents a proactive God who seeks the lost and confronts them with his presence, not a passive deity waiting to be discovered—making him trustworthy and worthy of love. unit #23
  6. Christ's confrontation of Saul was utterly effective and one-sided—the proud persecutor was instantly overpowered, humbled, and reduced to submission before the risen Lord. unit #24
  7. Christ's second action in salvation is conviction, understood not as inner motivation but as forensic guilt—the sinner's legal standing of guilt before God's perfect righteousness. unit #25
  8. Christ's conviction brought Saul to the realization that all his religious efforts were in vain, that he was a guilty transgressor, and that his only hope for deliverance was found in Christ himself. unit #27
  9. Christ's confrontation and conviction of Saul led to his heart being totally transformed and covered by Christ's perfect righteousness—a pattern true for every regenerate believer. unit #29
  10. Christ's third saving action is consecration: the transformation of the dead heart into a heart of flesh alive to God, whereby believers are permanently designated as belonging to Christ. unit #30
  11. Christ's consecration includes purposeful assignment—believers are saved not only from something but to something: to be Christ's witnesses and vessels for honorable use in bringing others to saving knowledge of him. unit #31
  12. Though Western Christians lack physical persecution, the cultural rejection and intellectual mockery of the gospel constitutes real suffering that makes faithful witness costly and difficult. unit #39
  13. Christ's fourth saving action is commission: he sends his followers out to carry the message of salvation to those he is actively pursuing, using saved people as his instruments in the salvation of others. unit #41
  14. Though unique in external circumstances, the pattern of Saul's conversion is universal—every believer has been confronted, convicted, consecrated, and commissioned by the same Christ through the same miraculous grace. unit #45

18 · Summarizes Saul's Damascus mission and emphasizes that his later self-description as a persecutor understates rather than overstates the murderous intensity of his pre-conversion campaign against Christians

20 · Identifies the voice addressing Saul as the resurrected Christ, establishing that this is a direct, personal encounter between Jesus and his chief persecutor

21 · Introduces the sermon's structural framework—four actions Christ takes in every conversion—and defines the first: confrontation, where God sovereignly interrupts the sinner's path and forces a reckoning with divine reality

And this is going to mark the first of the 4 actions that we're going to see Jesus take when he saves an unbeliever. In every instance of salvation, at the beginning of the process that results in a heart that's transformed by God, there must first take place a confrontation. A confrontation between the Almighty One and the rebellious creature, between the author of life and a person who is dead in their trespasses. God brings the unrepentant sinner face-to-face with himself. He interrupts them on their way to destruction. He disrupts their pattern of following after a lost world, and he forces them to reckon with who he is.

22 · Refutes a passive model of salvation by analogy, arguing that a non-confrontational Christ would reduce salvation to luck—a lottery ticket stumbled upon by chance—contradicting Scripture's portrayal of God's active pursuit of sinners

Jesus is not a passive Savior. Jesus is not distant or far off from people when they encounter him. Jesus does not sit idly by waiting for individuals to stumble upon him by some chance discovery. For what kind of a Savior would that make him to be? If Christ was non-confrontational in the way he goes about saving us, he might as well be viewed as a winning lottery ticket that a lucky few found on the metaphorical sidewalk. The eternal life that he promises is only going to be experienced by those who have great luck. But that's not the Savior of Scripture, and by his own teaching, Jesus tells us we don't see God taking a backseat.

23 · Contrasts two views of God: the unbiblical passive deity who 'plays cat and mouse' versus the scriptural God who proactively seeks and confronts the lost, placing himself before sinners and inviting them to his loving-kindness

We do not see him sitting on his hands waiting for people to just unearth this promise he puts forward of justification and sanctification and reconciliation to God, found by grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone. Who would really and should really trust a God like that? That's a God who plays cat and mouse, and that's a difficult God to love. No, Scripture here is telling us that God acts in the exact opposite manner. Here on these pages, we're reading about a God who is proactive. Seeking the lost, a God who willingly places himself before us and he bids us to come toward his loving-kindness. And Jesus, in the perfect timing of his infinite wisdom, confronts the one who is blindly stumbling down and around in the darkness, unable to find the way on their own.

24 · Demonstrates the effectiveness of Christ's confrontation in Saul's case: the proud persecutor is instantly overpowered and humbled, his resistance broken, his posture transformed from crusader to conquered bondservant

Jesus, who is building his kingdom by transforming the hearts of his people, brings about the salvation of his people first by confronting his people. And for Saul on the Damascus Road, this confrontation is one-sided. For as impressive as a persecutor of the church he had been, he is completely overpowered in this moment. From the dirt, as Saul looks up to see who is speaking to him, he asks, who are you, Lord? And see how rapidly Saul's demeanor becomes one of submission and of servitude. Notice the change that's taking place. The Crusader has just become the conquered. The eminently proud persecutor of the church is immediately humbled and made low like a bondservant in the presence of Christ. Christ's purpose in meeting Saul on the Damascus Road is instantly realized, and the result is Saul's submission before him.

25 · Defines the second action in Christ's saving work—conviction—disambiguating between legal guilt and inner motivation, and establishing that Christ's conviction is forensic: declaring the sinner's guilty standing before God's perfect righteousness

So the first action that Christ performs when he saves us is to confront. The second action that he takes is conviction. Now we use this word conviction in many ways and oftentimes we use it when we're talking in legal context. Conviction is used to convey that there's been someone that's broken the law, they've been found guilty by a jury of the crimes they've been accused of. Other times though, we can speak of conviction as being an inner belief that's motivating us to take certain actions or do certain things. And that is a really— that's a very appropriate use of the word, but that can still be subjective and look different individually at times. So here in Acts 9, when I say we are beholding the Christ who convicts, I mean the former definition. The conviction that Christ brings to bear upon unbelievers is the guilty standing that they have before him.

26 · Applies the doctrine of conviction to Saul's specific case, showing how his encounter with Christ's perfect righteousness exposed the inadequacy of his lifelong pursuit of lawkeeping—his self-assessed blamelessness crumbled when measured against Christ

Placed before him who is perfect righteousness, here Saul's unrighteousness is laid out in front of him for him to deal with. Think of what this realization would have meant for Saul in his life, the Pharisee, the Hebrew of Hebrews. His life had been devoted to studying the law, meditating on the writings of Moses and the prophets, and making every effort to keep the whole law, obeying every single one of those 613 commandments, memorizing Scripture in order to never forget it, spending hours every day around mature and faithful lawkeepers like Gamaliel and other leading teachers. All those efforts, all those years of devotion, were intended to bring about his righteousness. Here again, we can jump to Philippians 3, and we can hear him describe himself as to righteousness under the law, blameless. But that's not the position he finds himself in as he looks up into the beautiful face of Jesus. Instead, what he finds is he does not measure up to the standard of Christ's righteousness. Rather, he's made aware very quickly of how short of the glory of God he's fallen. Fallen.

27 · Describes the internal effect of Christ's conviction in Saul's heart: total awareness of his guilt, the futility of his self-righteousness, and his complete dependence on Christ alone for deliverance

The work of Christ in Saul's heart in this instant brought conviction to weigh heavily upon the Pharisee, that although he had given his life to pursuing the righteousness of God, all those efforts had actually been in vain. Now kneeling before the resurrected Christ, Saul is keenly aware that he has missed the mark and that he is in fact a guilty transgressor of righteousness. There's nothing found in him, nothing he can offer to the Lord that would be of merit, nothing in and of himself that can fix what he now is aware is broken within him. Nothing except for the one who stood before him now. Saul's only hope for deliverance from this crushing conviction was found in the beautiful, righteous person of Jesus.

28 · Applies Saul's experience of conviction to the congregation, asserting that every believer has experienced the same realization of lost condition and sinful corruption, and that unbelievers remain in that state presently

And we must understand that for us today, without that conviction of sin and without knowing what our station in life is prior to being covered by the blood of Jesus, We identify with Saul in that moment right there. We also, we likewise, were hopelessly lost even if we convinced ourselves it was all figured out. Because sin has corrupted the desires of our hearts. Our hearts are desperately sick because of the effect of sin's curse on creation. And if you today are a true follower of Jesus, then this is who you used to be. If you are not a believer and if you are not a Christian, this is who you are right now. The power of sin has a death grip on your life and forces you to naturally seek after everything else but the things of God.

29 · Traces the trajectory from conviction to transformation in Saul's experience and every believer's, showing that Christ's confrontation and conviction lead to covering by his righteousness and heart regeneration that results in repentance

For everyone who has had their heart regenerated and is now seeking after the things of God, this has been what you've experienced. Christ has confronted you. Christ convicted you. And he's covered you with his perfect righteousness. By the blood that he shed on the cross, your sins have been washed away. You have been made white as snow, and now you enjoy communion and relationship with God because of what Christ accomplished. And Saul, in this moment of deep, difficult, and demanding conviction, has his heart totally transformed. The purpose that Christ pursues in the life of Saul is not just conviction. It is conviction that leads to repentance.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the fourfold pattern of Christ's saving work revealed in Saul's conversion: confrontation, conviction, consecration, and commission—the same grace operative in every believer's salvation.

Monday Acts 8:1-3

Saul's frenzied persecution of the church—dragging believers to prison, consenting to Stephen's death, breathing threats against disciples—reveals a man consumed with religious zeal against Christ. Yet this very passage shows us that God's sovereign plan for salvation is not contingent on our seeking or worthiness; Christ actively pursues even the most hostile and lost. Our conversion, like Saul's, flows entirely from God's predetermined grace, not from our spiritual competence or willingness to be found.

Tuesday Matthew 14:22-33

When the disciples saw Jesus walking on the water, their first response was terror—a confrontation with transcendent power that shattered their assumptions and demanded recognition of his lordship. Like that moment, Saul's encounter with the risen Christ on the Damascus Road was an irruption of divine glory that stopped him mid-stride and forced absolute reckoning with who Jesus truly is. We too have experienced this confrontation, whether sudden or gradual—the moment Christ's presence became undeniable and we could no longer ignore his claim on our lives.

Wednesday 1 Timothy 1:12-17

Paul himself reflects on his conversion with stunning clarity: he was a blasphemer, a persecutor, acting in ignorance and unbelief—yet he received mercy because Christ revealed the depth of his guilt and the sufficiency of grace. This passage shows us that conviction is not self-generated remorse but the forensic reality of standing guilty before God's perfect righteousness, combined with the Spirit's witness that only Christ's righteousness can cover that guilt. When we grasp, as Paul did, that our best religious works cannot save us and that we are hopelessly lost without Christ's substitutionary death, we experience the transformative power of true conviction.

Thursday Philippians 3

Paul's testimony in Philippians 3 shows the radical reversal wrought by consecration: everything he once counted as gain—his Pharisaic credentials, his zeal, his moral achievement—he now counts as loss for the surpassing worth of knowing Christ. Consecration means that Christ has claimed us wholly, covering our guilty hearts with his perfect righteousness and remaking us into vessels devoted entirely to his glory and purposes. We are not merely forgiven; we are permanently designated as belonging to Christ, our old identities crucified and new ones born in him.

Friday Acts 1:8

Jesus promised his disciples power to be his witnesses to the ends of the earth—a commission that Saul, transformed into Paul, would fulfill with extraordinary faithfulness despite suffering and rejection. Every one of us who has been confronted, convicted, and consecrated by Christ receives this same commission: we are sent as his witnesses, entrusted with the gospel message to carry to those he is drawing. Our salvation is not a private transaction but a corporate calling—we have been saved not only for ourselves but to be Christ's instruments of grace in the salvation of others.
