  1. analogy · unit #14 — This unit uses Tozer's piano illustration to demonstrate how church unity works through shared orientation toward Christ rather than interpersonal affinity.   2. hypothetical · unit #24 — This unit creates imaginative sympathy for Saul's isolation and the church's fear through a hypothetical scenario. It makes the difficulty of Saul's incorporation visceral by asking the congregation to imagine their greatest enemy walking into the room.

Theological claims· 7
  1. Greater-to-lesser reasoning is a biblical form of argument where God's provision for greater needs guarantees his provision for lesser needs. unit #2
  2. If God can transform the church's greatest enemy into one of its greatest participants, he can effectively incorporate any believer into his church — this is God's universal plan for all he saves. unit #3
  3. Being Christ-centered begins with seeing Christ in all of Scripture, and without this spiritual sight, even thorough biblical knowledge can produce opposition to Christ rather than faith in him. unit #11
  4. Genuine conversion and effective incorporation into the church requires supernatural revelation by the Holy Spirit opening eyes to see God's glory in Christ — a work that cannot be accomplished through human teaching alone. unit #13
  5. Paul's memory of being Christ's enemy and experiencing conquering grace shaped his approach to his new enemies — he saw them as potential recipients of the same transforming grace he had received. unit #20
  6. The church's ultimate success depended on Saul's escape from Damascus, which depended on the faithfulness of rope-holding disciples — demonstrating how God uses small acts of service for monumental purposes. unit #29
  7. The text's emphasis on Ananias, the rope holders, and Barnabas rather than Saul himself reveals that God builds his church through the faithful service of members who incorporate converts into the body. unit #32

One example would be Matthew chapter 6, verse 25. Jesus says, therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will put on, even Is not life more than food and the body more than clothing? Romans 8:32, another example: He who did not spare his own Son but gave him up for us all, how will he not also with him graciously give us all things? In other words, I have made the greatest sacrifice to meet your greatest need. Do you think now that I will not provide for your lesser needs? The logic is if the greater is true, the lesser will be true. If you can believe the greater, if God would do that for us, if he would redeem us, save us, do you not think he would take care of you the basic needs.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how God's transformation of Saul from persecutor to participant demonstrates his power to incorporate anyone into his church—a gospel reality that shapes how we welcome converts and serve one another.

Monday Romans 8:32

Paul writes that God, who did not spare his own Son, will freely give us all things. This principle of greater-to-lesser reasoning anchors the sermon's core claim: if God accomplished the monumental work of converting his supreme enemy into his most faithful apostle, we can trust him to incorporate any convert into our church body. The God who gave his Son for our redemption will certainly provide what we need to welcome and integrate believers into his family.

Tuesday Galatians 1

Paul's autobiographical account in Galatians 1 mirrors Acts 9: he was a zealous persecutor who knew Scripture intimately, yet that knowledge served his opposition to Christ until the risen Jesus himself arrested him. The sermon's claim emerges sharply here—Saul's transformation was not merely an intellectual adjustment to new doctrinal information, but a supernatural opening of his eyes to see Christ's glory where before he had seen only heresy. Our own growth in Christ requires not just more Bible knowledge, but the Spirit's work opening our hearts to behold Jesus in every page.

Wednesday 1 Thessalonians 2:7-8

Paul reminds the Thessalonians that his apostolic labor flowed from maternal affection for them, willing to share not just the gospel but his own life. This tenderness mirrors Ananias laying hands on the terrified, freshly-converted Saul; it mirrors the rope-holders risking themselves to lower him through the Damascus gate. The sermon's emphasis on secondary characters—not Saul, but those who incorporated him—finds its echo here: God's kingdom advances not through the heroic solo acts of converted enemies, but through the faithful, loving service of ordinary disciples who welcome the wounded and the wavering into Christ's body.

Thursday Philippians 3:12

Paul presses on toward the goal of knowing Christ more fully, yet his relentless pursuit is marked by gratitude for the mercy that seized him as a persecutor. The sermon traces how Saul's conversion history became his pastoral lens: having been Christ's enemy and finding only grace, he approached his new foes not with vengeance but with persuasion. This same posture animates Paul's later ministry—the one who violently opposed the church becomes the one who bears with the weak and resists enemies through prayer, shaped permanently by the transforming grace he himself received.

Friday 1 Thessalonians 2:19-20

Paul's joy and crown consist in his converts standing before Christ—a vision of maturity and belonging that energizes his labor of presence and vulnerability. The sermon's closing application echoes this: we are called to be Ananias-like and Barnabas-like incorporators who welcome new believers into genuine church membership, patient with their doubts and hesitations, helping them stand firm in Christ. Our small acts of faithful service—explaining Christ's mercy, vouching for a convert's sincerity, walking someone through their first weeks in the body—participate in God's work of building his eternal kingdom.
