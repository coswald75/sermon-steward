The Road to Emmaus

Luke 24:13-35 March 27, 2016 R. C. Sproul
Thesis The resurrected Christ revealed Himself to the disciples on the road to Emmaus by opening the Scriptures to show that all of redemptive history—from Genesis to Malachi—testified to the necessity of His suffering and glory, igniting in their hearts a burning conviction that marks genuine faith.

0 · Opening prayer invoking the Holy Spirit's illumination for understanding the text about to be exposited, acknowledging human weakness in comprehension and faith

Our Father and our God, we ask that You help us, because we are fragile in our understanding and feeble in our faith. We ask that You condescend and give to us the Holy Spirit, the Spirit of truth, that He may illumine for us the meaning of the text we have just heard. For we ask these things in the name of Jesus. Amen.

1 · Establishes the narrative frame—two disciples on the Emmaus road encounter the unrecognized risen Christ who asks about their conversation, prompting their astonishment that He seems unaware of recent events

Luke recounts the story of two men walking to Emmaus from Jerusalem, a short enough trip of about seven miles, but one that would take about an hour and a half at a normal pace. As they were walking together on their journey, they were discussing with each other when a stranger appeared behind them and listened in on their conversation. Luke tells us that Jesus Himself drew near and went with them, but their eyes were kept from recognizing Him. He asked them a question: "What is this conversation that you're holding with each other as you walk?" This stopped them in their tracks. "What is this conversation that we're having? Are you the only person in Jerusalem who doesn't know what's been going on over the past few days?" It was as if they said to Him, "Where in the world have you been?"

2 · Sets up a hypothetical response from Jesus, emphasizing His omniscience—He alone fully comprehends the events of the crucifixion and resurrection

Jesus did not answer either the specific or implied question precisely and directly, but had He answered that question, I think the answer would have been something like this. To their first question, He could have said to them, "I'm the one person on earth who understands comprehensively every single thing that has happened in the last few days."

3 · Imaginative reconstruction of what Jesus could have told the disciples—a vivid, first-person retelling of the passion events from Gethsemane through crucifixion to resurrection, making the enormity of their ignorance palpable

Had Jesus answered the implied question of the disciples on the road to Emmaus, He could have said: "Well, let me start on Thursday night. I was with some of My friends, and we celebrated the Passover together. After that, I went into the darkness to the garden of Gethsemane on the Mount of Olives, and I prayed so intensely that I was sweating drops of blood, begging My Father concerning the mission He had set before Me. When that prayer was finished, I went across the brook Kidron. There in the darkness of the night, I was met by a company of soldiers carrying torches and swords, led by a former friend who came up and gave Me the kiss of death, and they arrested Me. Then the soldiers took Me into the city, and they shuttled Me back and forth between the Jewish and Roman authorities, until finally I was put on trial before the Roman procurator. After My interrogation, he said to those standing by, 'I find no fault in Him.' He was about to release Me, but the screaming crowd began to cry for My blood, and they said, 'Crucify Him.' Being a politician driven by expediency, Pontius Pilate surrendered to the voices of the crowd. He had Me scourged, beaten, and crowned with a crown of thorns, then led out of the city of Jerusalem, outside the camp, outside of Zion, to a barren hillside called Golgotha, where I was executed by crucifixion. I was not simply executed as a criminal to satisfy bloodthirsty men, but I was subjected to the unmitigated wrath of God, where before His face, I was forsaken. He placed upon Me His holy curse and counted Me as sin, as He placed upon Me the sins of My people, something no human being had ever been asked to endure in the history of the world. Finally, it ended. It was finished. I commended My soul and spirit to the care of My Father, and I died. They took Me down from the cross, and somebody interceded on My behalf so that, as God had promised, His servant would not suffer corruption. I was entrusted into the hands of a secret disciple whose name was Joseph of Arimathea, a wealthy man who owned a marvelous sepulchre in which I was entombed. They took My dead body, wrapped it in grave clothes, anointed Me with spices, and posted a guard in front of the cave, also guarded by a huge stone rolled in front of it. There I was, a corpse in the tomb. Early this morning, a cosmic burst of creative power supernaturally came upon Me and returned My soul to My body. My brain waves began to operate anew. My heart began to beat and pulsate with blood driving through My veins and arteries and I opened My eyes. Alive, and by the power of God, I was able to escape from the binding grave cloths that held Me there. Angels came and rolled the stone away, and I walked out into the dawn alive. Where have you been for the last few days?"

4 · Pivots from the hypothetical reconstruction back to the biblical text, signaling that Jesus did not respond with such an explanation

Luke's record, however, does not record anything like that.

5 · Exposes the actual response Jesus gave—not an explanation of recent events but a rebuke for failure to believe the prophetic Scriptures that foretold His suffering and glory, followed by a comprehensive scriptural exposition

Luke does not record Jesus answering the question that the disciples raised, but instead, He rebuked them. Listen to the rebuke: "'O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?' And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself."

6 · Unpacks the biblical category of "fool" as a moral rather than intellectual designation, arguing that unbelief stems not from lack of evidence but from willful suppression of God's manifest self-revelation because humans crave autonomy over divine authority

Listen carefully, dear ones, to the rebuke: "O foolish ones, slow to believe all that the Prophets have taught concerning the Messiah." He did not call them stupid. He did not say that they were unintelligent or uneducated. He called them "foolish." In Jewish categories, the term "fool" does not describe somebody of low intelligence. It is not an intellectual assessment. It is a moral one. To be called a fool by God is to come under His judgment, because it is the fool who says in his heart there is no God. You know, we have a national holiday to celebrate what could be called "atheist day"—the first of April. "You are foolish and slow of heart to believe." Why? Because they had a poor teacher? No. God has revealed Himself clearly to every human being in His eternal power and deity. Everybody in the world knows without a doubt of the existence of God, even His eternal power and deity, because God Himself has manifested it to them. The word there in the Greek is phaneros. In Latin, it is manifestum. It is not some isolated esoteric, obscure, shadowy knowledge. It is plain. It is manifest. You do not even need the testimony of creation, because God has planted the knowledge of Himself in your very soul and conscience. You know who He is, but you are slow to acknowledge Him. Why? Because the Scriptures tell us, dear ones, that we do not want to have God in our thoughts or our knowledge. The biggest obstacle and barrier to what we perceive as our joy and happiness is the law of God, and we want to do away with it. We want autonomy. We want the freedom to do whatever we want to do, not what He commands us to do. So, the Scriptures tell us, we will not have Him in our thinking. The deepest and most pernicious bias of all human inclination is that bias against our blessed Creator Himself.

7 · Establishes the magnificence of Jesus's Emmaus Road Bible study—an unparalleled exposition spanning all of redemptive history in roughly ninety minutes, with Christ as the interpretive key

After Jesus rebuked the men, we are told: "'Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?' And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself"—in about an hour to an hour and a half. This was a tour de force of pedagogy. No instructor or professor has ever so eloquently, persuasively, convincingly, and rationally laid out the whole text of sacred Scripture and a summary of all redemptive history as in that hour on the road to Emmaus. Jesus Himself was the instructor: "Let Me tell you what the Scriptures say about Me."

8 · Reconstructs the likely starting point of Jesus's scriptural exposition—the protoevangelium in Genesis 3, where God promises the serpent's defeat through the seed of the woman

Jesus started with Moses. Luke does not tell us in which text He started in Moses' writings, but I imagine it was early, when God pronounced the curse upon the serpent, saying that the seed of the woman would crush the head of that serpent while the seed of the serpent would bruise His heel.

9 · Continues the redemptive-historical survey through the Noahic and Abrahamic covenants, showing that Abraham's imputed righteousness originated from the coming Messiah who would fulfill all righteousness

Surely, He mentioned the covenant with Noah and the covenant with Abraham, how Abraham in Genesis 15 believed God and it was counted to him as righteousness. Where did that righteousness come from? Not from Abraham, but from the One who would come and fulfill all righteousness.

10 · Traces the typological connection between the binding of Isaac and the crucifixion—Abraham's son spared by a substitute ram on the same mountain where God's Son would be sacrificed without intervention two millennia later

God had commanded His disciple Abraham to sacrifice his only son, the one whom he loved, Isaac, and sent him on a few days' journey to Mount Moriah, where he was to offer his son as a living sacrifice. While they were walking, the boy looked at his father and said, "I see the firewood and the ropes, but where's the lamb to sacrifice?" Abraham looked at his son—I think he crossed his fingers for a moment—and he answered, "Jehovah-Jireh, the Lord will provide." When Abraham and Isaac reached the mountaintop, Isaac was tied in the ropes and placed upon the altar, and his father's blade was above his chest. Just as Abraham was ready to plunge the blade into his son, a voice came from heaven saying, "Abraham, Abraham, lay not thy hand upon thy son, for now I know that you trust Me." Behold, there was a racket on the side, and Abraham turned, and there was a ram caught by his horns in the thicket. Jehovah-Jireh. The substitute was given. The ram was slain on Mount Moriah, on the exact location that two thousand years later was named Golgotha, where God took His Son, His only Son, the Son whom He loved, Jesus, and offered Him on a wooden cross, and nobody said, "Stop."

11 · Rapidly surveys the patriarchal narrative through the exodus, listing major redemptive-historical moments from Jacob to Moses's commission at the burning bush

Then Jesus continued to tell the disciples about Jacob and his sons, Joseph, the migration to Egypt, the enslavement of the people, and the appearance of God in the burning bush to Moses, saying: "Moses, Moses, put off your shoes from off your feet, for the ground that you're standing on is holy ground. I have heard the cries of My people in Israel, and I want you to go to Pharaoh and say, 'Let my people go,'" and Moses went.

12 · Highlights the Passover as the climax of the exodus narrative, establishing the typological pattern of blood-bought deliverance that the people were commanded to remember perpetually

Pharaoh resisted and fought against God through plague after plague, until finally came the night of Passover, when God dispatched the avenger, the angel of death, to destroy the firstborn of all the Egyptians. But He said to the Jewish people, "Take the blood of the lamb, sprinkle it on your doorpost, and when I see that blood, My angel will pass over." Moses was instructed by God to set a memorial day that would be celebrated every year, so that the people would never forget the exodus from Egypt.

13 · Identifies the tabernacle as a symbolic foreshadowing of Christ's incarnation, with the tabernacle structure itself prefiguring Jesus as the place where God dwells among His people

Then came the construction of the tabernacle, which was a symbolic description of the person and work of Jesus, who later was the living tabernacle, who became incarnate and dwelt and pitched His tent among us.

14 · Catalogs Jesus's comprehensive survey through the prophetic literature, demonstrating that all of Scripture—from the first book to the last—testifies to the Messiah

Jesus took the men through Isaiah, Jeremiah, Daniel, Ezekiel, the valley of dry bones, Micah's short prophecy about a tiny village that would be the exact location the Messiah would be born, the Sun of Righteousness that would appear, even to the last recorded prophecy in Malachi. From Genesis to Malachi, Jesus intellectually opened up the Scriptures to these men walking to Emmaus.

15 · Asserts the divine necessity and sovereignty behind all redemptive events—nothing occurred by accident, but according to God's eternal decree, making the disciples' slowness to believe all the more culpable

Notice that Jesus said that all these things happened by necessity. It was not an accident that Judas betrayed Jesus that night. It was not an accident that the bloodthirsty rulers of the Jews conspired to destroy Him. These things were ordained from the foundation of the world. They had to happen. God prepared two thousand years of prophecy for His people's information, but how slow they were to believe all those things written in the Prophets and the Law.

16 · Narrates the climactic moment when Jesus broke bread, the disciples' eyes were opened to recognize Him, He vanished, and they testified to the internal burning they had experienced during His exposition

Finally, the men came to the end of their journey. Jesus was going to move on, but they persuaded Him to stay for supper. We read, "When he was at table with them, He took the bread, blessed it, broke it, and gave it to them," and suddenly their eyes were opened. They recognized Him, then just as suddenly, He vanished from their midst. Cleopas and his friend were left alone at the table, and one of them said to the other, "Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures?"

17 · Distinguishes the disciples' experience from mere intellectual assent—the Spirit produced a visceral, heart-level burning that accompanied and authenticated the truth being communicated

Do not miss this. It was not simply a matter of intellectual conviction. It was not just the setting forth of the notitia, which is the necessary data of the content of our saving faith. There was more here than simply the engagement of the mind. It was visceral. The Spirit of God pierced their souls and hearts, and they said one to the other: "I know that my heart was burning inside, and I can't believe that yours wasn't, too. Did not our hearts burn within us as He spoke to us from His Word?"

18 · Sproul testifies to his own conversion experience, identifying personally with the disciples' burning hearts and establishing his ongoing experience of spiritual fire as evidence of genuine conversion

I know exactly what these men were talking about. I know that the day I was converted to Christ, my heart was set on fire, and to this very day, I have fire in my bones that will not quit.

19 · Directly addresses unconverted listeners, diagnosing their spiritual condition as one of frozen hearts unchanged by the Spirit—a deadly coldness toward God

I know there are people listening who are unconverted, and the Holy Spirit has never changed the disposition of their souls, and their hearts are frozen in a deadly coldness to the things of God.

20 · Issues a concrete, urgent charge to unconverted hearers to pray that very night for God to ignite their souls, promising transformation from spiritual coldness to joyful fire that makes casual worship impossible

I pray that before you go to bed this night, when you put your head on the pillow, that you will earnestly pray to God that He will light a match to your soul, so that you will know the joy of Easter, you will have that fire in your bones, and you can never again go to church in a casual or cavalier manner. Oh, that God would set fire to your soul.

  2. Jesus walked the disciples through all of Scripture—from Moses through all the Prophets—to show them something specific about the Messiah. What was the central truth about Christ that the entire Old Testament testified to, and why was this truth so difficult for the disciples to accept?

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc of Christ's self-revelation through Scripture: from the moral character of unbelief, through the divine necessity of His suffering, to the Spirit-wrought burning that marks genuine faith.

Monday Psalm 14:1

The psalmist declares that the fool says in his heart, 'There is no God'—a statement of moral rebellion, not intellectual confusion. The disciples on the road possessed the Scriptures and eyewitness testimony, yet refused to believe; their slowness was not ignorance but willful resistance to what God had plainly revealed. We too face this choice daily: will we acknowledge what God has made manifest, or will we, like the fool, suppress the truth that accuses our hearts?

Tuesday Romans 1:18-21

Paul teaches that God's wrath falls on those who 'suppress the truth in unrighteousness'—they know God, yet they exchange His glory for idols through deliberate rejection. The disciples' inability to see that Christ 'had to suffer' arose not from scriptural obscurity but from hearts unwilling to embrace a Messiah who bore weakness and shame. Our resistance to God's revealed truth often masks a deeper refusal to surrender our will to His.

Wednesday Genesis 3:15

In the very moment of humanity's fall, God announced that the serpent's head would be crushed by the seed of the woman—the first promise of redemptive suffering. From this Genesis whisper through all of Isaiah, Jeremiah, and the Psalms, God wove a two-thousand-year tapestry testifying to Christ's necessary passion. When Jesus opened the Scriptures on the Emmaus road, He was not improvising a defense; He was unfolding the eternal counsel of God that made His cross not a tragedy but the climax of redemptive history.

Thursday Isaiah 53

Isaiah's portrait of the suffering Servant—wounded for our transgressions, bruised for our iniquities, bearing the chastisement of our peace—stands as the clearest Old Testament prophecy of substitutionary atonement. The disciples' despair at the crucifixion sprang partly from missing how these very words pointed to a Messiah who must suffer vicariously for His people. As we meditate on Isaiah's vision, we join the ancient prophets in confessing that Christ's path to the cross was not a detour but the highway God Himself ordained.

Friday Luke 24:32

The disciples reported, 'Did not our hearts burn within us while he opened to us the Scriptures?'—revealing that genuine faith ignites not mere mental agreement but a deep, affective transformation of the soul. The Spirit's work in us does not stop at the head; it sets the heart ablaze with conviction, love, and longing for Christ. When we gather this Sunday to hear Scripture opened, let us pray not merely for understanding but for hearts burning with the fire of the Spirit, that we might be moved from spectators to worshippers seized by the reality of the risen Christ.

Sunday-evening family table

When Your Heart Burns Within You

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to reflect on the difference between knowing something in your head and feeling it in your heart—the kind of deep conviction that changes how you live. Listen for moments when your children describe experiences of sudden clarity or joy about Jesus, and affirm those as signs of the Spirit's work.

On the road to Emmaus, the disciples' hearts burned within them as Jesus opened the Scriptures to show them He had to suffer and rise again. Can you think of a time when you suddenly understood something true about Jesus in a way that made you feel something—not just think about it, but *feel* it? What was that like?
works for ages 8+ — younger children can listen and share simple moments; teens will naturally go deeper into the nature of conviction and faith
