The Whole Big Story for the Bible

Genesis 3 December 29, 2025 Kevin DeYoung
Thesis Genesis 3 introduces the fundamental patterns and problems that the entire biblical story exists to resolve, culminating in Christ's work that reverses the curse and restores humanity to God's presence.

Theological claims· 21
  1. The Bible's central story is about a promised seed—a baby—beginning with Genesis 3:15 and culminating in the Christ child. unit #19
  2. The tabernacle and temple are deliberately designed as types of Eden, and the New Jerusalem fulfills this pattern as the ultimate Holy of Holies where God dwells with His people. unit #21
  3. The fundamental question arising from Genesis 3 that structures the entire biblical narrative is: how can a holy God dwell in the midst of an unholy people? unit #22
  4. Genesis 3's banishment establishes a typological pattern repeated throughout Scripture, with expulsion from God's presence as the consistent consequence of sin. unit #24
  5. The church in this age functions as a type of Eden—a heavenly place on earth—which is why its ethics differ from those governing nations, and final judgment is the ultimate banishment echoing Genesis 3. unit #25
  6. If the Bible is a story about banishment, it is equally a story about coming home to God's presence through the sacrificial system, culminating in the garden city of the New Jerusalem. unit #27
  7. God's provision of garments of skin for Adam and Eve is the first act of divine making after the rest of creation, establishing the pattern of God as both Creator and Savior who covers sin. unit #30
  8. Human efforts at self-justification are as inadequate as fig leaves; sin can only be covered through the death of a substitute, prefigured in the animal skins God provides. unit #31
  9. The Bible traces a consistent pattern from original righteousness through shameful nakedness to divine covering, culminating in the wedding garments of Revelation that represent imputed righteousness. unit #32
  10. The Bible is structured around eating, from the forbidden fruit through the curse of toil to the dietary laws and feast days that prefigure salvation by eating. unit #33
  11. The Bible's eating motif culminates in Jesus' reversal of Eve's 'take and eat' at the Last Supper, where the same action that brought destruction now brings salvation when directed toward Christ. unit #34
  12. The Bible's tree motif shows humanity ruined by a tree (the forbidden fruit), saved by a tree (the cross), and restored by a tree (the tree of life in the New Jerusalem). unit #35
  13. Angels function throughout Scripture as guardians of God's sacred presence, beginning with the cherubim guarding Eden's entrance. unit #36
  14. Angels attend every major moment in Christ's earthly life, and the resurrection angel's role is not to release Jesus from the tomb but to invite witnesses to see the empty grave. unit #38
  15. The folded grave clothes in John's Gospel indicate that Jesus' resurrection was effortless—death had no claim on Him once sin was atoned for. unit #39
  16. The resurrection reverses the angelic role from Eden: instead of guarding to keep people out, the angel at the tomb invites people in to witness that death cannot contain Jesus. unit #40
  17. Marriage is woven into the fabric of creation as the culmination of complementary pairs, ultimately imaging the union of heaven and earth and Christ and the church. unit #42
  18. Revelation's climactic marriage of heaven and earth reunites the first couple separated by sin, which is why the biblical definition of marriage cannot be treated as a minor theme. unit #43
  19. Revelation 12 deliberately echoes Genesis 3's fundamental cast—woman, seed, and serpent—showing that the Bible's story remains fundamentally about these three characters. unit #45
  20. Revelation systematically eliminates the antagonists in reverse order, culminating in the serpent's destruction and leaving only the man and woman—now as Christ and His bride. unit #46
  21. The covenant formula 'I will be your God and you will be My people' runs from Genesis through Revelation, fulfilled ultimately in God's eternal dwelling with redeemed humanity. unit #47

29 · DeYoung summarizes the second and third themes in parallel structure before moving to the fourth theme

So if the Bible is a book about banishment, it's also a book about coming home.

30 · DeYoung introduces the fourth theme—the Bible as a story about clothing—rooted in God's provision of garments after the fall, showing God's role as both Creator and Savior

Don't need the grave clothes because the wages of sin is death, and when sin has been atoned for, death has no claim on him. He literally could not stay dead.

40 · DeYoung completes the angel theme with the resurrection reversal: the angel at the tomb no longer guards to keep people out but invites them in to witness that Jesus cannot be kept in

You say, what does this have to do with angels? Well, remember, what do angels do? All throughout the Bible, they guard things. They guard the presence of the Holy. They keep... Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week traces how Genesis 3's fundamental patterns—the promise of a seed, the problem of God's holiness dwelling with unholy people, the covering through sacrifice, and the return home to paradise—structure the entire biblical narrative and find their fulfillment in Christ.

Monday Galatians 3

Paul roots the entire gospel in the singular promise God made to Abraham: 'to your seed' (3:16). The seed is Christ. From Genesis 3:15's cryptic announcement that a child would crush the serpent to Galatians' clarification that this child is Jesus, Scripture's spine is the movement toward one man who would undo Adam's fall. In the gospel, we grasp that all of history moves toward this one baby born to reverse our ruin.

Tuesday Zechariah 3

Joshua the high priest stands before the Lord filthy in guilt, yet the angel commands that he be clothed in clean garments and a clean turban placed on his head—a reversal of Adam's shame and nakedness. Zechariah's vision embodies the question that haunts all Scripture: can the unholy approach the Holy without perishing? The gospel answers: yes, when we are covered not by our works but by Christ's imputed righteousness, just as the high priest's garments prefigure our wedding garments in glory.

Wednesday John 6

Jesus declares, 'I am the bread of life; whoever comes to me shall not hunger.' The motif that began with Eve's fateful 'take and eat' of the forbidden fruit reaches its redemptive climax when Jesus invites us to eat His flesh and drink His blood. What destroyed us through disobedience now restores us through faith—the same action of eating, but directed toward Christ, transforms us from death to life. We eat not to grasp forbidden knowledge but to commune with the One who defeated the curse.

Thursday Revelation 12

John's final vision returns us to the cast of Eden: a woman, a seed (the child to rule all nations), and the great red dragon—the serpent himself. Yet now the outcome is certain. The woman is clothed with the sun and crowned with twelve stars, the child is born to rule with an iron scepter, and the dragon is cast down from heaven. The pattern established in Genesis 3 reaches its apocalyptic resolution: the seed has crushed the serpent, and the woman—now the bride of Christ—stands triumphant in glory.

Friday Revelation 21-22

The story that began with Adam and Eve banished from Eden's garden city ends with the bride of Christ—His church—dwelling eternally in the garden city of the New Jerusalem. God's promise from Genesis onward—'I will be your God and you will be My people'—reaches its consummation: 'Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people' (21:3). Our banishment is reversed, our exile ended, and our longing for home fulfilled as we feast at the tree of life in God's presence forever.
