The Hidden Beauty of the Gospel

1 Corinthians 1:18-25 Voddie Baucham
Thesis Christ is beautiful not because of His physical appearance—which Scripture says was unremarkable—but because the gospel of His substitutionary death on the cross is the only solution to humanity's sin problem and the only means by which guilty sinners can approach a holy God without experiencing His wrath.

Theological claims· 12
  1. Scripture declares Christ lacked physical beauty, creating a theological paradox with the church's confession of Him as beautiful Savior. unit #3
  2. Christ's beauty is not physical but theological—found in the gospel of His person and work on behalf of believers. unit #8
  3. Because the gate is narrow and few find it, the majority of humanity does not perceive the beauty of the gospel—proving that this beauty is not equally accessible to all people. unit #11
  4. Gospel beauty is inaccessible to many because God has sovereignly blinded eyes and hardened hearts, making spiritual perception fundamentally different from natural perception. unit #14
  5. Understanding that salvation is God's supernatural work rather than human achievement liberates believers from the impossible burden of believing their wisdom, winsomeness, or cleverness produces faith. unit #26
  6. The beauty of Christ is revealed only through the preaching of Christ crucified—only there does He become visible as fairest Lord Jesus and beautiful Savior. unit #33
  7. The cross, though objectively horrendous and barbaric, is where Christ's beauty is revealed because it displays Him as the suffering servant who bears humanity's sin. unit #35
  8. All humanity is guilty before God and innately knows it, as evidenced by the universal human sense that justice ought to exist and sin ought to be punished. unit #37
  9. The problem of evil argument fails because if God eliminated all evil, every human being would be destroyed, since all people commit evil worthy of God's wrath. unit #38
  10. All non-Christian religions fail because they cannot produce true heart transformation and provide no solution for past sin—problems that only Christ's substitutionary atonement resolves. unit #39
  11. Christ is beautiful because He is the only solution to humanity's sin problem and the only hope of escaping the deserved wrath of a holy God. unit #40
  12. The church's hymns of grace and substitutionary atonement testify that Christ's beauty is found in the gospel's provision of salvation for wretches who deserve wrath. unit #41

You see, in earthly wisdom, preaching the cross to these two groups of people, by the way, everybody falls into one of these two groups. According to earthly wisdom, preaching the cross is folly. It's foolishness. It makes no sense, and yet.

31 · Extends the exposition to 1 Corinthians 2:1-5 to demonstrate Paul's own apostolic practice—he deliberately rejected lofty speech and human wisdom in preaching, choosing only to proclaim Christ crucified so that the Corinthians' faith would rest on God's power rather than human persuasion

In the next chapter, 1 Corinthians chapter 2, here's how Paul starts the chapter. And I, when I came to you, brothers, did not come proclaiming to you the testimony of God with lofty speech or wisdom, for I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and Him crucified. And I was with you in weakness and in fear and much trembling. And my speech and my message were not in persuasive words of wisdom, but in demonstration of the Spirit and of power, so that your faith might not rest in the wisdom of men, but in the power of God. I didn't come sticking my wet finger in the air to see which way the wind is blowing so that I could know what these people want and what these people want and just give them what they want. I committed myself to the folly of preaching the gospel. This beautiful, glorious gospel.

32 · Brings in 2 Timothy 4:1-5 to show that Paul's apostolic charge to Timothy was to preach the word faithfully regardless of whether people want to hear it, warning that people will accumulate teachers who suit their passions rather than speak truth

In 2 Timothy chapter 4, that's exactly what he tells young Timothy to do. He says, I charge you in the presence of God and of Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing in his kingdom, preach the word, be ready in season and out of season, reprove, rebuke, exhort with complete patience and teaching. For the time is coming when people will not endure sound teaching, but having itching ears, they will accumulate for themselves teachers to suit their own passions, and will turn away from listening to the truth and wander off into myths. As for you, always be sober-minded, endure suffering, Do the work of an evangelist. Fulfill your ministry. We preach the folly of the gospel.

33 · Asserts that the beauty of Christ is made evident specifically and exclusively through the preaching of the cross—only when the foolishness of the crucified Christ is proclaimed does His beauty become visible to those whom God calls

Why? Because that's where the beauty of the gospel is evident. That's where the beauty of Christ is evident. That's there and only there do we understand why He is fairest Lord Jesus. There and only there do we understand why He is beautiful Savior.

34 · Expounds 1 Corinthians 1:24-25, emphasizing that those whom God calls from both groups—regardless of their ethnic or philosophical category—perceive Christ as God's power and wisdom, and that God's apparent foolishness and weakness surpass all human wisdom and strength

Verses 24 and 25. But to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ, the power of God. Notice, those who are called both Jews and Greeks. From both of those groups, ethnically, theologically, and philosophically. But to those who are called both Jews and Greeks, Christ, the power of God, and the wisdom of God. For the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men.

35 · Asserts the central paradox: the cross is objectively horrendous and barbaric, yet it is precisely there that Christ's beauty becomes visible because the cross reveals Him as the suffering servant who bears humanity's sin as prophesied in Isaiah 53

It is the cross. That's where we see the beauty. The cross is horrendous. And yet that's where we see the beauty of Christ. The cross is barbaric. It's heinous. And yet that's where we see the beauty of Christ. Why? Because that's where we see that he is the suffering servant. That's where we see. At the end of Isaiah's words, what did he say? All we like sheep had gone astray. Each of us had turned to his own way, but God hath laid upon him the iniquity of us all.

36 · Expounds the doctrine of substitutionary atonement through multiple New Testament texts, demonstrating that Christ's beauty consists in God laying human sin on the sinless Christ so that sinners might become God's righteousness, and that this substitution is the ultimate demonstration of love

God took my sin and laid it on Christ. Paul says it this way, God made him who knew no sin to be sin for us, so that we might come become the righteousness of God in him. God demonstrated his love for us in this, and that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us. That's the beauty. Greater love have no man than this, that he lay down his life for his friends. That's the beauty of the gospel.

37 · Establishes the anthropological foundation for the gospel's beauty by arguing that universal human awareness of fairness and justice demonstrates innate knowledge that we stand guilty before a holy God who must judge sin

Here's the beauty. The beauty is that all of us are guilty before a holy and righteous God. Every man everywhere is guilty before God. And we all know it. We are all aware of it. One of the first phrases that we learn to say with conviction, I don't care what language you grew up speaking, one of the first phrases you learn to say with conviction is some variation of, that's not fair. Amen? I mean, kids can say that with conviction, that's not fair. Because we know that the world ought to be, we know that there ought to be justice. We get that. And because we know that there ought to be justice, we know that there ought to be justice in terms of sin and righteousness. We know it.

38 · Confronts the problem of evil argument against God by turning it inward—showing that if God eliminated all evil, the objector himself would be destroyed, because every person commits evil worthy of God's wrath, making human guilt the real problem rather than God's justice

When we see evil appearing to prevail in the world, We cry out. In fact, there are many people who say that the reason that they don't believe in God is because they see evil in the world. How can there be this good, all good and all powerful God if there's evil in the world? In other words, they recognize the fact that a good and righteous and powerful God has to crush evil, but they fall short of recognizing the fact that if God got rid of all evil last night, none of us would be here this morning. Amen somebody. Because on yesterday you thought and said and did things for which you deserve the wrath of God. You. It's easy to point out there and say that's not fair. It's easy to point out there and say that's injustice. But it's very difficult to point in here and recognize that in you dwells all of that evil and all of that injustice and that a holy and righteous God owes you wrath.

39 · Establishes the inadequacy of all non-Christian religions by demonstrating two fatal flaws in the works-righteousness system: first, fallen humanity cannot achieve moral goodness because the heart remains wretched despite outward religious behavior; second, even if future goodness were possible, past sin remains unaddressed, leaving no solution for guilt

What are you going to do? What are you going to do? The rest of the religious world basically says this, you need to have a religious experience, And then after you have your religious experience, you need to do more good things than bad things and hope for the best when you die. That's all the other religions in the world in a nutshell. Is it an oversimplification? Only slightly. But essentially, that's it. That's it. Two problems with that. Problem number one, you can't be good. You can't. you won't you have your religious experience and then you go living your life maybe you wear some special clothes you know that identify you as part of your religion and you you take on you know different vocabulary and different habits and you know you go to your services and you do this that and the other you give money to the poor but your heart is still wretched you still harbor hatred and envy There is still rage in you. There's still lust in you. Yes, you're going through all the motions outwardly, but inwardly those things are still there. So here's my question. What deals with that sin? Here's the other problem. You have your religious experience. And from that moment on, you try to do more good things than bad things. But what about the things you did before your religious experience? What washes that away? What? You don't have an answer. There is no answer apart from Christ.

40 · Declares the climax of the sermon's argument: Christ's substitutionary death is beautiful because He alone solves humanity's otherwise insoluble problem of deserved wrath, offering mercy and salvation by grace to those who deserve only condemnation

Christ died for sin once for all, the just for the unjust, in order that he might bring us to God. What is beautiful and glorious about the gospel? What is beautiful and glorious about Christ? He is the only answer to our sin problem. He is the only hope that any of us has of facing a righteous and holy and just God. and coming away from that encounter with anything other than wrath, death, and hell. Because you deserve that. And it's all you deserve. Wrath, death, hell, that's it. But God, being rich in mercy, because of the great love with which He loved us, made us alive together with Christ. By grace, you've been saved. By grace, there is nothing more beautiful than that.

41 · Grounds the church's doxological response in the gospel's beauty, citing hymns that celebrate salvation by grace and cleansing by Christ's blood, demonstrating that the church's worship confirms the sermon's thesis about where Christ's beauty resides

That's why we sing songs like Amazing Grace, how sweet the sound that saved a wretch like me. I once was lost, but now I'm found, was blind, but now I see. We sing songs like, what can wash away my sin? Nothing but the blood of Jesus. What can make me whole again? Nothing but the blood of Jesus. Oh, precious is that flow that makes me white as snow. No other fount I know, nothing but the blood of Jesus. And we could go on and on and on. And it's beautiful. And it's precious. And there is nothing more beautiful and nothing more precious in all the world. The beauty of Christ is found in the beauty of the gospel.

42 · Issues direct evangelistic appeal to those who do not yet perceive Christ's beauty, calling them to flee to Christ alone for salvation from sin and wrath, and concluding with prayer that hearers would recognize that Christ's righteousness is sufficient for all who call on Him

I don't need a picture, a painting, or a carving. to know that Christ is fairer than anything, that He is indeed a beautiful Savior. And if He is anything other than that to you, then I would beseech you, I would plead with you to flee to Christ. to place your trust in Christ and Christ alone, to save you from your sin, to save you from the wrath of God. Because you know that justice is required. And you know that righteousness is required. And you also know that your righteousness is insufficient. My prayer for you is that you now know that Christ's righteousness is beautiful and glorious because it is indeed more than enough, not only for you, but for all who would call on his name.

43 · Closes the sermon by turning the congregation's attention from horizontal proclamation to vertical worship, praising God for the beauty of Christ and the gospel, and petitioning that hearers would trust Christ alone for salvation

Let's pray. Our great God and Heavenly Father, we thank You and praise You for Your goodness and mercy and kindness toward us. Through the person and work of Your beautiful, majestic Son, our Lord and our Savior, Jesus Christ. Christ who is fairer than sun, moon, stars, Flowers of spring, meadows, anything, anything that we have ever seen or will ever see. Christ who shines brighter than anything that we will ever see. We praise You and thank You for the beauty of the Gospel. Praise you and thank you for the grace found therein, that grace whereby we might be saved from your wrath when we come to you in repentance and faith. Grant by your grace that we would do precisely that, that we would trust you today, that we would cling to the cross today and only to the cross. to Christ and only to Christ. For it is in his name that we pray and ask all these things. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we meditate on the paradox at the sermon's heart: Christ's beauty is not physical but theological—found solely in the gospel of His substitutionary work, sovereignly revealed to those whom God calls, and accessible only through the preaching of the cross.

Monday Isaiah 53:1-6

Isaiah saw the Servant stripped of comeliness, bearing our iniquities, crushed for our transgressions. Here we glimpse the deepest beauty: not a handsome face, but a willing heart that ransoms guilty sinners through His own suffering. The gospel's beauty emerges precisely where human eyes see only disfigurement and shame—in the cross where Christ becomes our substitute.

Tuesday John 12:36-40

John declares that many saw Jesus's signs yet did not believe—their blindness fulfilling Isaiah's prophecy that God hardened their hearts. We cannot perceive the beauty of the gospel through human wisdom or effort; God Himself must open our eyes to see Christ's glory. This sovereign work liberates us from the burden of believing our cleverness converts anyone.

Wednesday Romans 5:8

While we were still sinners—helpless, hostile, guilty—Christ died for us. The cross displays not human moral progress but divine love breaking into a world of condemned rebels. In this moment of substitution, where the innocent bears the penalty of the guilty, the beauty of the gospel becomes visible: Christ alone stands between us and the wrath we deserve.

Thursday 2 Corinthians 5:21

Christ was made sin for us so we might become His righteousness—an exchange we could never earn or merit. God accomplished what no human work, no religious effort, no clever argument can accomplish: He substituted His own Son in our place. This radical divine initiative strips away all pretense that our evangelism, our eloquence, or our goodness secures anyone's salvation.

Friday 2 Timothy 4:1-5

Timothy is charged to preach the word, to reprove and exhort with patience and teaching, knowing that people will not endure sound doctrine. Yet this faithful proclamation of the gospel—simple, central, unadorned—is where Christ's beauty shines brightest to those with ears to hear. Our calling is not to make the cross more palatable but to herald it boldly, trusting God to open blind eyes through the foolishness of preaching.

Memory verse this week

1 Corinthians 1:23-24

but we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles, but to those who are called, both Jews and Greeks, Christ the power of God and the wisdom of God.

Why this verse: This verse crystallizes the sermon's central paradox: the cross appears as weakness and folly to the perishing, yet to the called it reveals Christ as the power and wisdom of God. It anchors the entire argument that Christ's beauty is accessible only through sovereign grace, not human wisdom, and is found uniquely in the preaching of His substitutionary death.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. When Isaiah 53:1-6 describes the Servant as having 'no beauty that we should desire him,' what does this declaration mean for how we understand what makes Christ attractive to believers?
    Isaiah 53:1-6
    → How does this square with the language we use in worship—calling Jesus 'fairest Lord' or 'beautiful Savior'—if He lacked physical comeliness?
  2. According to 1 Corinthians 1:18-25, what is the fundamental difference between how the perishing perceive the cross and how those being saved perceive it?
    1 Corinthians 1:18-25
    → What does Paul mean when he says the cross is 'foolishness' to some and 'the power of God' to others—is he describing different interpretations of the same event, or something more fundamental?
  3. The sermon claims that the beauty of the gospel is not equally accessible to all people, pointing to the narrow gate of Matthew 7. What does this mean about who gets to see Christ as beautiful, and how does that reality sit with you?
  4. Jesus tells His disciples in Luke 10:21-22 that the Father has 'hidden these things from the wise and revealed them to infants,' and John 12:36-40 describes Jesus saying many 'could not believe' because God had blinded their eyes. How does God's sovereign work in opening and closing spiritual perception reshape the way you think about witnessing or evangelism?
    John 12:36-40
    → Does this sovereign blinding mean some people are hopeless, or does it mean our faith-producing work is God's alone—and what difference does that make to how you pray and speak to unbelievers?
  5. The sermon argues that all non-Christian religions fail because they cannot address past sin or transform the heart from within—and that only Christ's substitutionary atonement (2 Corinthians 5:21) does this. How does understanding Christ's death as bearing your sin in your place change the way you see your own standing before God?
    2 Corinthians 5:21
    → When you sin this week, how will remembering that Christ has already borne your guilt change the way you confess and receive His forgiveness?
  6. If the beauty of Christ is revealed only through the preaching of the cross, what does that imply about our responsibility as a church to proclaim Christ crucified, and what does it imply about where we should direct our deepest attention and devotion?
