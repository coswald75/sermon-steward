The Heart of a Father

Proverbs 3:1-7 Voddie Baucham
Thesis A father's heart, modeled in Proverbs and fulfilled in the gospel, seeks not merely to inform his children with basic instruction or transform them through moral formation, but ultimately to lead them to redemption through repentance and faith in Christ.

Theological claims· 8
  1. The cultural war on masculinity and patriarchy is wicked because it prevents fathers from fulfilling their God-designed roles, producing the opposite of what families need. unit #15
  2. Proverbs 3:5-8 is the gospel in the Old Testament, and the father's ultimate goal is that his children submit the entirety of their lives to God. unit #29
  3. The father's goal is to raise children who trust God with their whole hearts, seek His mind in all things, and acknowledge Him in everything, not just in crises. unit #31
  4. Proverbs 3:7-8 is the gospel in the Old Testament, and the promise of healing and refreshment is the good news—this is the ultimate desire of a father's heart. unit #34
  5. The war on masculinity and patriarchy is wicked because God shares His name with the office of father, revealing the supreme importance of fatherhood. unit #35
  6. Financial provision is not enough—fathers have a God-given duty to instruct their children toward transformation, maturity, and redemption through the gospel. unit #36
  7. If a father provides material needs and basic instruction but fails to give his children the gospel, he has failed—producing well-educated, well-fed children who are hell-bound. unit #37
  8. The true heart of a father seeks his children's redemption, not just their provision or instruction, and the weight of this calling is overwhelming—no father is qualified in himself. unit #38

Sir, God bless you for bringing in money for school fees. God bless you for putting food on the table, but that is not enough. You have a duty to uphold, a responsibility to fulfill, to instruct your children so that they might be transformed and come to maturity and ultimately, by God's grace, that they might hear the gospel and that they might respond to it in repentance and faith.

37 · The pastor makes a stark personal declaration: if his children have material provision and even basic instruction but do not receive the gospel from him, he has failed

If my children have school fees and food on the table, But don't get from me the good news of the person and work of Jesus Christ. I failed. I've absolutely failed. If the only thing that they can remember at the end of the day is some instructions that I gave them about how to do this and how to do that and how not to do this and how not to do that. They have not gotten from me the essence of the gospel. I've missed it. I've missed it. And I will just have well-educated, well-fed children who are hell bound.

38 · The pastor reiterates the sermon's main thesis—a father's heart seeks redemption, not just provision or instruction—and acknowledges the overwhelming weight of this calling, confessing his own inadequacy

No, that's not the heart of a father. This, this is the heart of a father. And it's overwhelming. It is absolutely overwhelming. I am not qualified for this job. Amen, somebody. I'm not.

39 · The pastor illustrates the impossibility of being 'ready' for parenthood with a humorous hypothetical scenario about couples waiting to be ready, only to discover after a few hours with the baby that they weren't ready after all

I chuckle, you know, when young couples are, you know, talking about having children and, you know, we just want to make sure, you know, to wait until we're ready. You'll never have kids. You'll never have kids. Because you'll never be ready. You'll wait until you're ready and then here's what'll happen. You'll say, okay, fine, we're ready. And then you'll get pregnant, the baby will come, you'll start parenting, and you will eventually, you know, after about, three, four hours, you will eventually say, we were not ready for this. So you might as well just go ahead and do it now. Amen.

40 · The pastor issues the sermon's central application: since no father is ready or qualified, the heart of a father must be utterly dependent on God—and must model, point, and remind children of this same dependence, grounded in the father's own redemption through Christ

You're not ready for it, which is why the heart of a father is a heart that is utterly dependent upon God always. That's the only way to get there from here. Utterly dependent upon God. Modeling utter dependency upon God. Pointing our children again and again to utter dependency upon God. Reminding our children that we are who we are because we've come to God in repentance and faith. Because we've been transformed Because we've been redeemed by the person and work of the Lord Jesus Christ. And that because of it, our greatest goal and desire and highest calling is to point them in that very same direction.

41 · The pastor closes with a doxological charge, praying that God's grace would enable fathers to fulfill this calling for God's glory

May it be so of us. by God's grace for his glory.

42 · The pastor closes with a pastoral prayer thanking God for the privilege of calling Him Father and carrying the title of father, asking for grace to be faithful in instructing, transforming, and calling children to redemption in Christ, acknowledging that children ultimately belong to God

Let's pray. Our gracious God, we thank you and we praise you for the privilege of calling you our father. And for those of us who are fathers, for the privilege of being able to carry that title. It is beyond humbling. It is literally overwhelming. Grant by your grace that we might be found faithful, that we might be found faithful to the end that we seek to instruct and transform our children and call them to redemption in Christ. For ultimately they are not ours, they belong to you. Grant that we might raise them and teach them and train them and instruct them as though we believe that to be true. Grant this we pray, for Christ's sake and in His name, Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the father's heart from its foundation in the fear of the Lord, through the gospel's transformative power, to the overwhelming joy of seeing our children redeemed in Christ.

Monday Proverbs 1:7

Solomon begins his fatherly counsel not with rules or rules, but with the premise that wisdom itself flows from reverent submission to God. As fathers, we cannot transmit what we do not possess; our instruction is only as weighty as our own trembling before the Lord. The fear of God is not terrified avoidance but glad acknowledgment of His supremacy—and this becomes the atmosphere in which our children learn to think, choose, and live.

Tuesday Proverbs 1:2-4

Notice that Solomon's stated purpose includes not just knowing wisdom and instruction, but receiving disciplined wisdom and righteousness—virtues that shape character from within. A father's words are meant to penetrate the heart, producing the kind of discernment that enables a young person to navigate life with moral clarity. We are called to raise our children not as mere repositories of facts, but as reflective, wise stewards learning to think God's thoughts after Him.

Wednesday Ephesians 6:1-4

Paul's charge to fathers in Ephesians is unambiguous: the father himself, not the school or the church alone, is accountable for the spiritual formation of his children. The phrase "bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" echoes the Old Testament picture of a father personally binding truth on his child's heart. This is not a burden to bear alone, but a stewardship to steward in dependence on God and in community with the church—yet the father remains the primary disciple-maker in his home.

Thursday Proverbs 3:5-8

These verses crystallize the gospel in the Old Testament: trust God with everything, lean not on your own understanding, acknowledge Him in all your ways, refuse the pride of self-reliance, and receive the healing that comes through submission. A father's deepest desire is not that his children succeed by the world's measure, but that they experience the wholeness, peace, and vitality that flow from complete surrender to Christ. This is the "good news" every father longs to see his children grasp and live out—not redemption as escape, but as entrance into the abundant life God intends.

Friday Romans 5

Romans 5 unfolds the gospel: we are sinners justified by faith in Christ's substitutionary work, and our standing before God rests not on our righteousness but on His. A father who has tasted this grace—who has been humbled by the reality of his own sin and overwhelmed by unmerited favor in Christ—becomes a living testimony to his children of the very message he seeks to instill. Our weakness and acknowledged need for redemption are not disqualifications for fatherhood; they are the very credentials that make us faithful stewards of this calling, pointing our children not to our sufficiency but to Christ's.

Sunday-evening family table

What Does Dad Trust?

For the parent

This prompt invites children to notice the difference between a father who *tells* his kids what to do and a father who actually *shows* them how to live. Listen for whether your kids can see that a real father's job is bigger than rules—it's about pointing them to Jesus.

Dad, can you tell us about a time when you had to trust God instead of just trusting yourself? What did you learn, and how did that change what you do as our father?
works for ages 8+ — younger kids can listen and learn from dad's answer; teens will grasp the deeper connection between dad's faith and his leadership
Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What does Solomon mean when he tells his son to 'bind' steadfast love and faithfulness around his neck and 'write them on the tablet of your heart' (Proverbs 3:3)? What is the difference between a father merely telling his children what is true and a father whose instruction is bound to them in this way?
    Proverbs 3:3
    → How have you experienced truth in your own life shifting from something you merely knew intellectually to something woven into the fabric of how you actually live?
  2. The sermon claims that Proverbs 3:5-8 is 'the gospel in the Old Testament.' Walk us through what Solomon is calling his son to do in these verses—what does it mean to 'trust in the Lord with all your heart' and 'acknowledge him in all your ways'?
    Proverbs 3:5-8
  3. According to the sermon, a father's heart has three movements: instruction, transformation, and redemption. Why is it not enough for a father to provide material needs and basic information? What would a well-fed, well-educated child lack if his father never pointed him toward Christ?
    → Can you think of ways a father might accidentally communicate that obedience to God's commands is the ultimate goal rather than trust in Christ as the ultimate goal?
  4. The sermon emphasizes that no father is 'qualified in himself' to raise redeemed children—the calling is 'overwhelming.' What does this confession reveal about the fallen condition we all face as fathers or parents? And how does this weakness become the very place where we must depend on God?
  5. Proverbs 1:7 teaches that 'the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge.' How does a father cultivate in his children a reverent awe of God—not mere rule-following, but a deep conviction that God is to be feared and trusted with everything?
    Proverbs 1:7
    → What does it look like when you, as a parent or mentor, model that same fear of the Lord in your own decisions—especially in moments where your children are watching?
  6. The sermon presents the gospel as the heart's remedy for the son's condition (Proverbs 3:8 speaks of 'healing to your flesh and refreshment to your bones'). How does the gospel of Christ—His substitutionary death and resurrection—accomplish what Solomon calls his son to in these verses? What does it mean that a redeemed child can now 'trust in the Lord' because Christ has already secured his redemption?
    Proverbs 3:8, Romans 5
