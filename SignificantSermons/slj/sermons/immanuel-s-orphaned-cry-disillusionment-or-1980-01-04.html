Immanuel's Orphaned Cry – Disillusionment or Dependence?

Matthew 27:45-49 January 4, 1980 S. Lewis Johnson
Thesis The cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' reveals that Jesus Christ died as a penal substitute, bearing divine judgment for sin under a judicial rather than paternal relationship with God, making substitution both necessary and sufficient for atonement.

Illustrations· 3
  1. cultural reference · unit #5 — Johnson uses the irony of Unitarian hymnody to illustrate his point. Even those who deny the deity of Christ and his atoning work have recognized (in Bowring's hymn) that the cross is where 'all the light of sacred story gathers.' This cultural-religious illustration reinforces the claim that the cross cannot be a tragedy.
  2. personal story · unit #27 — Johnson narrates a personal moment of exegetical insight — while studying on a flight, he realized Jesus cried with a loud voice not for his own sake but for the sake of witnesses at the cross. This is a personal story used to illustrate a theological point: the cry was pedagogical, intended to reveal the meaning of Jesus's suffering. The story builds ethos (personal investment in the text) while making the claim.
  3. historical example · unit #28 — Johnson illustrates the doctrine of substitution with a missionary story from Henry Mabie. A tribal chief, hearing the gospel for the first time, interrupts the missionary to cry out that Jesus doesn't belong on the cross — 'I belong on the cross.' The Holy Spirit illuminates the chief's heart to see substitution. This narrative illustration is intended to move the heart and demonstrate the power of the gospel to convict and convert when the doctrine is preached clearly.
Theological claims· 14
  1. The death of Jesus was qualitatively different from all other noble deaths in history — it was marked by desolation, despair, and apparent divine abandonment, which demands theological explanation. unit #3
  2. The Christian reverence for the cross throughout history proves that the cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' cannot be a cry of disillusionment but must contain positive theological meaning. unit #4
  3. The cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' is the interpretive key to the entire doctrine of the atonement. unit #6
  4. Penal substitution is the primary meaning of the atonement, but there are depths to Christ's suffering that we will continue to explore throughout eternity without ever exhausting the mystery. unit #11
  5. The shift from 'Father' to 'My God' reveals that at the cross, Jesus's relationship with God was no longer paternal but judicial — he was addressing God as a human being under divine judgment, standing as a judicial substitute and sacrifice. unit #13
  6. The cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' is a cry of dependence, not disillusionment — the possessive 'my' reveals unbroken trust even under divine judgment. unit #14
  7. Jesus asked 'why hast thou forsaken me?' because in his human nature he had to fully experience what he bore, and the question itself reveals his conscious innocence — he knew no sin in himself that would warrant divine judgment. unit #18
  8. Substitution is the only explanation for Jesus's forsaking — a loving God would never forsake the only good man for his own sins, so he must have been forsaken as a substitute bearing the sins of others. unit #19
  9. Penal substitution offends modern sensibilities because it forces us to confront our guilt and need for a substitute, but the Bible plainly teaches that Jesus died under divine judgment as a substitute for sinners. unit #20
  10. The cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' is the inevitable sequel to Gethsemane — the cup Jesus prayed to avoid was the cup of divine forsaking, which he drank on the cross. unit #21
  11. Substitution is necessary because the law demands perfect love of God and neighbor, which no human being (including the greatest biblical saints) has ever achieved. unit #22
  12. Because Christ paid the penalty for sin as our substitute, God cannot justly demand payment again from those who are in Christ — the debt is fully satisfied. unit #24
  13. At the moment Jesus cried 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' he was becoming the curse and being made sin for us, so that we might be made the righteousness of God in him. unit #25
  14. The darkness and forsaking Jesus experienced on the cross reveal what hell is — infinite and eternal separation from God — and Jesus bore it in our place. unit #26

3 · Johnson establishes the interpretive problem: unlike Stephen's radiant death or Socrates' philosophical resignation, Jesus died in apparent despair and desolation

I am sure that as you think about the death of Jesus Christ, there is one thing that stands out about was a very strange exodus. The death of Jesus Christ was not as radiant. Stephen's death as someone has said a martyr's death under a hail of stones. It was not as Socrates died in scornfully superior resignation. Rather, the Lord Jesus died with a helpless, despairing cry in the most desolate isolation. It is strange from that standpoint, and I don't think that we shall ever be get over the fact that that characteristic pertains to our Lord's death. Joseph Klausner, a Jewish student who wrote a very important book on the Lord Jesus, entitled Jesus of Nazareth, said, the dream of his life had vanished. His life's work had perished. The thought was unbearable. In his terrible anguish of heart, he summoned up all his remaining strength and cried out in his mother tongue, in the language of the book he loved, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? H.G. wells, who has never been noted as a fundamentalist, said the symbol of the crucifixion, the drooping, pain drenched figure of Christ, the sorrowful cry to his Father, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? These things jar within our spirit. Finally someone else has said, Jesus died in agony as an outcast and a criminal. His father failed him at last. Jesus died utterly disappointed. He was deserted and disillusioned.

4 · Johnson reframes the interpretive problem as a contradiction: if Jesus died disillusioned, how can Paul glory in the cross and Luther call the theology of the cross 'the theology of light'? This rhetorical question pivots the sermon from problem to resolution — the cry must mean something other than despair

Was our Lord deserted and disillusioned by his death on the cross? If that were true, it would be very difficult to explain how Christians have felt about the cross of the Lord Jesus down through the centuries. After all, was not the Apostle Paul the person who said, when he wrote to the Corinthians, when I was in your midst, I determined not to know anything among you, save Jesus Christ and him crucified? He said, God forbid that I should glory save in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, by which the world is crucified unto me and I the world. How is it possible for Christians to so revere him and his Cross and at the same time make the claim that the Lord Jesus died utterly deserted and disillusioned? Luther did not feel that way. He wrote, theologia crucus, theologia lucus, or the theology of the cross is the theology of light.

6 · Johnson pivots from the Bowring illustration to his governing claim: the cry 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' is not a stumbling block but the very key to understanding the atonement

It is true, all the light of sacred story does gather round his head sublime. And the statement, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Rather than being a stumbling block, might well be the insight that would lead us to a full and true understanding of what happened when Jesus Christ died.

7 · Johnson catalogs the seven words from the cross and demonstrates that 'My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?' occupies the structural center (fourth of seven) and is the only saying recorded in two gospels

If you look at all of the statements that our Lord uttered when he was upon the cross, and there are seven of them, one of the things you notice is that this statement is the central one of the seven on the cross. The Lord Jesus said, father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. Then second he said, today shalt thou be with me in paradise. Thirdly he said, woman, behold thy son, behold thy mother, as he turned to John. Then the fifth statement was, I thirst. And the sixth, it is finished. And the seventh, father, into thy hands I commit my spirit. The fourth one, the central one, is, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? When you look at these seven statements, you discover another interesting thing about them. Three of them are found in the Gospel of Luke in the 23rd chapter, and three of them are found in the Gospel of John in the 19th chapter. Incidentally, those three are not found in Matthew and Mark. So three are found in Luke and three are found in John. One statement is found in two Gospels. Only one of them is found in two Gospels. It is the statement here, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Which is found in Matthew and Mark.

8 · Johnson uses source-critical reasoning (whether Mark or Matthew was written first) to argue that the earliest gospel prioritized recording this particular cry

If you were sitting in a theological classroom and taking a course on the Introduction to the New Testament, or if you were in a religion class at one of our universities or colleges and studying the introduction to the New Testament or to the Bible, you would probably hear professors say that the first Gospel of the four written was the Gospel of Mark. It is the general opinion of contemporary scholarship that the first Gospel written was the Gospel of Mark. That is the viewpoint of modern scholarship. Ancient believers believed, however, that the Gospel of Matthew was the first, and generally speaking, that is their viewpoint. Although there is some question about the precise meaning of some of the testimonies, it is generally regarded that the early church thought Matthew was the first Gospel written. There are a few scholars who believe that Matthew is the first gospel written. It doesn't really make a bit of difference so far as the point I want to make is concerned. The facts are that the first statement of our Lord from the cross that was written in Christian literature, in the inspired literature, the first of the statements that he made from the cross was this statement, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? In other words, it would appear from this that the early church, the earliest church, thought that of all the seven statements that he did make, perhaps the most important is this very one, because it is found in our earliest Gospel, whether it be Matthew or Mark. It is almost as if they felt that in this statement we have the lesson, the doctrine, the revelation concerning the significance of the death of Jesus Christ. It is my opinion that they were right in this, whether that was their intention or not. It is true that in this statement, My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? We have in truth the lesson of the atoning work of the Lord Jesus when he suffered and died upon the cross.

In other words, in the taking of the animal outside the gate and in the burning of the animal outside the gate, there was an expression of the fact that this individual whom the offerings pointed to was a curse and under divine judgment, and therefore should be put to death outside the city, the place of communion. 12 · Johnson contrasts Jesus's characteristic use of 'Father' (170+ times, including six of the seven cross-sayings) with the singular use of 'My God' in this cry

The final statement that he uttered from the cross was, father, into thy hands I commit my Spirit. He began by using the term Father. He concludes by using the term Father. But in the middle he says, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Let's stop for just a moment and ask ourselves, is it possible that there is some significance in the statement My God, my God. Since in the other six statements twice he has said Father. Well, it's a very interesting thing. If you will take your concordance and look at the New Testament, you will discover that the Lord Jesus, when he refers to God almost 200 times, I really think it's about 170 times. That's a striking thing that might not seem strange to you now because we're so used to calling God Father, but that is something unique. No individual Israelite ever looked up to God and said, my Father. As we read back in chapter 11 and verse 27, I will not refer to that great passage because we spent an entire morning on it. By the way, the term that was used, abba, which became very prominent in Christian circles. And Paul says, the Holy Spirit has been sent into our hearts whereby we cry abba, Father. 13 · Johnson completes the argument: Jesus addressed God as 'Father' in every recorded prayer except this one

Now, the thing I'm getting to is that our Lord Jesus characteristically used the term Father when he spoke to God. He used it many times, 170 times at least. It was a messianic sign. But here, in the midst of his suffering on the cross, he cries out, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Oh, well, of course, he can use God every now and then, can he not? Just for stylistic purposes. Well, did you know that our Lord we have a record of 21 of his prayers in the New Testament? These prayers, some of them are parallel, and I'm using the total figure. There would be about 16 or 17 prayers in all. Of all of the prayers that our Lord uttered to the Father. Only one of them, in only one of them did he ever address God as God in them. In every other petition that he addressed to heaven, he said, father, Father, into thy hands I commit my spirit. Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do. The only time that he ever addressed God as God here, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Why? For the simple reason that when he was hanging upon the cross, the relationship that existed between him and God in heaven was no longer simply paternal, it was judicial. It is not now a son and a father. It is as a human being under the hand of his creating and judging God. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Never forget that it is as a judicial substitute and sacrifice that he addresses God. Here.

14 · Johnson resolves the central question: the cry is not disillusionment but dependence

Now we ask in the beginning, was our Lord's cry a cry of disillusionment? Or was it a cry of dependence? Well, will you look at what he said? He said, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? Even in the midst of this suffering, of the punishment for our sin, in which he was separated from the Father, even in the midst of that, his trust did not fail. It is not, O God, O God, why hast thou forsaken me? It is, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? It is, as Arthur Pink said, a cry of distress, but not a cry of distrust, as Mr. Spurgeon said. It was an instance of man in weakness, but not man in revolt. And the way in which our Lord acted in the midst of his sufferings is so different from the way in which we act when we get into difficulty. We don't address God. We talk to our friends, we complain to them, and we speak of what God has done to us. We address ourselves to the world. We address ourselves to the world and talk about our distress. But he addressed himself to God.

15 · Johnson explains that Jesus's quotation of Psalm 22 was not spontaneous despair but deliberate Messianic self-identification

Incidentally, this is not a thoughtless word that our Lord uttered. That's why I had you read Psalm 22. Our Lord, in the midst of this suffering, reached back into the Old Testament, picked out a specific passage from Psalm 22, which he knew as a Messianic psalm, and took that statement, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? As his own? And then offered it back in prayer to God, recognizing at that very moment that he was the Messiah, and therefore identified completely with the statements that David had written hundreds of years before. As someone has said, he was not in some cosmic darkness, in some deserted no man's land, but he was praying within the congregation of the faithful. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?

16 · Structural pivot from the address ('My God, my God') to the question ('why hast thou forsaken me?')

Now when we come to the interrogation, why hast thou forsaken me? We immediately launch into a discussion of questions concerning the atonement.

17 · Johnson surveys competing atonement theories (moral influence, Christus Victor, Anselmian satisfaction) and critiques them by their terminus: man, Satan, or God

I will pass over the historical interpretations of the atonement. I have made reference to them before. If you have a copy of the Believer's Bible Bulletin, there are a few sentences there concerning the Abelardian or moral influence theory of the atonement preached so often in our Christian churches today, which, while having a truth and expressing a truth, is so fundamentally limiting in the understanding of the atonement of Jesus Christ, and is, if it is the only understanding of the atonement that we have, sub Christian, a person who preaches only a moral influence or exemplary theory. The Socinian view of the atonement is preaching A theory of the atonement that is not Christian. There is the so called classic theory of the atonement which was so prominent probably in the writings of some of the early church fathers like Irenaeus. And then there was the anselmic view of the penalty of the satisfaction of God's honor. These theories of the atonement are interesting because the moral influence theory of the atonement terminates on man. The classic theory of the atonement terminates on Satan. It is the penal substitutionary theory that terminates upon God. While truth is found in each of these theories, if we are to have a theory of the atonement that is satisfactory, we must include the ideas that our law died as a substitute that he died bearing the penalty for our sin. The statement says, why hast thou forsaken me?

18 · Johnson explains why Jesus, as a divine person, would ask 'why?' — because in his human nature he must enter fully into human experience to be perfected as High Priest

One might ask the question, why did the Lord Jesus have to ask a question like that? Our Lord was a divine person who possessed a human nature. And in his human nature it was necessary for him to have all of the legitimate human experiences. And so consequently he, as he was hanging upon the cross, had to, in his human nature ask all of the legitimate questions that a human nature might ask apart from sin. And this question is a question, a cry for information. It is a cry for more information. Though as a divine person he fully understood all of this and had actually participated in the plans of it in the ages before our worlds ever began. But as a human being, he must enter into the experience of it in order that he might be perfected as our High priest. The writer of the Epistle of the Hebrews, explains all of that in his marvelous exposition of the work of Christ as our High priest. Incidentally, when our Lord said, why hast thou forsaken me? It was evident that he was consciously innocent of having given in himself any ground of why God should forsake him. I would never pray anything like this, why hast thou forsaken me? I could think of 10,000 reasons why God would forsake me. But he said, why hast thou forsaken me? And in that very cry expresses the fact that he does not know any basis himself for the forsaking of God.

19 · Johnson builds a cumulative case for substitution through rhetorical questions

Now we come to what it means when he says, why hast thou forsaken me? I would like to suggest that there are some questions that I may rhetorically pass on to you that indicate that substitution is the only explanation of the question that our Lord asked. Let me ask you. Would a loving God forsake the only good man who ever lived? David said, I have been young. Now I am old yet never have I seen the righteous forsaken, nor his seed begging bread. But our Lord Jesus was forsaken, the Father said, with reference to him in him I am well pleased. Then would a loving God forsake the only good man who ever lived? No, not for his own sins or on account of his own goodness. That very fact indicates to us that there must be something else in this forsaking. Would a loving God injure the only innocent man who ever lived? Would he allow him to be? I can understand why he might allow others who are not innocent to be injured, but in his case, no. Why were our prayers always answered elsewhere? But it seems, if the psalmist is correct, that this petition is a petition that has no answer but the suffering of eternal judgment. I cry unto thee in the daytime and in the night seasons. I am not silent, and thou hearest not. The psalmist said, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? No answer comes. The only explanation of this is that our Lord Jesus is dying as a substitute for others. Not for his own sin, but for others. He is being forsaken for others sake. It is true that God is forsaking him, but it is not on account of anything in him. It is on account of others. Substitution can only explain it. As one of my teachers in Scotland used to say, the heart of the atonement is simply Jesus Christ stood in the stead of others.

20 · Johnson marshals and refutes an objection from George Buttrick, who called the God of penal substitution 'my devil

Now it seems to me that it is very strange in the light of this that many of our modern theologians seek to avoid the implications of the Cross of Christ. A great Presbyterian leader has said, if God dealt with him as if he were a sinner and the greatest sinner, then we must say of God, as a cynical Frenchman did say of the God of these penal theologies, your God is my devil. But I must say I prefer to follow our Lord Jesus and the apostle Paul and the other apostles than George Buttrick. The Bible, it seems to me, makes very plain that our Lord died as a substitute, and he died under the judgment of God. Penal substitution. It is an offense. It is an offense to be told that Jesus Christ bears the penalty of others sins. It is an offense because it tells us that we should die, and that is an offense to us. We do not like to be told ye must be born again. We do not like to be told that someone must die in our stead. We do not like to be told we are under divine judgment. We do not even like to mention the word sin in the 20th century. It is a bad word. It is too true. We cannot take it. But the Bible says very plainly we are sinners, we are guilty, we are under divine judgment.

21 · Johnson connects the cry from the cross back to Gethsemane, showing narrative-theological continuity

The cross and this statement especially is the inevitable sequel to Gethsemane. We studied a few weeks back our Lord in the garden of Gethsemane, and there he was, writhing upon the ground, crying out, o my God, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me. The inevitable sequel of that is this cry which has in itself a kind of ruthless my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? That's what he was groveling on the ground about in Gethsemane. He knew what faced him here.

22 · Johnson establishes the necessity of substitution by appealing to the law's demand for perfect love and the universal human failure to meet it

Is substitution necessary? Substitution is necessary for these simple reasons. The law says that a man must love the Lord his God with all his heart, with all his soul, with all his mind, and his neighbor as himself. Thou shalt do you know, my dear friend, sitting in the audience, that you will never get to heaven unless you have perfectly kept that law. Have you done it? Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart. Don't hold up your hand. If we were to ask the apostles to stand on this platform and the prophets and all of the other great saints of the Bible to explain to us how they kept the law perfectly, they would all shout in thundering tones, we have not kept the law, the law perfectly. We have sinned. Abraham, I am but dust and ashes. Job, I had heard of thee by the hearing of the ear, but now mine eye seeth thee, wherefore I repent, abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes. Daniel, my comeliness after I have seen thee is turned into corruption. Isaiah I am a man of wicked and unclean life and lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people that are similar. And all of the others down through the years, they've all confessed it. That's why substitution is necessary.

23 · Johnson returns to Psalm 22:3 to ground the necessity of substitution in God's holiness

Did you notice the third verse of the Psalm after he had said, I cry unto thee in the daytime and in the night seasons. I'm not silent. Thou hearest not, the text goes on to say, but thou art holy. In other words, it is the holiness and righteousness of God that condemns us. For we must have a holiness and righteousness that is satisfactory to this holy and righteous God. We cannot present it to him apart from our Lord's saving work.

24 · Johnson completes the substitutionary logic: the law demands perfect obedience or death

The law says, you either keep the law perfectly, which we have not done and cannot do, or you pay the penalty, and the penalty is eternal death. And if we therefore pay the penalty we are lost forever. There is no hope of our having any relationship to God. We have broken the law. We cannot pay the penalty. We need a substitute in whom we may pay the penalty. That is what God has given us. That's the gospel. He has given us a substitute, a surety, a redeemer, in whom we have paid the penalty. His people. He came to die for his people. He came to save his people from their sins. And when he suffered, he paid that penalty. Therefore, since he has paid it, there is no way in which heaven can demand additional payment. Heaven is satisfied. The holiness and righteousness of God is satisfied. Toplady is right. Payment. God cannot twice demand. First from my bleeding surety's hand, and then again at mine. I have paid the penalty in my substitute. That's why, incidentally, the Lord Jesus came to die for his people, for he paid a penalty. He was a true substitute. He really paid it. Therefore it would be unjust for heaven to demand further payment. Don't you see that? Isn't it wonderful? Isn't it wonderful to have a substitute in whom I have paid the penalty?

25 · Johnson identifies what was happening at the moment of the cry: Jesus was becoming the curse, becoming sin

That is what is happening when our Lord Jesus cries out, my God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? For he is becoming at that point, the curse. The darkness points that out. He is the curse. Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law being made a curse for us. He hath made him to be sin for us who knew no sin that we might become the righteousness of God in him. A curse for us, Sin for us. My God, my God, why hast Thou forsaken me? That's why, as a result of that, we say, as we so often do, he will never leave us nor forsake us.

26 · Johnson identifies an eschatological implication: the darkness and the cry reveal what hell is — eternal separation from God

Well, I wish I had time to expatiate upon this, but we don't. It is a significant fact that our Lord, the light of the world, died in darkness. It is almost as if the light went out. There is a solemn warning, I think, in this. We have often wondered what it is. What is it to be in hell? This gives us a kind of clue. One of the things hell is, is infinite and eternal separation from God. The apostle Paul writes about the fact that when he shall come in his second coming, he shall mete out everlasting destruction from the presence of the Lord. That's what we have here. My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?

27 · Johnson narrates a personal moment of exegetical insight — while studying on a flight, he realized Jesus cried with a loud voice not for his own sake but for the sake of witnesses at the cross

Many years ago, I was coming back from Charleston, South Carolina, my home, and I was giving a series on the seven last words of our Lord. I was to give a message on Sunday, and this was Saturday. On this very text I had my Greek Testament with me. And I was meditating on the Greek text. As I left Atlanta on Delta Airlines and made my way back toward Dallas. I remember still sitting in my seat with the Greek Testament before me, just looking at the various words in the text and thinking about the message that I would be giving. I was impressed as I was trying to look at this text by the words Jesus cried with a loud voice. That seemed rather strange. Why did he cry with a loud voice? Why was that necessary in the first place? As I meditated upon finally came to me that it is possible Our Lord cried with a loud voice. Voice not for Himself at all, but rather for those around the cross. In other words, it was something that he uttered in the terror and the anguish of that moment, but also since he was in full control of his faculties with a view to others understanding what was happening there. So when he cried out with a loud voice, my God, my God, why hast Thou forsaken me? It was not primarily for him. It was for them around the cross that they might have this illuminating word concerning the sufferings that he was undergoing and what they really meant. I am convinced that's why we have Jesus cried with a loud voice. I cannot prove that. I think my studies of the death of Christ go back many, many years, as probably most Christians do.

28 · Johnson illustrates the doctrine of substitution with a missionary story from Henry Mabie

There was a man by the name of Henry Mabie, who was a missionary statesman who has written several books on the cross. They are simple books, but nevertheless, at one time they were very good books as far as I was concerned. I remember a story that he told of the preaching of the Gospel in a tribe. For the first time. The missionary repeated the story of the cross. And as he finished, the chief of the tribe asked him to go over it again. So he repeated it again. Finally, when he came to the story of the cross again and he was unfolding that the chief stood up and interrupted him and said, hold on, hold on. Take Jesus Christ down from the cross. Take him down, I say. Jesus Christ doesn't belong on the cross. I belong on the cross. And the Holy Spirit has illuminated his heart so that he understood that it really was someone dying in the stead of others in perfect love. He dies for me. He dies for me. O all atoning sacrifice I cling by faith to Thee.

29 · Brief exhortation following the missionary illustration

May God help us to respond in that way.

30 · Closing prayer recapitulating the sermon's main points: we cannot exhaust the mystery of the cross, but we know God laid our iniquity on Christ

Let's stand for the benediction, Father. We know that we shall never exhaust shall only touch the fringes of the great truth of the death of our Lord Jesus Christ. We thank thee, Lord, however, that the Scriptures do tell us that thou hast laid our iniquity upon him. What a heavy burden. And we praise thee that our penalty is paid in our substitute and we are free. Lord, we thank thee. We worship thee. We pray that if there are some here who have not believed in our Lord Jesus Christ, O God, touch their hearts and minds. Bring them to faith in him whom to know is life eternal. For Jesus sake. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we follow the arc of Christ's substitutionary suffering: from the scandal of divine abandonment, through the shift from paternal to judicial relationship, to the depths of what he bore in our place, and finally to the unbroken trust that sustains us.

Monday Isaiah 53:6

Isaiah's vision of the Servant bearing 'the iniquity of us all' establishes the theological necessity of substitution: we have turned to our own way, yet the Lord laid on him the transgressions of all of us. This is not punishment for his sin but for ours, and it reveals why the cross cannot be understood apart from substitutionary atonement. In the cry from the cross, Jesus experiences what Isaiah prophesied—he is forsaken not for his own guilt but as the bearer of ours.

Tuesday Psalm 22:1-3

The psalmist cries out in desolation yet continues, 'But thou art holy,' demonstrating that abandonment and trust coexist in genuine faith. When Jesus speaks the opening of Psalm 22, he inhabits this very posture—not disillusionment but the raw expression of bearing what we deserved. The possession 'my God' even in forsaking reveals that his dependence on God remained absolute, and we are invited to find in his trust the foundation of our own when we stand helpless before judgment.

Wednesday Leviticus (sin offering outside the gate)

The Old Testament sin offering was slain outside the camp, separated from the covenant community, to bear away defilement. Jesus fulfilled this type absolutely—he was forsaken outside the gate, cut off from communion with the Father, taking upon himself the full weight of the law's curse. The darkness at noon was not metaphorical but the judicial reality of God's wrath meeting him as our substitute, transforming the cross from tragedy into the very means by which we are restored to God.

Thursday Gethsemane accounts (Matthew 26)

In the garden, Jesus agonized, 'Let this cup pass from me,' yet submitted to his Father's will. The cup he dreaded was not physical suffering but judicial abandonment—the cessation of the Father's presence and the experience of bearing divine judgment. When he cried out on the cross, he was drinking that cup to its dregs, not in disillusionment but in faithful obedience to the Father's redemptive will. His willingness to drink it reveals the depth of his love for us and secures our redemption.

Friday Galatians 6:14

Paul boasts in nothing but the cross of Christ because there alone is our glory found—not in our righteousness but in Christ's substitution for us. As Jesus bore what we deserved, the debt is paid, judgment is satisfied, and we stand justified before a holy God. We are compelled to glory in the cross precisely because it reveals the magnitude of our need and the sufficiency of Christ's sacrifice, making us debtors to grace and free to love Him with all our hearts.
