Barabbas – The Man for Whom Christ Died

Matthew 27:15-21 January 4, 1980 S. Lewis Johnson
Thesis Barabbas, the notorious criminal freed in place of Jesus, serves as a vivid physical illustration of the penal substitutionary atonement whereby Christ died in the place of guilty sinners, bearing their penalty and securing their righteousness before God.

Theological claims· 6
  1. Israel's preference for Barabbas over Jesus reveals the human tendency to glamorize revolutionary violence while rejecting the one who exposes sin and calls for repentance. unit #16
  2. Barabbas's hopeless condition as a condemned criminal awaiting execution is a picture of the spiritual state of every sinner under the judgment of God. unit #22
  3. Barabbas's experience of being delivered while another took his place on the cross gave him an experiential understanding of substitutionary atonement. unit #23
  4. Believers in Christ have borne their penalty in their substitute, and therefore cannot bear it again—the divine law has no case against those whose penalty has been paid in Christ. unit #25
  5. The Barabbas account illustrates not only penal substitution but also justification—Barabbas stood righteous before Roman authority because Christ bore his judgment, just as believers stand righteous before God through the imputation of Christ's righteousness. unit #26
  6. The multitude's mad choice of Barabbas over Jesus is the same choice every person makes when they prioritize anything—business, family, pleasure—over the claims of Christ. unit #27

Of course, we are talking about the physical as an illustration of the spiritual. But think for A moment about the condition of Barabbas. He was a man whose condition was hopeless. The wages of sin is death, the Bible says, but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ, our Lord. As far as Barabbas was concerned, he had no future at all. He was a murderer, a seditionist. The Romans were going to put him to death by crucifixion. All of the hours before his release, he had no doubt been thinking about what was going to be his future. Nothing. I am told, incidentally, that people who are to be executed cannot escape from thinking about what faces them. For example, people who are going to be hanged. It is the testimony of those who have observed them that they cannot keep their hands away from their throats. If a person is going to be gassed, electrocuted by gassing, they practice holding their breath. In fact, practice holding their breath so long that their eyes almost pop out of their head because they know that they have few breaths left. Now, Barabbas was thinking about the Roman judgment of crucifixion, and no doubt his thoughts were concentrated upon that. He was a man who was a sinner. He was guilty and he stood condemned. A beautiful picture, of course, of all of us, for that is what we are. We are sinners. We are guilty before God, and we are therefore condemned.

23 · Johnson advances from condemnation to redemption, arguing that Barabbas became an unwitting theologian through his experience of having Christ die in his place

But Barabbas had a release. We read in the New Testament, in the epistle that Paul wrote to the Galatians, christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law being made a curse for us. I am sure that it did not take a great deal of thought for Barabbas to have a pretty good theory of the atonement. C.I. schofield once said, it is easy to see. Barabbas need not have been a theologian to form a good working theory of the atonement. He knew from experience what it was to be delivered and to have someone else take his place.

24 · Johnson reconstructs Barabbas's experience of release in vivid narrative detail, imagining the sounds and sights that led to his freedom and possibly his witnessing the crucifixion

For you see, ultimately, the Lord Jesus is the one who hangs upon that middle cross in the place of what Barabbas thought would be his cross. We might reconstruct this. I'm not sure exactly how this did happen, where the Roman prison was, with a view to the place where our Lord was crucified. We know from what we know now of that area that it might have been possible for one of the prisoners to look out across to the mountain, the Mount Golgotha, where the Romans did their work of execution. It might have been possible for him to look out and see preparations made for the three men who were Going to suffer death. It certainly would have been, Barabbas thought that he would be one of those men. On the day of the crucifixion, as the jailers came to take the prisoners out, it might well have been his experience that they came. And he heard the sound of the tramping of the soldiers. He heard the clanging of the chains. He heard the lock inserted into the cell, and he heard it opened and one of the prisoners go out. We, of course, do not know all about this account. It is possible that he might have seen one go out to the mount and there by the cross. And he might have seen the same thing happen for the second and still for the third, thinking that as he heard the sounds, it was this time for him. But instead, as the door was swinging open, he heard a strange voice from the Roman soldiers. Barabbas, you are free. And then learned that it was by virtue of the multitude's choice that he himself had obtained redemption from the death that he was to d. So he had a kind of theory of the atonement that put in the words that we might put it in would be a good statement of the atonement. The Messiah, Jesus, who is called the Christ, died for me in my place. In other words, the cross on which our lord Jesus was hanging was the cross ultimately that Barabbas should have been hanging upon. It is even possible that he went out to that mountain and took a look, for surely he would have been greatly impressed by what happened to himself. And if he had gone out there with that multitude, and as he gazed upon our lord Jesus, he could truly say, in the physical sense, that man died for me. That man is the one in whom, as my substitute, I have borne the penalty because he bears the penalty that I should have borne.

25 · Johnson makes the central theological claim of the sermon explicit: in our substitute, believers have borne their penalty, meaning they cannot bear it again

One of the things that we stress in the atonement that our lord Jesus has accomplished is that in our substitute, in the lord Jesus we have borne our penalty. That's why we shall not have to bear the penalty again. He has borne the penalty for sinners. And in the case of those who have believed in him, they have a substitute in whom they have borne the penalty. That's why we cannot bear the penalty. That's why the divine law has no case whatsoever against us because our penalty has been paid in our substitute. So Barabbas beautifully illustrates that. I don't guess there's anybody who could sing with more gusto. He breaks the power of canceled sin. He sets the prisoner free, transferring that from the physical to the spiritual sense. We have an Illustration of the atoning work of our Lord Jesus. He was our penal substitute.

26 · Johnson extends the substitutionary atonement doctrine to include justification: Barabbas not only escaped penalty but stood righteous before Rome because Jesus bore his judgment

Then, if I may just emphasize one more thing, Barabbas, by virtue of the fact that he had been freed by the authorities, and by virtue of the fact that Jesus had died under the judgment of Rome, Barabbas is a man who now stands righteous before the Roman government. O mysterious, wonderful exchange, the ancient fathers used to say, by which my unrighteousness becomes the unrighteousness of the Savior and the righteousness of the Savior becomes my righteousness. O mysterious, wonderful exchange. He hath made him to be sin for us, him who knew no sin, that we might become the righteousness of God in him. Barabbas, as he stood looking at the cross, stood looking as a free man, one who had been delivered by the authorities and now stood right before them.

27 · Johnson shifts from exposition to confrontation, universalizing the crowd's choice

The second thing, I think, that is so important is to notice the perennial madness of the multitude's choice. Which of the two will ye that I release unto you? They said, Barabbas. And to make it even worse, in a moment, after Pilate has sought to free himself from guilt, they have answered and said to Pilate, his blood be on us and on our children. Oh, the madness of the choice of the multitude that selects a Barabbas instead of a Jesus. But don't be too critical. It's that same kind of choice that we have made until the day that by the grace of God, we came to Jesus Christ as our own savior. When a man decides that he will put his business over the claims of Christ or his family over the claims of Christ or his pleasure over the claims of Christ, he is guilty of the same kind of decision that prefers of Barabbas to the true son of the Father, the Lord Jesus Christ.

28 · Johnson uses George Tynworth's artwork as a visual illustration of the sermon's thesis: the crowd celebrates the wrong savior while a lone woman with a child represents the faithful remnant who recognize Jesus

George Tynworth, in one of his terracottas, has a beautiful master touch. He has pictured Pilate seated on his judgment throne. He has pictured two doors, and out of one has come Barabbas. And the crowd is gathered around him. The Roman soldiers are extending hands of congratulations to him rudely, coarsely congratulating him on the good fortune that has happened to him. And the crowd of his friends among the insurgents is patting him on the back. And all of that is going on on one side of Mr. Tynworth's work. But on the other side, there is the Lord Jesus coming out in chains, being led out by a Roman soldier. But there is a woman with a little child in her arms over on the side, and she's looking on our Lord with the compassion and love of Someone who has come into relationship with him. It's a beautiful picture of the madness of the multitude's choice and I think also the madness of the majority's choice, because it is that way.

29 · Johnson issues a direct evangelistic appeal, placing the listener in Pilate's position: which Jesus will you choose? He contrasts what Barabbas cannot do (cleanse, heal, forgive, justify) with what Christ can and will do (cleanse, forgive, justify, sustain, perfect)

May I close by saying, there is a need for a decision and a need always for a decision. Now. Now is the accepted time. Now is the day of salvation. The Apostle Paul has said, there stand before us the two men, Barabbas, who is the son of the Father, and Jesus, who is the true Son of the Father, who is the Christ. But this Barabbas will never cleanse you. He will never heal you. He will never be able to give you forgiveness of sins. He will never enable you to stand justified before God. He will never be able to meet the trials of life by the comforting, strengthening power of the Holy Spirit. On the other hand, there is the Lord Jesus Christ, who has offered an atonement for sinners. And he is able to cleanse you, he is able to forgive you, he is able to give you justification of life. And he doesn't stop there, but he goes on and gives us all that we need throughout our Christian lives and ultimately ushers us into his presence, perfects us, and uses us for his glory throughout all eternity. Eternity. The two men stand before you, Jesus, Barabbas and Jesus, who is called the Christ. We don't have to talk about the simplicity of the answer that we should give. What do you do when you're sick and someone comes in and says, I have the medicine that is designed specifically for the thing that you have? What do you do? You trust the medicine, you take it. Or if there is a blind man unable to get around and someone comes and says, I have someone or something that will be a guide for you. What do you do as a blind person? You trust the guide. You see, the answer is trust.

30 · Johnson delivers concrete evangelistic instruction: acknowledge sin, acknowledge Christ's atoning sacrifice, receive the benefits as a free gift

We trust the Savior I set before you then Jesus, Barabbas and Jesus, who is the Christ. May God by His wonderful grace, enable you to put your trust in him who is able to save to the uttermost the Lord Jesus Christ. If you're here this morning and you've never believed, we encourage you to put your trust in him, to abandon all of these other trusts, abandon all of these other choices which were wrong, and make the one decision that brings to life, by the grace of God, faith in Jesus Christ. It's very simple. All one need do is within his heart, acknowledge before God his own sinful condition. Acknowledge that the Son of God has offered the atoning sacrifice and receive the benefits of it. As a free gift. It's that simple. But it's that significant. May God work in your heart to that end.

31 · Johnson closes with a pastoral prayer for deliverance from flesh, world, and devil, for the Spirit's enabling work in repentance, and with an evangelistic plea that God would give no rest to the unconverted until they come to Christ

Let's stand firm for the benediction. Father, we are so grateful to Thee for these wonderful accounts in the Gospels which almost in each text challenge us to give ourselves over to a loving triune God. Deliver us, Lord, from the pulls and the stresses of the flesh, the world and the devil. Enable us by thy grace, through the ministry of the Holy Spirit, to repent and to turn to our Lord Jesus to receive life. O Father, if there is some one person here who has not yet come to Christ, give them no rest nor peace until they do. May, O God this day, the first day of the new year, be the day of new life for some. Thou knowest, Lord, how often we have prayed. O God, give us souls. Give us souls. For the glory of the Son of God we do pray. May grace, mercy and peace be with us for Jesus sake. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the gospel reality of substitutionary atonement through Barabbas's story: from the sinner's desperate condition, through Christ's substitutionary death, to the believer's righteous standing before God.

Monday Romans 6:23

Paul's declaration that 'the wages of sin is death' names the reality Barabbas faced in his cell—not as an outlaw alone, but as every sinner stands before God's throne. We, like Barabbas, deserve execution; our sin has earned us a debt we cannot pay. Yet this very extremity is where grace becomes intelligible: we must grasp our true condition before we can receive the gift of a substitute.

Tuesday 2 Corinthians 5:21

Here Paul names the transaction that Barabbas alone witnessed in flesh: Christ 'became sin' on behalf of the sinner, so that we 'might become the righteousness of God' in him. Barabbas walked free not because he deserved it, but because another bore his penalty. In the same way, we stand righteous before God not by our works, but because Christ has transferred to us his perfect obedience.

Wednesday Galatians 3:13

Paul teaches that Christ became a curse on our behalf, redeeming us from the law's condemnation. When the court declared Barabbas free, it had no further claim against him—the penalty was satisfied. So too, because Christ has exhausted God's wrath against sin, the Father's justice can bring no charge against the believer. We are permanently acquitted.

Thursday 2 Corinthians 6:2

Paul's urgency—'now is the day of salvation'—echoes the choice Jerusalem faced: Barabbas or Jesus. The crowd's preference for a revolutionary who promised earthly freedom over the Messiah who offers forgiveness reveals the human heart's tendency to embrace false saviors. Today, we stand in that same moment, choosing whether to surrender business, family, or pleasure to receive the one who alone cleanses and justifies.

Friday Acts 3

Peter's proclamation to Jerusalem repeats the apostolic testimony: Jesus, whom you rejected, is the Christ who saves. The same city that chose Barabbas now hears the call to repent and receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. As we hear these words, we too are summoned to the same decisive moment—to acknowledge our sin, embrace Christ's substitution, and receive the justification and eternal life only he can give. Grace makes the choice simple; our response makes it real.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What do the details Matthew gives us about Barabbas in verses 16-17 tell us about the kind of man the crowd chose to release? What does their choice reveal about what they valued or desired in that moment?
    Matthew 27:16-17
    → How might the crowd's preference for a violent revolutionary over Jesus reflect the same spiritual condition we struggle with today—the tendency to trust in earthly solutions rather than the Savior?
  2. Barabbas was condemned to death for his crimes and awaiting execution when Pilate offered the crowd a choice. What was his actual condition—not just legally, but spiritually—before he was released? How does the sermon present this as a picture of every sinner's condition before Christ?
    Matthew 27:15-16
  3. The sermon argues that Barabbas's release while Jesus took his place is 'a vivid physical illustration of the penal substitutionary atonement.' What does it mean that Christ died in our place, bearing our penalty? How is Barabbas's experience unique in this regard?
    Galatians 3:13
    → When you say 'Christ bore my penalty,' what exactly was that penalty, and how does knowing it was already paid change the way you approach God?
  4. Read 2 Corinthians 5:21 together. The sermon connects Barabbas's justification before Roman authority—he stands righteous because another bore his judgment—to our justification before God. How are these two ideas the same, and why does it matter that we grasp the connection?
    2 Corinthians 5:21
  5.     2 Corinthians 6:2

The Man Who Got Away

For the parent

This prompt anchors in the historical fact that Barabbas walked free while Jesus went to the cross. Help your family grasp that Barabbas is not just ancient history—he's a picture of what happened for us. Listen for whether your kids can connect his release to their own need for rescue.
