18 · Through vivid personal testimony of examining Edwards' interleaved Bible at Yale, Piper illustrates Edwards' extraordinary commitment to studying Scripture itself, having manually created a study Bible to record notes on every passage

He had taken a Bible, cut every page out himself, taken a blank piece of paper, inserted it between every page and drawn a line down the middle of that blank page on both sides and sewn it himself back together. In fact, if you take this second volume here, there are about 180 double column pages called Notes on the Bible that just go from one passage after the other as he wrote down his thoughts about the Bible.

28 · Piper identifies Edwards' key practice of reading with pen in hand as the means to train oneself in disciplined thinking, quoting Dwight's description of how this practice molded Edwards into a thinking being

Even while a boy, he began to study with his pen in his hand, not for the purpose of copying off the thoughts of others, but for the purpose of writing down and preserving the thoughts suggested to his own mind. This most useful practice he steadily pursued in all his studies through life. His pen appears to have been always in his hand. From this practice, he derived the very great advantages of thinking continually during each period of study, of thinking accurately, of thinking connectedly, of thinking habitually at all times, of pursuing each given subject of thought as far as he was able, of preserving his best thoughts, associations, and images, and then arranging them under their proper heads, ready for subsequent use, of regularly strengthening the faculty of thinking and reasoning by constant and powerful exercise, and above all, of gradually molding himself into a thinking being.

40 · Piper establishes the tragedy that Edwards' integration of rigorous thought with passionate affection has not been replicated, calling for recovery of 'logic on fire'—the marriage of reason and emotion, study and worship

And here he is wonderfully exemplary for those of us who have a bent to study. Mark Knoll, you remember, said Edward's piety continued on in the revivalist tradition. His theology continued on in academic Calvinism. But there were no successors to his God-entranced world view. 41 · Piper establishes that Edwards did not view passion for God as optional add-on to Christianity but as essential to saving faith itself, which requires a sense of God beyond what reason alone can provide

Edwards pursued a passion for God. He was as intent on this pursuit as he was on all the other pursuits. But he did not pursue a passion for God because it was icing on the cake of Christianity, of decision, of commitment. For him, faith, saving faith, was grounded in a sense of God which reason alone could not mediate.

42 · Through Edwards' sermon, Piper establishes the crucial distinction between knowing theological propositions about God and having a spiritual sense of His beauty—arguing that only the latter has spiritual value and differs from demonic knowledge

Let me read you a key quote from a sermon entitled Blessed are the pure in heart for they shall see God. A true sense of the glory of God is that which can never be obtained by speculative reasoning. And if men convince themselves by argument that God is holy, that never will give a sense of His amiable and glorious holiness. If they argue that He is very merciful, that will not give a sense of His glorious grace and mercy. It must be a more immediate, sensible, that word doesn't mean reasonable, it means sensed, felt, sensible discovery that must give the mind a real sense of the excellency and beauty of God. In other words, it is to no avail merely to find out and believe that God is holy. That is of no spiritual avail. That belief to have any saving value must have in it a sense, a spiritual sense of the beauty and delightfulness, he said, amiableness of God's holiness and mercy. We must have a true delight in what we believe is true for it to have any spiritual value. Otherwise, our knowledge is no different from the devil's.

And if you discover more than Aptet, just call me up, Greg, and tell me, because I want to know more how to walk by the Holy Spirit. I want to know how to live in another person's strength. It's a great mystery to me, and I'm still learning.

62 · In response to a question about whether the Spirit must work through the intellect, Piper argues that while God could bypass mental processes, He normally doesn't because unreflective action would be robotic and virtuous only in a mechanical sense

You want to follow up on it? Okay, that's true. How does the intellect relate to walking by obedience and holiness, and specifically the influence of the Holy Spirit in our lives? Does the Holy Spirit and His work have to go through the intellect in order to reach the will in the heart, or can He short-circuit that process? That's my prejudicial description. He can. He can, I suppose, but it isn't His practice, and it shouldn't be sought, because there is no virtue in doing that for which you have no spiritual ground. That would be an automaton. That would be a robot. To act unreflectively, to have no understanding about God that informs your virtue would mean that you are acting like a sleepwalker, like twitches, like ticks in your life. So, if God were to make a practice of circumventing our thought process, He would turn us into robots. And so, I think Edwards is right, and virtually all Christians would say, I think, that God normally, and this should always be pursued, guides us and inspires us and leads us through a mental handling of spiritual truth.

63 · Piper clarifies that non-intellectual people still think (though less articulately), citing Edwards' concept of a spiritual nature in simple believers that guides them toward righteousness even when they cannot articulate their reasoning, so long as biblical principles have informed their thinking over time

Now, the reason I said mental handling is because the difference between a non-intellectual and an intellectual is not that one doesn't think and one thinks, but that one likes to think and thinks better, and the other one doesn't like to think and may think worse. But he must think. He must have a reflective process when a person is doing something and you ask them why to do it. Edwards has a long section in the Religious Affections on a spiritual nature that is developed in simple saints that guides them like an aroma. They're just guided towards righteousness, like this. And if you ask them, explain, explain to me all of the principles and reasons for why you're acting this way, they would have such a hard time explaining. I mean, why... If you took an ordinary, simple, young fella, or I don't know who, I don't want to sound... Any ordinary person and said, give me reasons why you think homosexuality is wrong. And they would say, well, just... It's not right. It's just... They would have a hard time putting into words, perhaps. They might say, the Bible says so. That would be all right. And so, I do believe that people do have mental processes going on that yield righteous behavior that are very hard to articulate for them sometimes. And these mental processes have been informed by biblical principles over the years. And to the degree that they haven't, to that degree is this virtue probably not spiritual.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace Edwards' recovery of God-entranced vision through five practices: the primacy of knowing God Himself, the discipline of active Scripture reading, the redemption of time through rigorous study, the integration of theology with passionate affection, and the grace-empowered obedience that makes such disciplined pursuit not legalism but joy.

Monday Isaiah 6:1-4

Isaiah saw the Lord sitting on His throne, high and lifted up, and the seraphim cry, 'Holy, holy, holy!' This is the vision Edwards possessed—God in His majesty, glory, and supremacy—and it must captivate the pastor first before it can kindle that same entrancement in the congregation. When we glimpse God's exceeding greatness as Edwards did, every other pursuit loses its pull, and we become single-minded in our devotion to Him.

Tuesday Psalm 119

The psalmist's refrain—'I will delight in your statutes' and 'your word is a lamp to my feet'—expresses the unified, passionate orientation toward God's truth that Edwards modeled. This is not academic detachment but the soul's militant focus on knowing and obeying the living God, resisting the diversions that pull us into spiritual lethargy.

Wednesday 2 Timothy 2:4

Paul reminds us that a soldier on active duty does not entangle himself in the affairs of civilian life; his loyalty is undivided. Edwards understood this warrior's mentality—that the pastor's work is a sacred struggle requiring uncompromised focus. The disciplines of study, prayer, and theological labor are not optional refinements but essential weapons in the spiritual conflict we wage for our people's souls.

Thursday John 15:7

When we abide in Christ and His words abide in us, our prayers are answered and our desires align with His. Edwards exemplified this union: his rigorous study of Scripture and doctrine flowed from and fed back into intimate communion with Christ, never separating intellect from love. True theology, rightly pursued, draws us deeper into relational intimacy with God rather than away from it.

Friday 1 Corinthians 15:10

Paul declares, 'By the grace of God I am what I am,' yet adds, 'I labored more abundantly than they all'—grace and labor are inseparable. Edwards' unrelenting pursuit of theological excellence, his reading with pen in hand, his redemption of every moment—these were not human willpower but grace at work, the Spirit's enabling power moving him to gladness in the very disciplines that shape a pastor who feeds his flock well.

Sunday-evening family table

What Makes a Pastor's Heart Matter Most

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to think about what a pastor—or any leader—really needs to be spending time on. The goal is to help kids see that being "full of God" matters more than being busy with tasks, and that adults need to guard their hearts the same way they guard their time.

John Piper talked about Jonathan Edwards today—a pastor who spent so much time studying God and the Bible that he was absolutely amazed by who God is. He says churches get sick when their pastors get distracted and busy with other things instead of staying amazed by God. If you were a pastor, what do you think would be the hardest thing about keeping your heart focused on God instead of getting pulled into a hundred other activities? And what could help you stay focused?
Works for ages 9+. Younger kids (6–8) can listen as parents explain and share their own answers first.
