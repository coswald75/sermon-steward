The title of the message this morning is True Greatness. True Greatness. And I have three points to bring to your attention from this text.

Greatness desired. Greatness defined. And greatness demonstrated.

Sunday-evening family table

Who Do You Want to Become?

For the parent

This prompt invites kids to surface their own hidden ambitions—what they secretly hope people will say about them—and opens a conversation about why Jesus flipped the script on greatness entirely. Listen for where their dreams land, then gently help them see how Christ's example of serving others rewires what 'great' actually means.

If people could say anything about you when you grow up, what would make you feel really proud or happy? (After they answer:) Jesus said the greatest people aren't the ones everyone talks about or admires—they're the ones who serve others. Why do you think He said that instead of telling people to try to be famous or important?
Works for ages 7+; younger children can listen and answer the first part with help; older kids and teens will engage the second question with theological depth
Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Mark 10:35-38, James and John ask Jesus for positions of honor at His right and left hand. What does their request reveal about what they believed true greatness to be, and how does the timing of this request—immediately after Jesus announced His death—expose the depth of their misunderstanding?
    Mark 10:35-38
    → How do you see this same pattern of selfish ambition showing up in your own prayers or desires, especially when you're anxious or uncertain about the future?
  2. Jesus responds in Mark 10:43 by saying that 'whoever wants to become great among you must be your servant.' How does this definition of greatness fundamentally contradict the cultural standards of the Roman world—and our own world—regarding power, status, and recognition?
    Mark 10:43
  3. According to the sermon, Jesus's redefinition of greatness as servanthood is not merely a behavioral instruction but is grounded in His substitutionary death as 'a ransom for many' (Mark 10:45). What is the connection between Christ's sacrifice and our capacity to serve others humbly rather than for personal gain?
    Mark 10:45
    → How does understanding that Christ died to ransom us from sin's captivity change the way you approach serving someone who irritates or frustrates you?
  4. The sermon notes that the disciples' selfish ambition mirrors an ongoing tendency in every believer's prayer life. What does it look like to repent of approaching God with self-centered requests, and how might submission to God's will reshape what we ask Him for?
    Mark 9:31
  5. How would you describe the difference between serving others as a means of earning approval or recognition versus serving as a grace-enabled response to the gospel? What makes that difference practically visible in the way you serve?
  6. Christ's servanthood culminated in His death on the cross, the ultimate act of 'being served' turned upside down. As you look at this week ahead, where is God calling you to imitate His servanthood in a specific relationship or situation—and what grace do you need from the Spirit to do it gladly rather than resentfully?
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how Christ's redefinition of greatness as servanthood—rooted in His substitutionary death—overturns our selfish ambition and empowers us to serve others for God's glory.

Monday Matthew 16:16

Peter's declaration of Jesus as 'the Messiah, the Son of the living God' reveals the disciples' initial grasp of Jesus's identity—yet they remained captive to worldly notions of what His kingdom should accomplish. This passage establishes the foundation: only those who truly know Christ can be freed from the lie that greatness means status and power. We must begin, as Peter did, by confessing Christ rightly; only then can we be transformed by His countercultural definition of true greatness.

Tuesday Mark 9:31

Here Jesus explicitly foretells His betrayal, death, and resurrection—yet the disciples' hearts remain unmoved toward humility and service. Their inability to hear His words about the cross exposes how thoroughly sin blinds us to our need for Christ's ransom. We, too, hear the gospel's announcement of Christ's death for us, yet our hearts drift toward the same selfish pursuits the disciples craved. Only as we meditate on the cross's necessity and power do we begin to grasp that our liberation from sin's slavery cost Christ everything.

Wednesday Mark 9:33

The disciples' argument about who is greatest mirrors the hidden motives we bring to prayer—requests for recognition, comfort, and status cloaked in spiritual language. Jesus's gentle rebuke invites us to honest self-examination: do our prayers reflect submission to God's will, or are they windows into our lingering love of self-promotion? Grace invites us to confess these patterns not in shame but in gratitude, knowing that Christ's death has already purchased our freedom from such bondage.

Thursday Mark 9:34

The disciples' silence when confronted with their dispute reveals the shame of selfish ambition laid bare. Yet Jesus does not condemn; He redefines. He shows that greatness is measured not by how many serve you but by how many you serve in love. This is possible only because Christ served us unto death, paying the ransom that frees us from slavery to self. As we are gripped by His substitutionary love, the natural response is to rejoice in lowly service, finding our true honor in His cross.

Friday Mark 10:45

Jesus's declaration—'The Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many'—anchors all Christian virtue in His atoning work. Every act of humble service we offer flows from the gospel's power, not from our natural inclination toward greatness. As we reflect on Christ's willing sacrifice this week, ask yourself: How is His ransom reshaping my motives in prayer, relationships, and work? Let the cross compel you toward glad, gospel-driven servanthood.
