Jesus: He Stoops to Conquer - John 13:1-20

John 13:1-20 Sinclair Ferguson
Thesis Jesus reveals His truest glory not through public signs of power but through the private humiliation of service, showing His disciples that His divine identity is most fully expressed in His willingness to stoop to the lowest place to cleanse and save His own.

1 · Ferguson shares his personal encounter as a teenager with Frank Houghton, illustrating how a brief 15-second pastoral interaction can have lifelong spiritual impact, setting up the theme that intimate moments with Christ transform us permanently

There is this section in John's Gospel that to me is just one of the most amazing sections in the whole of Scripture, in which Jesus gathers His disciples, eventually dismisses Judas Iscariot from the room, and spends what was obviously several hours teaching His disciples as they gather together for the Passover meal. So of all places in the Gospels, this is the place where we meet Jesus surrounded by His very best friends. And we're going to work our way through these chapters, John 13, 14, 15, 16, and 17 on Wednesdays as we move towards Easter.

4 · Ferguson establishes the literary structure of John's Gospel, identifying chapters 1-12 as the 'book of signs' where Jesus publicly displays His glory through mighty works, setting up the contrast with what follows in chapters 13-21

The Gospel of John, which is such a rich and deep gospel, appears to divide into two very different halves. And sometimes those two halves are described in the following way. Chapters 1 through 12 are sometimes described as the book of the signs. And you remember how throughout that part of John's Gospel, Jesus does mighty works. He does many signs. Indeed, as John says towards the end of the Gospel, if all the signs he had done had been written down in a book, the whole world wouldn't be able to contain them. But John has selected a number of those signs in order to put on display the glory of the Lord Jesus Christ. For example, at the end of the turning of the water into wine at the wedding in Cana, John says this was the first sign that Jesus did in which he manifested His glory. He showed His glory through the signs.

5 · Ferguson traces the narrative pivot at John 12:36-37 where Jesus withdraws from public ministry after the people's rejection and gathers His disciples privately, inaugurating the 'book of glory' where Jesus reveals His identity more intimately to those who believe than anywhere else in Scripture

But you remember how John begins his Gospel. He came to his own people and his own people refused to receive him. And so right at the end of chapter 12 we find these verses, John 12:36-37: When Jesus had said these things, I wonder if you have ever noticed this verse: When Jesus had said these things, he left and hid himself from them. Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him. So he had done all these signs, displayed his glory as the Son of God, but they refused to believe in him. And so He withdrew and He did no more public signs. But He gathered His intimate disciples with Him. And in chapter 13 to the end of the book, that whole section is sometimes, I think, well described as the book of glory. We have been told in John's Gospel that Jesus is going to be glorified. And now He gathers His disciples and He begins to reveal to them His glory. And at least in my own view, there is nowhere in the Bible you could possibly go, not even in John's Gospel, where the glory of the Lord Jesus Christ is more beautifully, clearly, magnificently, and intimately revealed. This is Jesus with his friends.

6 · Ferguson articulates the reciprocal dynamic of trust between Jesus and His disciples: as we trust ourselves more fully to Him, He entrusts more of Himself to us, revealing truths the unbelieving world cannot receive because rejection creates incapacity for deeper revelation

And it's so clear in what we read earlier on in John's Gospel that when people don't come to believe in Jesus, Jesus doesn't entrust himself to them. But when people do come to believe in Jesus, as these disciples with all their sins and faults, warts and all, had genuinely come to believe in Jesus, Jesus begins to entrust himself. So the more we trust ourselves to him as Savior and Lord, the more of himself he entrusts to us. And this is what he was doing here. He was showing them things that the world could never see, because the world that had rejected him wouldn't have had the capacity to take in the marvelous things he was going to say to them and the glorious things that he was going to show to them.

7 · Ferguson identifies the foot-washing as a private sign—Jesus' final acted parable—that dramatizes His entire redemptive mission in a way the disciples should be able to understand, transitioning the exposition toward the symbolic meaning of Jesus' actions

And he begins, as we see here in chapter 13, although he does no more signs in public, he begins by doing a sign in private. And the way John recounts this, the story of Jesus in the middle of the meal, getting up and going and getting the basin of water and taking out His outer clothes and putting the slave's towel around Him. And then as they sat at table, systematically going round each of the disciples and then saying to them, 'Do you understand what I have done?' It is obvious that although this is the book of glory, Jesus is wanting to show them His glory in a way they ought to have been able to understand in this sign. And of course, in this single sign, Jesus is giving them a kind of dramatic representation of all that he has come to do.

8 · Ferguson announces his sermon's five-point structure and states his pastoral aim: that seeing Jesus' glory in this passage would lead to deeper trust and reciprocal revelation, then introduces the first point

And so I want us to notice today very quickly, I want us to notice 5 things about this familiar passage that will draw us in to see the glory of the Lord Jesus Christ. Help us to trust ourselves more to him as Savior and Lord and begin to discover that as we do that, he entrusts more and more of himself and a knowledge of himself to us by his Holy Spirit. First of all, I want you to notice Jesus' sense of occasion.

9 · Ferguson expounds John 13:3 to show Jesus' profound self-awareness at the foot-washing: He acts in full consciousness of His eternal divine identity (having come from and returning to the Father's bosom) and simultaneously in awareness of the devil's active work through Judas, demonstrating His extraordinary sense of the cosmic occasion

You know how some people have that and some people clearly lack it. You know, people do things on certain occasions and you say, 'He had no sense whatsoever of the occasion.' And John tells us what the occasion was in verse 3: Jesus knew that the Father had given all things into his hands and that he had come from God and was going back to God. In other words, in a deep consciousness of His own eternal, most glorious divine identity. He had come from God and He was going back to God. It was in the consciousness, as John says at the beginning of his Gospel, that Jesus' place was in the bosom of the Father. And that's so interesting because you remember one of the things that seemed to mean so much to Peter and John when they were in the upper room was that John was reclining on the bosom of Jesus. It was just the way they sat. And John was so near to Jesus, could hear everything Jesus said. And it's the picture of our Lord Jesus living in the bosom of the Father so that nothing is hidden between them, so that every secret, every whisper is revealed. This is who He really is. This is where He really belongs. He is, as Paul says, the Lord of glory. He is the King of kings. He is Himself the Son of God. He is from all eternity. He is the Creator of the universe, the sustainer of all things. And He has this extraordinary sense of occasion that though He is the Lord of glory, He knows that the devil has put it into the heart of Judas Iscariot to betray Him. In other words, He's not only conscious of His own identity, He's conscious of a very important thing about His ministry.

10 · Ferguson traces Jesus' consciousness of cosmic conflict back to Genesis 3:15, showing that Jesus understands the foot-washing to occur at the decisive moment of redemptive history when the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head—a battle already beginning with Satan's work through Judas in the room

I wonder if you remember those words that God spoke in the Garden of Eden when the man and the woman had sinned. And He said, I'm going to put enmity between you and the serpent, between his seed and your seed. There will come a day when your seed will bruise or crush the head of the serpent. And God was saying, the way in which I'm going to bring restoration and salvation to this broken and lost world is going to be in a night of intense darkness and in a moment of extraordinary conflict. And so Jesus is not only, as it were, conscious that he has come from all eternity, he's conscious that he's He's right at the most significant point of all history when this great battle is going to be fought. And He's conscious that the enemy is already in the room seeking to attack Him. He knew who was going to betray Him.

11 · Ferguson expounds the phrase 'loved them to the end' (John 13:1), identifying John's characteristic double meaning: not merely temporal endurance until Jesus' death but purposeful love driving toward God's ultimate goal for the disciples—their complete sanctification and glorification

And it's in that context that we read in verse 1 that When the time had come for him to depart out of this world to the Father, having loved his own, that's the disciples, he loved them to the end. Now John loves double entendres. You know, saying things that you think, does he mean this or is there more to it? He doesn't just mean that Jesus loved them to the end of his days. He means, I think, that Jesus loved them to the end that God had for them.

12 · Ferguson expounds Jesus' humiliation in verses 4-5, noting that John uses the same verb ('lay aside') for Jesus' removing His garments as Jesus used for laying down His life, creating a deliberate verbal echo

And this is a marvelous picture, therefore, of Jesus' sense of occasion. And it's a holy privilege, I think, to feel, almost to sense, you could almost be there and listen to him speak. But if he has a sense of the occasion, he now engages in an act of profound humiliation. He knows that all things are under his authority. And John means all things. Not just the menu, not just the evening, but absolutely everything. Remember the beginning of the Gospel? Without Him was not anything made that was made. He is the Lord of everything, and He engages in this amazing display of humiliation. He rises from supper. He leaves the head of the table where He belongs. He goes and gets the basin and he takes off his outer clothes and he puts on the servant towel. And then he systematically goes round each of these disciples, including incidentally Judas Iscariot. Including Judas Iscariot. And he is prepared to wash their feet. Actually, what's so interesting about this is that John uses here a verb he had used in translating Jesus' presumably Aramaic words. When Jesus had said, 'I have the power to lay down my life.' And I think the reason John uses this verb when he says Jesus lays aside his outer garment is to say to us as we read through John's Gospel, do you see the connection between these two things? This is what he is portraying here. And again, it is so interesting. If you at your leisure read through those verses, I think you will notice, as I sometimes say, that the action suddenly seems to go into slow motion. And every last detail of what Jesus did is recorded. And John is saying, I want you to see this played out in slow motion. And if you have got time sometime, Get 2 Bibles and turn one Bible to John 13 and turn the other Bible to Philippians 2:5-11. And notice the similarities between the steps that Jesus physically took in the upper room and the description the Apostle Paul gives of what Jesus has done for our salvation. Though he was in the form of God, he didn't count equality with God something that would give him special privileges, but he emptied himself, made himself of no reputation, took on the form of a servant, humbled himself even unto death, death on a cross. Therefore, God has highly exalted him and given him a name that is above every name, that every knee should bow to him and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord. And you will notice that the same rhythm is present here in John's Gospel as Jesus stoops down and down and down and then eventually resumes His place at the table as Lord and Master.

13 · Ferguson identifies the foot-washing as Jesus' acted prophecy of the cross—showing the disciples in slow-motion physical detail what it means that He will be stripped to save them

And He is setting before them, it must have been to any of them who were sensitive, almost excruciating to them, that He was doing this. It was clearly in the most literal sense, in a way, excruciating because He was showing them what He was going to do for them on the cross, why He had come into the world, how He would be stripped in order to save. And He comes to Peter. And typically, because Peter was always doing this, whenever Jesus sent him a message about what he was going to do, Peter resisted. That gives us the third thing that we find here. Jesus' sense of occasion. Jesus' act of humiliation. Thirdly, Jesus' dramatization of his work of salvation. Laying aside the manifestation of his glory, becoming the servant. I wonder if they remembered how he had said about himself, the Son of Man came not to be deaconed to, not to have table servants, but to become a table servant. And it was actually being worked out before their eyes that Jesus was the servant.

14 · Ferguson highlights the dramatic speed of Peter's transformation—from adamant refusal ('not in a million years') to complete submission within five minutes—demonstrating Christ's power to overcome our deepest resistances through intimate ministry, echoing the earlier Bishop Houghton illustration about the power of brief encounters

And Do you know what Peter says quite literally when Jesus says to him, 'Peter, I want to wash your feet'? Peter says, 'Not to eternity.' I guess the way we would say it would be, 'Not in a million years. You will never wash my feet in a million years.' And I think one of the most significant things— think about what I said a few minutes ago about Bishop Harton. 15 seconds touching me for the whole of my history. Peter says, 'You will not do this in a million years.' Jesus so ministers to him, teaches him. Subdues him, draws him to understand and to believe that what Peter said would never take place in a million years had actually happened within 5 minutes. I'll come back to that in a moment.

15 · Ferguson expounds verse 12, showing that Jesus' resuming His place at table after the foot-washing dramatizes His resurrection and exaltation

So his sense of occasion, his profound humiliation, his dramatization of the work of salvation, and a kind of acted parable or prophecy of His coming exaltation. The language in verse 12 here, when He had washed their feet, He put on His outer garments and resumed His place, is also reminiscent of those words Jesus had spoken that's recorded in John chapter 10 when He said, I have power to lay down my life and I have power to take it up again. And so He puts on His clothes and He goes to the table. And now He sits as the host at the table. And if only they had eyes to see, which of course they didn't. You don't understand what I'm doing, but afterwards you will understand. They understood when He rose again. They must have thought, that's what He was trying to say to me. And I never saw it. He was speaking to me about His glorious exaltation. And that's so important to remember, isn't it? That we don't have a dead Christ. That we aren't mourners looking back to Jesus and saying, He was a wonderful teacher and oh, if only He could still be with us. But of course we know He's dead. Gone. You know, there is a religion that's like that, but it's not Christianity. No, the heart of being a Christian is that what you didn't understand, the risen Lord Jesus in His power and His grace opens your eyes to understand.

16 · Ferguson introduces the fifth sermon point—practical application—by showing that Jesus connects genuine understanding of the foot-washing to imitation: the proof that the disciples grasp what He has done for them is that they will do the same for others

And so we've got this picture not only of His humiliation, we've got this picture of His exaltation. So His sense of occasion, His amazing humiliation, His picture of His work of salvation, the prophecy of His coming exaltation, and of course His very practical application. You see what I've done to you. The mark that you understand what I've done to you and for you is that you will do this. To and for others. I have given you an example. Now go and do the same.

17 · Ferguson traces Peter's later teaching in 1 Peter back to the foot-washing, showing that Peter's instructions to 'clothe yourselves in humility' and follow Jesus' example deliberately echo Jesus' actions in the upper room, revealing that Peter spent decades reflecting on this moment and finally understood that Christ's death provides both pardon and a pattern to imitate

You know, if you fast forward, let's say 30 years, 40 years, maybe 30 years, you find Peter who was there in the upper room giving little indications in his first letter towards the end of the New Testament that these moments had made an indelible impression on him and helped him to understand the gospel. Because I wonder if you remember how he says to leaders in 1 Peter. He says, 'Clothe yourselves with humility.' And he uses language that's deliberately reflective of this incident when Jesus clothed himself clothe yourself in servant's garb. He says clothe yourselves in humility. And then he says something else. He says now Jesus died for us in order to bring us pardon, but He also died for us in order to leave us an example that we should follow in His footsteps. The word he uses is very interesting. It's the word that was used in the in the classical world of the handwriting that a teacher would do on a slate and then say to the child, 'Now you copy what I've done.' Actually, that was how I was taught to write. Some of you remember those books where there was a space for the teacher to write and then there was a space for you to write. I was actually taught A light stroke up and a heavy stroke down and don't blot the page. And Peter is saying, I think at last I've got it. That when I understand that He has died to bring me forgiveness, out of my love for Him, I want to become like Him. And so, in my life, I begin by God's grace to imitate the handwriting of the Lord Jesus Christ. Himself.

Now back to that point I said I would pick up.

19 · Ferguson applies Peter's resistance pattern to the congregation, asking them to identify areas where they are saying 'never' to Jesus, and encouraging them that Christ can overcome 'never in a million years' resistance within minutes—both in their own lives and in those they evangelize

Peter said, never to eternity, never to eternity. Let me ask you a question. Is there anything in your life, anything in your life about which the Lord Jesus speaks to you, perhaps even just coming to trust in Him. 20 · Ferguson directly addresses potential non-Christians present, suggesting that their presence itself may be evidence that Christ is already working to overcome their 'never in a million years' resistance, and invites them to surrender to Christ today, recognizing He offers both forgiveness and transformation

And actually, that might kind of ring a special bell in your mind today. Don't you see how by By power I can not only forgive you but transform you. 21 · Ferguson addresses believers who may be withholding specific areas of life from Christ's lordship, asserting that the very thing we most resist surrendering is precisely what Jesus has come to transform, and calls for complete yielding

Jesus, and his best friends. And to think that we can be his best friends too.

23 · Ferguson closes with a pastoral prayer asking that the Spirit would pour Christ into the hearers as Jesus poured Himself into the disciples, and that they would understand the connection between the foot-washing and the cross, leading to lives cleansed from sin and lived in conscious awareness of the risen Christ's presence

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how Jesus' humiliation reveals His glory—from His sovereign power to accomplish all things, through His willingness to stoop in service, to the transformation He works in those who surrender to His touch.

Monday John 1:3

Before we see Jesus stoop to wash feet, we must grasp what He is stooping from—the One through whom all things were made and by whom all things hold together. The foot-washing is not the act of a powerless servant but of the all-powerful Creator choosing to express His truest glory through radical humiliation. This sovereign power is precisely what makes His voluntary service in John 13 so staggering.

Tuesday Philippians 2:5-11

Paul frames Jesus' descent into human form and obedient suffering as the very means of His ascent to the highest place. The foot-washing in John 13 is a microcosm of this pattern: Jesus strips Himself, performs the lowliest task, and in doing so reveals the nature of divine glory itself. We are compelled to see that true greatness in the kingdom of Christ is inseparable from willing service.

Wednesday 1 Peter 2:21

Peter, who refused to have his feet washed, later understood that Christ's stooping was a pattern for his own life and for all believers. When Jesus says, 'I have set you an example that you should do as I have done,' He is not merely teaching etiquette—He is inviting us into a way of life shaped by humility and service to one another. The foot-washing becomes the template for how the redeemed are to love their own.

Thursday John 10:17-18

Jesus did not stumble into the foot-washing or the cross through weakness; He chose both with full knowledge and sovereign consent. His claim—'I have authority to lay it down'—assures us that every act of stooping, every humiliation, flows from His will and power. This transforms our understanding: His service to us is the supreme expression of His freedom and lordship, not a contradiction of it.

Friday Mark 10:45

The towel and basin are instruments of salvation. In washing the disciples' feet, Jesus performs in miniature what He accomplishes on the cross: the removal of defilement, the cleansing of our sin, the restoration of our communion with God. Whatever you are resisting—whether full surrender or a particular area of transformation—recognize that Jesus has already proven His power and love by descending to the lowest place to make you clean. His touch is redemptive, and His authority is absolute.

Memory verse this week

John 13:3-5

Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, rose from supper. He laid aside his garments, and girded himself with a towel. Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the towel that was girded around him.

Why this verse: This passage crystallizes the sermon's central paradox: Jesus exercises absolute cosmic authority ('all things into his hands') yet expresses His truest glory through self-humiliation and service. Memorizing this verse anchors the radical claim that divine power and lowly stooping are not contradictions but the very definition of Jesus' redemptive identity.
