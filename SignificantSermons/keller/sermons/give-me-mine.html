Give Me Mine

Luke 15:11-32 Tim Keller
Thesis The assault on community caused by our idolatrous disordered loves—seeking the Father's things rather than the Father himself—can only be overcome by seeing the beauty of God's agonizing, self-sacrificial love in Christ, which alone heals our souls and frees us to love.

Theological claims· 12
  1. The Parable of the Prodigal Son should be understood as a story about an assault on community caused by idolatry, which is only overcome by agony. unit #2
  2. Both sons assault the basic human community of the family, which Jesus' original audience would have immediately recognized as morally outrageous. unit #9
  3. The younger son's choice to prioritize the father's things over the father is a narrative representation of idolatry, which the Bible identifies as one of humanity's main problems. unit #14
  4. Elder brother idolatry, unlike younger brother immorality, manifests in self-righteous anger when obedience doesn't produce the expected blessings, proving that you're obeying God not for his beauty and worth but to secure blessings you believe he owes you. unit #21
  5. Both younger brother ('bad boy') idolatry and elder brother ('good boy') idolatry equally destroy community, as evidenced by how both tore the parable's family to pieces. unit #22
  6. Augustine theorized that all of our problems come from what he called 'disordered loves.' unit #26
  7. All problems in your life and in the world stem from disordered loves, which starve you, emotionally enslave you, and divide you from others. unit #31
  8. Augustine argues that both atheism and unipersonal monotheism make power, not love, the ultimate reality—atheism because reality is impersonal, unipersonal monotheism because God would have known power before love. unit #40
  9. If God is triune, then loving relationships constitute ultimate reality because before God created the world, the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit existed as a community of love, making community more fundamental than power, achievement, or building. unit #41
  10. If we were made in the image of the triune God, then the first bottom line for human existence is that life is fundamentally about community and loving relationships. unit #43
  11. Being made in the image of the triune God means you were built to replicate the Trinitarian pattern by putting God at the center of your life, serving him rather than yourself, and meeting his desires rather than your own. unit #45
  12. If you center on God as the Trinity centers on each other, God will fulfill your deep needs for beauty, love, honor, power, and rest, freeing you to give to others; but if you seek those things in finite humans, you'll become too broken, needy, angry, or addicted to care about others. unit #46

But secondly, emotionally enslaved. There's a place, and I won't read it to you, where Augustine says the real problem, when you see something that you love, you want to repose in it. You want to rest in it. And the problem is there is no source of honor and love and power and rest that you can absolutely rest in except in God.

And this was the problem of the younger brother, and I mean, pardon me, of the elder brother.

36 · Illustrates emotional enslavement through the elder brother's rage over his threatened inheritance, showing that when a good thing (a good name, money) becomes an ultimate thing, any threat to it produces disproportionate rage because your very identity depends on it

Because you see, his heart was, I'm going to get all this money. I'm going to have the whole thing. I'm going to get everything now. But when it was jeopardized, he goes ballistic. Why? Here's why.

If you want a good name and someone ruins your good name, you're mad. But if you make a good name into the ultimate honor, because you really don't really know God, you're getting your good name from what people, you're getting your self-image from what people say. If you make a good thing into an ultimate thing, and somebody ruins your name, you go ballistic, you may kill him.

See?

37 · Applies the principle of emotional enslavement to the congregation: legitimate disappointment over lost inheritance becomes murderous rage when the inheritance is the ultimate source of your identity and self-worth rather than God

To know that suddenly your father is giving away part of your inheritance, and you were expecting to have this much, and now you're only going to have this much, that'll make you sad. It would make me sad, okay? It'll make you sad. But when that is the ultimate thing in your life, when that is how you feel good about yourself, when that is actually, you know, the thing you've mainly set your hope on, and somebody starts to shrink it, then you go ballistic, and you know, you might kill.

That's what Augustine means.

38 · Transitions to the third and final consequence of disordered loves—the division of community—positioning it as the ultimate reason idolatry destroys relationships

And here is the reason why, then, the ultimate reason why this divides us, why idolatry kills community.

39 · Introduces Augustine's doctrine of the Trinity, acknowledging the cognitive difficulty while promising an explanation that will illuminate why community is ultimate reality

There is nobody like St. Augustine at explaining the beauty of the Trinity.

I know the Trinity hurts your mind when you think about it. I know it gives you kind of a brain lock, you know, as you try to think three beings in one God. There's only one God, but the Father, Son, and the Holy Spirit are all, you know, divine persons in the one God.

You know, what does all that mean? Here's what Augustine says.

40 · Presents Augustine's contrasting scenarios: if there's no God, ultimate reality is impersonal power; if there's a unipersonal God, that God only knew power before creation, making power more fundamental than love

Augustine says, if the world is here by accident, if there is no God, then the meaning of life is, you know, ultimate reality is basically impersonal. And you're here because of powerful accidental eruptions and forces.

And secondly, he says, if there is a unipersonal God, a God that's only one personality, then that God would not have known love until he created the universe filled with angels and human beings, right? Because you can't have love unless you have more than one person. And if there was one God, a unipersonal God, before he made the universe, he would have had power, but not love, and only felt love later. Love would have come in second.

Love would have been peripheral, right? And therefore, if there's no God, then what life is really about is power. And if there is a God, a unipersonal God, then power is more important than anything else. It's first.

41 · Presents Augustine's Trinitarian alternative: because God is a community of three persons loving each other before creation, loving relationships are ultimate reality—more fundamental than achievement, building, or power

But if there, if the Christian God is, if God is the Christian God, if God is triune, then that means before they exercise their power to create the world, the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, God was a community, a community of beings loving and knowing one another and communicating with each other. And that means that community, that means that loving relationships are the ultimate meaning of life. It's ultimate reality.

It comes before achievement. It comes before building. It comes before power.

It comes first.

42 · Explains the mechanics of Trinitarian love: each person of the Trinity serves, glorifies, and adores the others rather than seeking glory for themselves, making mutual glorification the pattern of divine happiness

And the infinite happiness of God and of the love of God work like this. We know from what Jesus has told us, that the Father glorifies the Son, and the Son glorifies the Father and the Holy Spirit, and the Holy Spirit glorifies the Father and the Son.

What does that mean? Each person, each divine person serves the others, glorifies the others, adores the others, does not take glory, but gives it.

43 · Draws the first implication of being made in the image of the triune God: human life is fundamentally about community and loving relationships, not individual achievement

And if we were made in the image of God, that God, then there's two bottom lines for our existence. Number one, your life is about community.

Your life is about loving relationships. That's what life is all about.

44 · Applies the Trinitarian doctrine to New York City's dominant culture: prioritizing individual achievement, money, status, and advancement over community contradicts how humans were built, resulting in inevitable brokenness

If you come to New York City and do what pretty much everybody does, you put individual achievement, money, status, and advancement ahead of community, ahead of friendships, ahead of relationships, ahead of serving other people.

You're going to dash yourself on the rocks of ultimate reality. You were not built for that. There's going to be brokenness in your life.

45 · Draws the second implication: humans are made not just for horizontal community with each other but to replicate the Trinitarian pattern vertically by centering on God, serving him rather than self, and meeting his desires rather than one's own

But also, if you're made in the image of that God, it's not just that relationships in general are important, but you were built to do with God what God does within himself. You see, each of the divine beings center on the others. They don't say, me, me, me, me.

Each one gives glory and adoration to the others. And if we were made in God's image, you were meant to put God in the center of your life, to say, I'm going to serve God, not myself. I'm going to meet God's desires, not my own.

46 · Establishes the causal chain: centering on God allows him to fulfill the deep needs for beauty, love, honor, power, and rest, which then frees you to give to others; but seeking those things in finite humans makes you too broken, needy, angry, or addicted to build community

And if you do with God what God is doing within himself, you will know the joy because he will fulfill those great deep needs of beauty and for love and for honor and for power and for rest, and then you'll be able to give them to others. But if you try to seek for those things in other finite human beings, you know what's going to happen? You're going to be too broken up, too needy, too angry all the time like the elder brother, too addicted like the younger brother to care about others.

47 · Applies the doctrine of disordered loves directly: healing requires centering on God not merely as a means to blessings but as beautiful in himself, loved for his own sake rather than for what he gives

You have to have your disordered loves healed so that God is the beauty of your life and the center of your life.

And the most important thing, you can't just believe in him. You can't just obey him. Then you might just be a younger brother or an elder brother.

You have to center your life completely on him and to say, I don't care what you give me. I don't even care how many prayers you answer. I love you.

I find you not just useful to get things. I find you beautiful and I serve you for who you are in yourself.

48 · Signals the sermon's turn to its final movement, reminding the congregation this is an introductory overview while transitioning to the father's agony as the means of overcoming the assault on community

Now, finally, and I did tell you this was an intro to the whole, there's so many features of the story that we haven't even begun to touch on, but let's just look at one more.

49 · Explains the normal human response to deep relational wounds: closing your heart, turning love into bitterness, and nursing anger because that stops the pain—the expected patriarchal response the father refuses

Remember what I said, what the commentator said a Middle Eastern patriarch would have done when the younger son came and said, you must give me my third of the inheritance. He should have slapped him across the face and should have driven him out with physical and verbal blows.

And I'll tell you why most of us would do that. Even if you're not a Middle Eastern patriarch, when you're hurt badly by someone, you want to staunch the wound. And the best way to staunch the wound is to turn your admiration and your desire for that person into anger and bitterness and sour grapes. You want to start saying that person, why did I ever see anything in him? Why did I ever see anything in her? So what you want to do is you want to close your heart.

You want to harden your heart. You want to fill it with anger and bitterness because that stops the hurt.

50 · Establishes that the normal self-protective response would have made reconciliation impossible, setting up the contrast with the father's costly alternative

And if he had done that, if the father had done that, there never would have been reconciliation.

Son would have never probably come back, but even if he had tried to come back, the father's heart would never have been open. So what does the father do?

51 · Exposes the lexical detail commentators note: the text uses bios (life) instead of the ordinary word for property, pointing to the father's need to liquidate ancestral land—his family's identity and life—to honor the son's demand

One of the things that the commentators always find fascinating is that it says there, after the younger brother says, give me my part of the estate, it says, and so the father divided his property between them. But the word property, the Greek word there is not the ordinary word for capital or property or possession.

It's very strange. All commentators point it out. For some reason, Jesus' story uses the word bios there, and it's the word for life, bios, biology.

And it's an intriguing word because here's what it's saying. The only way for this father to have really given the son a third of the property is, you know, they didn't have cash. You didn't have ATM windows.

It didn't work like that. They didn't have banks. Better for them.

The only reason that they, the only way he could have given a third of his estate would have been he would have had to sell his land. He would have had to sell his ancestral land.

52 · Uses a line from the musical Oklahoma to illustrate the ancient understanding that families belonged to their land rather than vice versa, because ancestral land constituted family identity in ancient and even more recent agricultural cultures

You know, in the movie Oklahoma, even in the movie Oklahoma, there's a line that says, oh, we know we belong to the land and the land we belong to is grand.

How sweet. But did you hear that? Listen, it doesn't say the land belonged to them. Did you hear it? It said they belong to it.

Because see, in ancient times, in older times, even in Oklahoma's times, a family's land was their, was their identity. See, that was my family land, my ancestral land.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. When the younger son asks his father to 'give me mine' and the father divides his property, what does this reveal about the son's actual desire—what he really wanted from his father?
    Luke 15:12
    → How is this different from simply wanting to leave home or be independent?
  2. The elder brother becomes furious when the father celebrates the younger son's return. What does his anger tell us about what he was really seeking from his father all along?
    Luke 15:28-30
  3. The sermon claims that both sons assault the family's community in different ways—one through rebellion, one through self-righteous anger. How does placing your heart on the father's things rather than on the father himself tear apart relationships with others?
    → Can you think of a concrete example from your own life or observation where this happens?
  4. Augustine's doctrine of 'disordered loves' suggests that we take good things—honor, love, power, security—and make them ultimate things. What are the 'good things' you find yourself most tempted to pursue as if they were ultimate, and how do you notice that pursuit affecting your peace and your relationships?
  5. The sermon emphasizes that the father's response—dividing his 'life' to give to his sons—prefigures Christ's self-sacrificial love. How does seeing the beauty and agony of God's self-giving love in the gospel actually change what we desire and how we love others?
    → What needs to happen in you this week for that healing of your disordered loves to begin?
  6. If we were truly made in the image of the triune God—a community of love—what would it look like for us to center our lives on God himself rather than on his blessings, and how would that freedom change the way we show up for our families, our church, and our neighbors?
Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how idolatry—placing our hearts on God's gifts rather than God himself—tears apart community, and how only the self-sacrificial beauty of Christ's love can heal our disordered desires and free us to truly love.

Monday 1 John 2:15-17

John names the pattern: the lust of the flesh, the lust of the eyes, and the pride of life—all seeking from the world what only God can give. We recognize in this catalog the younger son's hunger for pleasure and the elder son's hunger for honor and recognition. When we make creation's gifts our ultimate source of identity and rest, we prove we've forgotten whose things they are.

Tuesday Romans 1:21-25

Paul traces how we exchange the glory of God for images of created things, and our hearts grow dark and our affections twisted in the process. The younger and elder sons both experienced this spiritual starvation—one in a far country eating pig slop, the other standing outside the feast consumed by rage. When we bow to lesser gods, we become captive to the emotions those false gods demand.

Wednesday 1 John 5:20-21

John's final exhortation—'keep yourselves from idols'—comes after declaring that the true God gives us understanding to know him. Both brothers kept themselves from different idols yet fell prey to the same disease: they sought security and identity in something other than God's beauty and worth. The assault on community we see in the parable is not fundamentally about behavior but about competing loyalties.

Thursday John 1:1-3, 14

The Word existing eternally in communion with the Father, and then taking on flesh—this is God revealing that love and community constitute his very nature. We were made in the image of this God, not to achieve or accumulate, but to love and to be loved in community. Our deepest hunger, beneath all our disordered loves, is the hunger for the relational beauty we see in the Trinity.

Friday Isaiah 53:5-6

The suffering servant bears our iniquities, taking upon himself the brokenness that comes from our idolatrous hearts. When we see Christ's willingness to be pierced and crushed for us—not for what he could get, but for our sake—we begin to taste a love that requires nothing from us and gives everything. This agony, this beauty, this grace is what alone can reorder our loves and free us to turn from ourselves toward God and toward one another.

What Do You Really Want?

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to notice how both sons in the parable wanted their father's gifts instead of their father himself—and to see that same pattern in their own hearts. Listen for moments when kids recognize how easy it is to want what God gives rather than God himself.

Can you think of a time when you wanted something from someone you love—like a gift, or praise, or help—so badly that you forgot to actually enjoy being with them? What happened?
