Suffering: If God Is Good, Why Is There So Much Evil in the World?

1 Peter 1:3-12 Tim Keller
Thesis Evil and suffering, though a mystery we cannot fully explain, can be faced and even transformed through looking back to Christ's suffering on the cross, looking ahead to the resurrection hope, and looking into the gospel to see that we are Christ's living hope.

Theological claims· 17
  1. Abandoning belief in God does not help you understand or handle suffering. unit #6
  2. Without God, there is no basis for calling any event or law unjust because there is no transcendent moral standard above human opinion. unit #7
  3. If God does not exist, there is no objective basis for calling anything wrong—only subjective personal feelings. unit #8
  4. Evil and suffering is a bigger problem for atheism than for theism because atheism provides no basis for calling evil wrong or demanding a better world. unit #9
  5. God's promise to believers is not that we will avoid suffering but that when we suffer, his presence with us will prevent us from being consumed and will instead refine us. unit #16
  6. On the cross, Jesus experienced every form of suffering we have ever suffered—unjust persecution, loss of loved ones, and existential anguish. unit #19
  7. Jesus's suffering in Gethsemane and on the cross was more acute than that of other martyrs, raising the question of why this was so. unit #20
  8. Jesus's suffering on the cross was infinitely greater than physical torment because he was experiencing cosmic separation from God—the just consequence of our sin—which is why his agony exceeded that of other martyrs. unit #21
  9. The cross does not tell us why God allows evil and suffering, but it proves that the reason cannot be that he doesn't love us or doesn't care, because he entered into infinite suffering for us. unit #23
  10. Even Albert Camus, a non-Christian, recognized that Christianity's claim that God himself suffered changes the moral calculus of the problem of evil. unit #24
  11. Resurrection is not compensation for the life we lost but the restoration of this world, our bodies, and our relationships—purified and perfected. unit #29
  12. Suffering will be swallowed up by victory—not erased, but taken into the resurrection glory in a way that makes it infinitely greater because of what was suffered. unit #30
  13. If the resurrection is true, then all suffering will be integrated into our future glory in a way that makes it infinitely greater than if we had never suffered—this is the final defeat of evil. unit #32
  14. If the resurrection is true, then everything sad will come untrue, and yet the final glory will be infinitely greater for having experienced that suffering. unit #33
  15. The gospel is not elementary material but inexhaustibly deep truth that even angels continually study. unit #36
  16. Jesus's living hope that sustained him through the cross could not have been return to heaven, since he already had that—it must have been something he did not yet possess. unit #39
  17. Believers are Christ's living hope—the one thing he did not have in heaven and for which he endured the cross—the vision of glorified, resurrected believers in his arms. unit #41

0 · Keller introduces the sermon series format and this week's specific topic: the problem of evil and suffering as a major objection to Christianity in contemporary culture

Every single week we're going to take one, just one of the big questions, big problems that people in our culture and especially in New York City have with Christianity. What are those big problems? Every single week we're going to look at one. And this week we're looking at the one that you could call the problem of evil and suffering.

1 · Keller presents the classic philosophical formulation of the problem of evil: either God lacks power to stop evil or lacks goodness to want to, both of which undermine biblical claims about God's nature

And it's a very, very formidable problem for Christians, Christian belief. And if I could put the argument, you might say, against Christianity on the basis of evil and suffering in a nutshell it would be like this. If God allows evil and suffering to continue because he can't stop it then he might be good but he's not all-powerful. On the other hand, if God allows evil and suffering to continue because he could stop it and yet he won't stop it then he might be all-powerful but he's not good. Either way, the good, all-powerful God of the Bible couldn't exist. It's a pretty formidable argument.

What do we say to it? How do we address it? Seven minutes to discuss the problem of evil and suffering to people who have been struggling enormously with it, personally with it for five years. 7 · Keller argues that without God, there is no transcendent standard by which to judge anything—including violence and suffering—as unjust, since all we have is nature, where violence is normative

Because he loves us that much and he hates suffering that much that he was willing to come and be plunged into our sufferings and experience it so that someday he could end all evil without ending us.

And that's the final and ultimate defeat of suffering and death.

33 · Keller cites Dostoevsky's eschatological vision to validate his claim that suffering will be justified and healed in the resurrection, making everything sad 'come untrue' while simultaneously enriching the final glory

And that's the reason why Dostoevsky writes these astounding words. He says, I believe like a child that suffering will be healed and made up for, that all the humiliating absurdity of human contradictions will vanish like a pitiful mirage, like the despicable fabrication of the impotent and infinitely small Euclidean mind of man, that in the world's finale, at the moment of eternal harmony, something so precious will come to pass that it will suffice for all hearts, for the comforting of all resentments, for the atonement of all the crimes of humanity, for all the blood that they've shed, that it will make it not only possible to forgive, but to justify what has happened. That's unbelievable. But if the resurrection is true, that's absolutely right. Everything sad will come untrue. And yet, the resurrection will be infinitely greater for it having once been true, all that suffering and all that evil.

34 · Keller completes the second movement (looking ahead) and transitions to the third and final movement (looking into the gospel)

So you have to look back at what he's done, and you have to look ahead and have a living hope at what's to come. But there's one more thing this passage tells us. It's at the very, very end. There's something we have to not just look back to, something we have to look ahead to, but look into.

35 · Keller exposits verse 12 to highlight the angels' obsessive, passionate longing to look into the gospel, emphasizing that the gospel is inexhaustibly deep even for beings far more intelligent than humans

Verse 12 is astounding to me, because verse 12 says, they spoke of the things that were now told to you by those who preached the gospel to you, the gospel, into which things even angels long to look. And that word long, my friends, that word long is an amazing word. It's the word epithumia, which really means lust. The angels obsessively, passionately look into the gospel. They look at the story of God saving us through Jesus Christ, and they never get tired of looking into it. They're constantly looking into it, all the time.

36 · Keller challenges the misconception that the gospel is elementary material to be moved past, arguing that the angels—far more intelligent and ancient than humans—never tire of studying it

You say, gospel? Isn't the gospel just that kind of elementary ABC stuff, the basic kind of minimum belief requirements to get into Christian life, and then we go on to more advanced stuff after that, don't we? Don't tell the angels that. The angels are a lot smarter than you and me. You know, they've been around forever, and yet they never, ever, ever, ever get tired of looking into the gospel. The gospel's the thing that they're looking into all the time. Why?

37 · Keller pivots to apply the sermon's central metaphor—the fiery furnace—to Jesus himself, asking what sustained him through his cosmic suffering

Let me show you how. This is what we have to do as well. Just for a second, let's think about all this in terms of the gospel. How did Jesus get through his furnace?

38 · Keller cites Hebrews 12 to show that Jesus endured the cross because of the joy set before him—a living hope—which raises the question of what that hope was

How did he get through? Jesus came through an incredible furnace of affliction. How did he get through his furnace? Hebrews 12 says, for the joy that was set before him, he endured the cross. So he had a living hope, of course. The only way you get through the furnace is through a living hope. What was his living hope?

39 · Keller reasons that Jesus's living hope could not have been return to heavenly bliss, since he already had that—the hope must have been something he did not yet possess

Well, that's a good question. What could it be? You say, well, maybe what he was hoping for was bliss in heaven with his father. But don't forget, this living hope is something that got him out of that. What was it that drew him away from heaven and put him through the fiery furnace? What was his hope? What could he possibly have been looking for that he didn't already have up there?

40 · Keller cites Isaiah 53 to identify Jesus's living hope: seeing the results of his suffering—the justification of many—which made infinite suffering worth it

Isaiah 53 says, the results of his suffering he will see and be satisfied. And that's an amazing statement. What could make infinite suffering worth it? And the answer is, my righteous servant, this Isaiah 53, shall justify many.

41 · Keller delivers the climactic doctrinal claim: believers themselves—glorified and resurrected—are Christ's living hope, the joy that motivated him through the cross

You are his living hope. That's the only thing he did not have in heaven and that he had to come to earth and go to the cross and plunge himself into the fiery furnace for. You, a beautified, unspoiled, unfading, perfect, restored, resurrected, glorified you in his arms. That is his living hope. That's what filled him with such joy. That's what filled him with such inexorable and infallible resolve that he went into the furnace and he came out like gold.

42 · Keller applies the doctrine: recognizing that we are Christ's living hope transforms him into our living hope, enabling us to walk through our own furnaces

And knowing that he, the thought that you are his living hope, the thought that you are his living hope will make him your living hope. The thought that you were his living hope and that's what drove him through the furnace will make him your living hope. And you'll be able to walk through the furnace with him.

43 · Keller closes the third movement by returning to verse 8, promising that continual meditation on the gospel will produce love for Christ and inexpressible joy even in suffering

And if you do, you see that wonderful verse eight? If you learn to look into the gospel the way the angels do and always see in new ways what he has done for you, the way we just tried to give you an example of, at sometimes you will actually, you don't see him, but you will love him. And you'll rejoice with joy unspeakable and full of glory.

44 · Keller concludes with a vivid metaphor: if believers look into the gospel as prescribed, their suffering (grease) will be taken up into Christ's redemptive story and transformed into gold

And if you do, your grease will be taken up into his story and turned to gold.

45 · Keller closes with a pastoral prayer that summarizes the sermon's three movements and transitions the congregation into the Lord's Supper, framing the sacrament as an opportunity to look into the gospel alongside the angels

Let's pray. Thank you, Father, for giving us a complete portfolio of what we need in order to face the horrible mystery of evil and suffering. It's a horrible mystery. We don't know why. It's not over. We don't understand the reason for individual tragedies and catastrophes. We don't. But when we look behind us, when we look ahead of us, when we look into the gospel of what your son has done, we have everything we need so that the evil and suffering, instead of consuming us, will turn us to gold. We pray, Lord, that you would help us right now as we go to the table and receive the Lord's Supper to look with the angels and all the hosts of heaven deeply into your gospel and come out with a strength and a joy that only that can give us. We pray this in Jesus' name. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we look back to Christ's infinite suffering on the cross, ahead to resurrection hope, and into the gospel to see ourselves as his living hope—three perspectives that transform how we face suffering.

Monday Matthew 27:46

When Jesus cried out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"—he was not simply enduring nails and mockery, but the just consequence of our sin: separation from his Father. This is why his agony exceeded that of any martyr. As we face our own suffering, we are upheld by a God who did not remain distant but plunged himself into the deepest possible anguish for us.

Tuesday Isaiah 53:10-11

Isaiah prophesies that the Lord was pleased to crush his servant—not out of cruelty, but to make his soul an offering for sin. The cross does not explain why God allows suffering, but it silences the accusation that he doesn't love us. When we suffer, we suffer not abandoned by an indifferent God, but upheld by one who has already borne the weight of cosmic justice on our behalf.

Wednesday Revelation 21-22

The vision of the new heavens and new earth is not escape to an abstract heaven, but the renewal of creation itself—our bodies glorified, our relationships healed, our losses restored. Suffering will be swallowed up in victory, not erased but integrated into our final glory in a way that makes it infinitely greater because of what we endured. This is not pie in the sky, but the concrete promise that anchors us through present pain.

Thursday Hebrews 12:2

Jesus "for the joy set before him endured the cross"—but what joy could motivate the infinite suffering of the Son of God? It was not return to a heaven he already possessed, but the sight of his redeemed people, glorified and eternal, standing before him. This means we are not accidental to God's plan; we are the prize that made the furnace worth entering. Our redemption was not his duty—it was his delight.

Friday 1 Corinthians 15

Paul's extended meditation on resurrection in 1 Corinthians 15 anchors our hope not in escape from suffering but in the reality that death—and all suffering—has been defeated. When we grasp that we ourselves are Christ's living hope, the one thing he did not have and for which he endured the cross, his hope becomes ours. We can face our own furnaces with unshakeable confidence, not because we understand suffering, but because we know the one who transformed it into victory.

Sunday-evening family table

What Made Jesus Keep Going

For the parent

This prompt draws on Keller's insight that Jesus endured the cross because of his hope in something he didn't yet have—us, his glorified people. It's concrete enough for younger kids to grasp ('What keeps you going when something is hard?') while inviting older kids and teens into the stunning reality that Christ's hope centered on his redeemed people. Listen for both the practical answers and the moment when the deeper truth lands.

The preacher said that Jesus went through the cross because he had a living hope—something he was looking forward to that he didn't have yet in heaven. What do you think he was hoping for? And when you have to do something really hard, what hope keeps you going?
Works for ages 7+; younger kids can answer the second part while older kids engage with the first.
Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In 1 Peter 1:3-5, Peter grounds Christian hope not in the absence of suffering but in the resurrection of Jesus Christ. What difference does it make that our hope is anchored to a past historical event (Christ's resurrection) rather than to our circumstances changing in the present?
    1 Peter 1:3-5
    → Can you think of a time when focusing on what Christ has already accomplished steadied you more than waiting for your problem to be solved?
  2. The sermon argues that without God, there is no objective basis for calling anything unjust or wrong. What does this suggest about the atheist's position when facing genuine evil and suffering in the world?
  3. Peter writes that believers are being 'refined by fire' through suffering (1 Peter 1:6-7). Based on the sermon's teaching, what is the difference between suffering that refines us and suffering that merely crushes us?
    1 Peter 1:6-7
    → What role does God's presence play in determining which outcome we experience?
  4. The sermon emphasizes that on the cross, Jesus experienced 'cosmic separation from God'—not merely physical pain but the infinite consequence of bearing our sin. How does understanding the depth of Christ's suffering on the cross change the way you interpret his promise to be with us in our own suffering?
    Matthew 27:46
  5. According to the sermon, the resurrection does not erase our suffering but restores and perfects all things—our bodies, our relationships, our world—and weaves our suffering into that glory. What comfort or challenge do you find in that vision rather than in the idea that our pain will simply be forgotten?
    Revelation 21-22
    → How might this reshape the way you grieve losses or hardships you are currently facing?
  6. The sermon concludes that you are Christ's living hope—the one thing Jesus did not possess in heaven and for which he endured the cross. How does knowing that your redemption and glorification was the deepest motivation for Christ's suffering affect your sense of worth and your ability to face your own trials?
    Hebrews 12:2
    → What would it look like this week to live as if you truly believed you are that precious to the risen Christ?
Couples · three questions over coffee

Looking Back, Looking Ahead, Looking In

  1. When you heard about Christ's suffering on the cross and his infinite separation from God for us, what stirred in your heart—comfort, awe, conviction, or something else?
  2. How do we tend to handle suffering differently when we remember that Jesus walked into the deepest furnace for us, and how might that change the way we face hard seasons together?
  3. What is one area of current or past suffering in our marriage where you'd like to ask the other to pray that we'd see Christ's presence refining us rather than consuming us?
