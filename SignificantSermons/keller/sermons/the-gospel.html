The Gospel

Isaiah 53:4-5 Tim Keller
Thesis The gospel produces three interrelated transformations—restructured identity free from cultural idols, removal of sin through Christ's costly substitutionary sacrifice, and complete reversal of worldly values—creating a radically new kind of human community.

Theological claims· 34
  1. The gospel is neither moralistic religion nor secular self-discovery, but a third category entirely. unit #5
  2. The gospel produces three transformative results: restructuring of heart, removal of sin, and reversal of values. unit #6
  3. Ancient cultures made family an idol by transforming childbearing from a good thing into the ultimate thing that determined worth. unit #11
  4. All cultures oppress by declaring that worth depends on possessing certain objects—ancient cultures said family/children, modern cultures say individual assets like appearance. unit #15
  5. Ancient cultures enslaved through collectivist idols (family), while modern Western culture enslaves through individualistic idols (looks, career, money). unit #16
  6. When identity is built on anything other than God, failing to obtain it produces psychological and social death. unit #17
  7. Every culture enslaves people because cultures cooperate with the human heart's desire for self-justification by defining worth through achievement. unit #18
  8. Escaping cultural enslavement is nearly impossible through human effort, but God offers a way to both emotional and cultural freedom. unit #19
  9. God's command for the barren woman to sing is culturally radical because it offers both internal emotional freedom from shame and external freedom from oppressive cultural structures. unit #21
  10. Children symbolize worth and honor in ancient culture, but God declares these are available apart from children—a revolutionary claim. unit #23
  11. Every religion except Christianity is built on performance: try hard to live up to standards, eventually receive positive verdict. unit #25
  12. Marriage is the perfect metaphor for Christianity because it's both intense love relationship and immediate legal status obtained through vows, not performance. unit #26
  13. Christianity uniquely offers an immediate positive verdict and God's delight now through union with Christ—no other religion dares make this offer. unit #27
  14. When God becomes your value, you receive restructured identity producing cultural and emotional freedom to appreciate cultural goods without enslavement. unit #29
  15. Heart restructuring flows from understanding sin's removal, which we learn from the suffering servant image in Isaiah 53. unit #31
  16. The first shocking element in Isaiah 53 is the violence of the Messiah's death, which seems to contradict his mission to end violence. unit #36
  17. The second shocking element is that Isaiah 53 describes a human sacrifice, yet the Bible categorically forbids human sacrifice everywhere else. unit #38
  18. The third shocking element is the voluntariness of the servant's death, which seems to contradict the Bible's condemnation of suicide. unit #39
  19. Isaiah 53 appears to contradict three clear biblical teachings: the Messiah's power, the prohibition of human sacrifice, and the condemnation of suicide. unit #40
  20. The symbolic interpretation fails because the passage's logic requires the servant to suffer instead of the nation, which is impossible if the servant merely symbolizes the nation. unit #42
  21. Jesus' identity as the incarnate Son of God resolves the suicide problem because God owns his own life and has the right to lay it down. unit #50
  22. True forgiveness is agonizing because the forgiver must absorb the cost of the wrong rather than making the wrongdoer pay—there is no forgiveness without suffering. unit #51
  23. If forgiveness requires suffering for humans with flawed justice, it requires it infinitely more for God with perfect justice—God had to suffer to forgive us. unit #52
  24. Moralistic religion produces no deep transformation because relating to a merely holy and demanding God through effort doesn't move the heart. unit #53
  25. Relativistic spirituality produces no deep transformation because relating to a God who accepts everyone regardless doesn't move the heart. unit #54
  26. The biblical God is infinitely holy and infinitely loving simultaneously, making grace costly rather than cheap or earned, which is why it moves the heart. unit #55
  27. The gospel simultaneously humbles through God's holiness (requiring Christ's death) and affirms through God's love (glad to die for us), destroying both pride and self-hatred. unit #56
  28. Only seeing what it cost God to remove sin produces the restructured identity free from both inferiority and superiority—no other method can achieve this. unit #57
  29. Unlike moralism which oscillates between pride and self-hatred, the gospel produces simultaneous humility (as sinner) and boldness (as loved), creating a radically new stable identity. unit #58
  30. Transformation happens when Christ's substitutionary death becomes both intellectually coherent and existentially melting through understanding God's infinite holiness and love producing costly grace. unit #59
  31. Understanding God's holiness and love that make grace costly, and what it cost God to remove sin, produces the restructuring of heart identity. unit #63
  32. Keller introduces the third main point by noting Isaiah 54 contains two images flowing from chapter 53: the barren woman (already discussed) and the afflicted city God promises to rebuild with precious stones. unit #65
  33. Isaiah and Revelation prophesy that at the end of time, God will renew the entire world, removing all disease, suffering, death, poverty, racism, and injustice. unit #69
  34. Keller poses the question of how believers participate in the coming renewal and notes God's consistent pattern of choosing the weak and excluded—eunuchs, barren women, unlikely candidates. unit #70

Now, let me put it in a nutshell. The more children you had, the better your family did. The more children you had, the more your land produced, the more your shop produced, because you had more labor. 11 · Keller transitions from cultural description to theological diagnosis: ancient cultures made family an idol by turning the good thing of childbearing into the ultimate source of worth and security

And therefore, a woman who bore children in those ancient cultures was a national hero. Let's take a look at it. First of all, it's shocking because of the violence of this death, the violence of this death. Who's supposed to bring an end to injustice is instead the victim of injustice.

35 · Keller provides close exegesis of Isaiah 53:5,8, explaining the Hebrew terminology for violent death and piercing to emphasize the extreme physical suffering described

Look at verse 8. By oppression and justice, he was taken away. And who can speak of his descendants? For he was cut off from the land of the living. And that cut off from the land of the living is a Hebrew term that means a violent death. And verse 5 says he was pierced for our transgressions. Literally, that word says pierced through. It's a verb that means to be run through. It means to have something come in the front of your body and go out the back. It is a term that describes the most painful and excruciating possible death.

36 · Keller articulates the theological problem: how can the Messiah accomplish his mission of ending violence and injustice by himself becoming the victim of violence? This is the first shocking contradiction requiring explanation

Now, this seems to contradict everything else we know about the Messiah. It seems to contradict everything else that we see. How could this be the Messiah? How could the Messiah possibly bring an end to violence and bring an end to injustice and bring an end to the brokenness of the world by being broken to bits himself? That makes no sense at all. So that's the first thing. The first thing that's shocking is the violence of this death.

37 · Keller moves to the second shocking element—vicariousness—by examining Isaiah 53:10's description of the servant as a guilt offering, explaining the Old Testament sacrificial system background

The second thing that's shocking, even more shocking, is the vicariousness of this death. Look at verse 10. It was the Lord's will to crush him and cause him to suffer. And though the Lord make his life a guilt offering, whoa, stop, wait, what? The Bible talks a lot about offerings, guilt offerings, in the tabernacle, in the temple. They were animal sacrifices. When someone was guilty, you did an animal sacrifice and an animal was slain and offered up and that took away the guilt. And many, this is all through the Bible.

38 · Keller identifies the second shocking contradiction: the servant is described as a human guilt offering, yet the Bible absolutely forbids human sacrifice everywhere else

But one thing is absolutely and totally clear in the Bible that never, ever, ever, ever was there to be a human sacrifice. Human sacrifice was in no uncertain terms condemned. Everywhere else in the Bible. And yet that's what this is. He was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities. Our punishment has gone to him. His peace has come to us. He's a guilt offering. And yet the Bible says everywhere, you must not do human sacrifice.

39 · Keller identifies the third shocking element: the servant voluntarily takes on suffering and death, yet suicide is condemned throughout the Bible

So first, the violence of the death is shocking. Secondly, the vicariousness of the death is shocking. And last of all, the voluntariness of the death is shocking. Look at verse four. He took up our infirmities. He carried our sorrows. Literally, that means he picked them up and put them on himself. He's voluntarily dying. But everywhere in the Bible, suicide's a sin.

40 · Keller summarizes the three contradictions—Messiah's power, human sacrifice prohibition, suicide condemnation—posing the interpretive problem that demands resolution

So this contradicts what the Bible says about the power of the Messiah. This contradicts what the Bible says about the forbiddenness of human sacrifice. It contradicts what the Bible says about the sinfulness of suicide. How do we make sense of this?

41 · Keller presents and then begins to critique the symbolic interpretation that reads the suffering servant as a personification of the nation's suffering rather than an individual

42 · Keller refutes the symbolic interpretation by showing the passage's logic requires substitution—the servant suffers instead of the nation, which is impossible if the servant is merely a symbol of the nation

Okay, but there's a huge trouble with this. Even poetry still has to have a point. And what is the point of this passage? The point of this passage is that the servant suffers instead of the nation. The servant suffers so the nation doesn't have to suffer. And so if the servant is just a symbol for the nation, how can the nation suffer so the nation won't suffer? How can the nation suffer in place of the nation? No. This is a human being. This is an individual human being. But who?

43 · Keller pivots from the interpretive problems to the solution: the passage's meaning depends entirely on correctly identifying who the suffering servant is

How can we make any sense out of it? Well, it all depends on who this is. Who is it?

44 · Keller begins the extended Acts 8 narrative illustration by introducing the Ethiopian eunuch who traveled to Jerusalem to worship and is now reading Isaiah on his return journey

Centuries later, after this was written, the book of Acts, chapter 8, tells us a story, an account of something that happened many years later. It tells us about an African, an Ethiopian, and we're told that he had gone all the way to Jerusalem in order to worship God, and now he was coming back reading the scroll of Isaiah.

45 · Keller unpacks the eunuch's spiritual desperation (traveling enormous distance seeking God) and the devastating rejection he experienced (turned away from the temple despite the journey because of his castration)

Now, even that little introduction tells us an awful lot about what happened to this man. Because first of all, in those days, even today I'm sure, but in those days the trip from Ethiopia to Jerusalem was enormously long, incredibly dangerous, and terribly difficult. And the only possible reason an individual would go all the way from Ethiopia to Jerusalem in order to worship is if this person was deeply spiritually dissatisfied, that he was searching, he was seeking, that there was a great spiritual need, longing, emptiness, let's say. And the first thing we know is that. The second thing we know is now that he's on his way back, he would have been turned away. Do you know why he would have been turned away? He was a eunuch. And the Mosaic law made it very clear that nothing deformed and nothing diseased could go into the presence of God. And this man had been castrated, and anyone who had been castrated was absolutely forbidden to go into the temple. After all those miles, hundreds, thousands, I don't know how, after all that way he went and he was turned away, unfit, unclean.

46 · Keller explains the cultural background of the eunuch's castration (required for royal officials) and its devastating significance in a culture where descendants meant everything, possibly driving his spiritual search

The reason he was castrated was because he was an official. And in those days in almost all of the royal, in all of the kingdoms, if you were an official who was going to work in close quarters with the royal family, the requirement was castration. And of course it was a terrible price to pay in a culture in which having descendants meant everything. And maybe that was one of the reasons why he was spiritually searching, I don't know, but he'd been turned away.

47 · Keller narrates Philip's encounter with the eunuch, who is reading Isaiah but unable to understand it without help, inviting Philip to explain

And now he's on his way back and he's reading the book of Isaiah. And Acts 8 tells us that Philip, a minister of the gospel, a minister, a missionary, finds this man, and this is what happened. We're told, Then Philip ran up to the chariot and heard the man reading Isaiah. Do you understand what you're reading? Philip asked. How can I? He said, unless someone explains it to me. So he invited Philip to come up and sit with him.

48 · Keller shows the powerful connection between the eunuch's condition (cut off from descendants) and the passage he's reading (servant cut off from descendants), creating an electrifying moment of identification

Now, this was the passage that the eunuch was reading. He was led like a lamb to the slaughter. And as a lamb before her shears his silence, so he did not open his mouth. And who can speak of his descendants? For he was cut off from the land of the living. Imagine how electrifying this must have been for a man who'd just been turned away from the house of God because he couldn't have descendants. And yet here's this servant of the Lord who's voluntarily taking this condition on.

49 · Keller reaches the climax of the narrative: Philip reveals that the suffering servant is Jesus, the answer to all the riddles in Isaiah 53

And so the text goes on. The eunuch asked Philip, tell me, please, who is this prophet talking about? Himself or someone else? And then Philip began with that very passage of scripture and told him the good news about Jesus. It's Jesus.

50 · Keller shows how Jesus' identity as the incarnate Son of God resolves the first riddle: it's not suicide because God has the right to lay down his own life, unlike humans who don't own their lives

And you know what? That's the answer to all the riddles. If this figure, this suffering servant, is the son of God, come down from heaven in human form, that begins to unravel the riddles. First of all, that shows this is not the sin of suicide. You know why? You and I, we did not give ourselves life. And therefore, we can't take our own lives. Our lives don't belong to us. They belong to God. But God's life is his own. And he laid it down for us.

51 · Keller introduces Bonhoeffer's principle that forgiveness requires suffering—the forgiver bears the cost rather than exacting payment—establishing the logic of substitutionary atonement at the human level

And secondly, it explains the vicariousness of it. Dietrich Bonhoeffer has written, forgiveness is a form of suffering. Do you know what that means? If someone has really, really wronged you, you've got to forgive them. You've got to forgive them. If you don't forgive them, you'll be eaten up with bitterness and you'll become part of the endless cycle of retaliation that makes the world the mess of a place that it is. So you've got to forgive. But Bonhoeffer's right. Forgiveness is agonizing. When you want to pay somebody back, but you don't pay them back, you'll pay. When you want to make them suffer, but you don't make them suffer because you're trying to forgive them, you suffer. Real forgiveness always entails suffering. There's no such thing as forgiveness without suffering, not if you've really been wrong.

52 · Keller extends the forgiveness-requires-suffering principle from human to divine level: if it's true for us with flawed justice, how much more for God's perfect justice—God must suffer to forgive

And if that's true for us, with our minuscule sense and flawed sense of justice, how much more is we to be true of God? And the Ethiopian eunuch realized this is God suffering in order to forgive us. If God was not going to pay us back, he had to pay, just like with us.

53 · Keller contrasts the emotionally flat moralistic response to God (try hard, earn approval) with the gospel's transformative power, showing why moralism doesn't produce deep change

And when the eunuch realized that this is God suffering in order to forgive him and receive him, it changed his life. And here's why it changed his life. I want you to think about this. We've said the gospel is not moral conformity or self-discovery. It's not moralism or relativism. Because you see, moralistic people have a God who's sort of holy and demanding. And the way you have a relationship with this God is you try very, very hard. But when people who have a God who's basically holy and demanding and they're pleasing through moral behavior, when they think of their relationship with God, it doesn't move them to tears. It doesn't galvanize them, electrify them. It doesn't change them from the inside out. They just say, yeah, yeah, of course I know God. I work very hard, you know.

54 · Keller contrasts the emotionally flat relativistic response to God (he loves everyone anyway) with the gospel's transformative power, showing why cheap grace doesn't produce deep change

And then there's people, New Yorkers, basically, who think, you know, if there is a God, I believe he loves everyone. He excludes no one. He accepts everyone. Yeah, I have a relationship with God. I'm spiritual because God loves everyone. But if that's what you believe in a God who just accepts and loves everyone, you know, no matter what, if that's what you believe, when you think of your relationship with God, it doesn't galvanize you. It doesn't move you to tears. It doesn't electrify. It doesn't change you from the inside out. You say, well, of course he loves everybody.

55 · Keller presents the biblical alternative: God is simultaneously infinitely holy and infinitely loving, which makes grace costly and therefore emotionally transformative in a way moralism and relativism cannot match

But the biblical God, the God of the gospel, the God of Isaiah, the God of the African eunuch, is infinitely holy and infinitely loving. And that's the reason why God's grace is costly. It's so costly, that's why it's so moving.

56 · Keller articulates the gospel's unique psychological power: simultaneous humbling (God's holiness required Christ's death) and affirmation (God's love made him glad to die) produces freedom from both pride and self-hatred

In other words, God's grace is infinitely costly and melts your heart when you realize that God was so holy he couldn't shrug evil off, but he was so loving he couldn't just punish us for it. And not until you're humbled down into the dust because he's so holy he had to die for you, and not until you're affirmed and valued into the sky because he loves you so much that he was glad to die for you, will you be humbled out of the pride that makes you look down on other people, and will you be affirmed out of the self-hatred that makes you look down on yourself at the same time?

57 · Keller claims that only understanding the cost of sin's removal produces the restructured identity free from both inferiority and superiority—this transformation is impossible through any other means

In other words, only when you see what it costs God to remove your sin will you finally have the death of all inferiority and the death of all superiority, and you'll be able to have freedom, the restructured identity that no one else has. It's impossible to have it any other way.

58 · Keller contrasts moralism's oscillation between pride (when succeeding) and self-hatred (when failing) with the gospel's stable identity of simultaneous humility and boldness

Sure, if you're a moralist then you feel good, you know, when you're living up, but you're proud, or you feel bad when you're living up, you know, but you hate yourself. But here this is proud is gone, and self-hatred is gone, because the gospel says you're so humbled because you're a sinner, and you're so bold because you're absolutely loved at the same time. It gives you a radically new identity.

59 · Keller summarizes the connection between understanding costly grace and transformation: when Christ's substitutionary death is both intellectually grasped and emotionally melting, life change happens

It's the removal of sin and the costliness of it. When the substitutionary death of Jesus Christ is intellectually coherent and existentially melting to your heart, that's when the changes happen. And that comes only when you see God is not just holy and demanding, or not just loving and accepting everybody, but infinitely holy and infinitely loving at once, and therefore his grace is costly. And when that melts your heart, that changes you.

60 · Keller narrates the climactic scene from A Tale of Two Cities where Sidney Carton forcibly substitutes himself for Charles Darnay, taking Darnay's punishment while Darnay receives Carton's freedom—a literary illustration of substitutionary atonement

You know Charles Dickens' novel A Tale of Two Cities. It's about Sidney Carton and Charles Darnay, and they both love the same young woman, but she marries Charles. And then they start, you know, they get married and they start having children. But this is the French Revolution, and so Charles Darnay is arrested, and he's taken to prison, and he awaits his execution. And on the night before his execution, Sidney Carton, who looks quite a bit like Charles Darnay, steals into the prison and says, look Charles, you have a wife, you have a child, let's switch places. Let's change clothes. I'll die in your place. And Charles Darnay says, no way! Are you kidding? I will never let you do such a thing. So what does Sidney Carton do? He smacks him over the head and knocks him out cold. He puts his clothes on him. Sidney's peace passes to Charles. Charles' punishment passes to Sidney. And he has some people take Charles, who's now out cold, and take him out, and he assumes his place in the prison waiting to be executed.

61 · Keller continues the Tale of Two Cities narrative to show how even the seamstress—not the direct beneficiary—is strengthened to face death by witnessing Carton's substitutionary sacrifice

And there's a little seamstress young woman who is also about to be executed, and she knew Charles Darnay previously. When she hears he's there, she seeks him out and comes up and starts talking. It's, oh, you remember this, and you remember that. And of course, Sidney Carton is looking away, hoping that she doesn't look too closely, and suddenly she sees. And she realizes it's not Charles Darnay. And her eyes get big. And she says, are you dying for him? And he says, yes, and for his wife and children. And she says, stranger, I don't think, I have been feeling I'm not going to be able to face my death, but could I hold your hand? Because if someone as brave and as loving as you holds my hand, I think I'll be okay. And Sidney Carton says, all right.

62 · Keller applies the illustration: if Carton's sacrifice (not even for the seamstress) strengthened her profoundly, how much more should Christ's sacrifice for us transform every dimension of our lives if we allow it

Now, he wasn't even dying for her, but his substitutionary sacrifice strengthened her to her roots. How much more will the substitutionary sacrifice of Jesus Christ for you absolutely change you and strengthen you if you would just let it have its way with you in your memories, in your attitude, in your personality, in the way in which you look at the world, in the way in which you look at life?

63 · Keller states the theological principle connecting the second and first main points: understanding the cost of sin's removal (section 2) produces heart restructuring (section 1)

To the degree you understand the holiness and the love of God that makes the grace so costly, and that you understand what it cost God to remove your sin, that will create that restructuring of heart.

64 · Structural pivot from the second main point (removal of sin) to the third main point (reversal of values)

And one more thing it'll create, and that is a complete reversal of values.

65 · Keller introduces the third main point by noting Isaiah 54 contains two images flowing from chapter 53: the barren woman (already discussed) and the afflicted city God promises to rebuild with precious stones

Very briefly, I must say that we not only have out of the flowing of the removal of sin in chapter 53 comes chapter 54, and we have two images in chapter 54, a barren woman who is singing for joy, and a deserted city, an afflicted city. This is talking about a city that is poor, that is devastated, and God says, I will rebuild your walls, and I will rebuild your gates, and I will rebuild your battlements and your towers, and I'll rebuild them out of sapphire, and out of rubies, and out of diamonds.

66 · Keller unpacks the significance of the jeweled city: it represents complete flourishing across all dimensions—military security, economic prosperity, and aesthetic beauty

Now, imagine a city like that. Do you realize that a city that would have been made out of diamonds, and sapphires, and rubies would have been, first of all, absolutely militarily and politically secure, because nobody could break down walls made of that. Secondly, it would have been incredibly economically prosperous, because it would be the most valuable and wealthy city in the world. And then thirdly, it would also be a place of aesthetic beauty. Its artistic life, and its economic life, and its political life would be flourishing.

67 · Keller notes the literal fulfillment never occurred, posing the interpretive question of what Isaiah is actually prophesying

And of course, this has never happened, we don't think, right? I mean, you know, Jerusalem was rebuilt after the exile, but it wasn't built like that. What is Isaiah foreseeing?

68 · Keller connects Isaiah's jeweled city prophecy to Revelation's vision of the new Jerusalem descending from heaven at the end of time

You have to go to the book of Revelation, where it depicts the end of time, and we see coming out of heaven this city with sapphires, and rubies, and diamonds at its base.

69 · Keller interprets Isaiah and Revelation's vision as God's promise to renew the entire world at the end of time, removing all brokenness and restoring it to original design

And what is that vision all about? What is Isaiah talking about? What is the book of Revelation talking about? It's talking about the fact that at the end of time, this heavenly power will come down to renew the world, create the world to be the way God meant it to be. He didn't want disease. He didn't want suffering. He didn't want death. He didn't want poverty. He didn't want racism. He didn't want injustice. He didn't want any of those things, and in the end, he's going to make the world renewed the way it ought to be.

70 · Keller poses the question of how believers participate in the coming renewal and notes God's consistent pattern of choosing the weak and excluded—eunuchs, barren women, unlikely candidates

How can we participate in that? Through the gospel. Because why do you think God always seems to be working with eunuchs and barren women? Why Sarah over Hagar? Why stupid Jacob over Esau? Why little David over all the big strapping older brothers? Why? Why does he always do that?

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week traces the gospel's three transformative effects—restructured identity, removal of sin through costly grace, and reversal of worldly values—moving from the cultural bondage the gospel frees us from, through the infinite holiness and love that makes grace costly, to the new community Christ creates.

Monday Isaiah 54:1

The command to the barren woman to sing is not sentimental comfort but revolutionary liberation. She has been imprisoned by her culture's verdict that childlessness means worthlessness—yet God calls her to rejoice, declaring her worth does not depend on what she cannot obtain. This passage embodies the gospel's power to free us from every cultural idol, ancient or modern, that whispers we are nothing without its approval.

Tuesday Isaiah 53:5

The piercing and crushing of the servant reveal that forgiveness is not cheap sentiment but infinitely costly action. God did not declare sin away; He bore its penalty Himself, demonstrating that true forgiveness requires the forgiver to suffer the cost. This is why the gospel transforms us in ways moralism never can—we are moved not by duty to a demanding God, but by awe at what a loving God endured to make us His own.

Wednesday Isaiah 54:5

Calling Himself the barren woman's husband reverses her shame and establishes her worth through relationship with God Himself, not through external achievement or possession. This marriage metaphor captures what no other religion offers: an immediate positive verdict and God's delight now, obtained through vows of union rather than performance. We receive a radically new identity that frees us to appreciate cultural goods without enslavement to them.

Thursday Colossians 3

When we grasp that infinite holiness required Christ's death and infinite love made Him glad to die for us, we are stripped of both superiority and inferiority. We see ourselves as sinners loved beyond measure, which produces neither the oscillation of moralism (pride-and-guilt cycle) nor the emptiness of relativism (accepted by no one who matters). This costly grace restructures our hearts to worship with both holy fear and glad confidence.

Friday Isaiah 54:11-14

The prophet paints a vision of a suffering, afflicted people becoming a city of jewels and precious stones—a people without wealth or power becoming more precious than the world's measures of value. This is the gospel's revolutionary reversal: we are not renewed by ascending the world's ladder but by being united to Christ, who descended to the lowest place and rose as Lord. Together, we become a community that delights in Christ's kingdom more than in the idols that enslaved us.

Sunday-evening family table

What Makes You Worth Something?

For the parent

Tim Keller talked about how different cultures tell us our worth comes from different things—ancient cultures said it came from having children, modern culture says it comes from how you look or how much money you have. This prompt invites your family to notice the lies their own culture is telling them about their value, then anchor their identity in something unshakeable.

In the sermon, Pastor Tim said that every culture tries to tell us our worth depends on something—having the right family, looking a certain way, being rich or successful. What's one thing our culture right now says you have to be or have to prove you're worth something? And how would your life change if you believed instead that God already thinks you're worth everything because of what Jesus did for you?
Works for ages 8+. Younger children can listen and share simple examples; teens and adults will engage with deeper reflection on cultural pressure and gospel identity.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. What does Isaiah 53:4-5 say the servant is doing, and what makes his suffering different from ordinary human suffering?
    Isaiah 53:4-5
    → How would you explain to someone unfamiliar with these verses why the servant's willingness to suffer seems to contradict what we know about God's power and authority?
  2. The sermon describes how both ancient and modern cultures enslave people by tying their worth to something they must achieve or obtain. What are the specific idols that your own culture—and perhaps your own heart—whisper will make you valuable?
  3. In Isaiah 54:1, God commands the barren woman to sing and rejoice even though she has no children. Why would this command be so radically countercultural, and what does it reveal about where true worth comes from?
    Isaiah 54:1
    → What would change in your life if you genuinely believed—not just intellectually but in your bones—that your value comes from being loved by God rather than from what you've achieved or obtained?
  4. The sermon contrasts three approaches to God: moralistic religion (earn your way through effort), relativistic spirituality (God accepts everyone), and the gospel. Why does the sermon argue that only the gospel—showing God as both infinitely holy and infinitely loving—has the power to actually transform our hearts?
  5. According to the sermon, what did it cost God to remove our sin, and how does understanding that cost reshape both how we see ourselves and how we relate to Him?
    → The sermon says this produces simultaneous humility and boldness—humility because we recognize our need, boldness because we're loved. Where do you find yourself tempted to swing toward pride on one hand or self-hatred on the other, and how might this gospel truth anchor you in neither extreme?
