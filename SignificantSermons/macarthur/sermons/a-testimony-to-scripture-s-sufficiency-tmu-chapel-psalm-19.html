0 · MacArthur frames his entire ministry and life around one controlling passion: love for the truth, identifying this truth as both the written Word (Scripture) and the incarnate Word (Christ)

The story of my life can can basically be tied to one thing, and that is the love of the truth. Everything's about the truth written, the truth incarnate. You can't divide them because the Word of God is the written word, and the Word of God is also the incarnate Word.

1 · Establishes the supreme authority and dignity of Scripture by asserting that God elevates His Word to equality with His name, making Scripture the necessary instrument for all legitimate worship and service

Psalm 138 says, God has exalted His Word equal to his name. The only way we can truly worship God, serve God, honor God, love God, and proclaim God is to do all of that with His Word.

That's the simplicity of the Christian life. That's why we talk about reading the scripture. Because as you read the Scripture, you are hearing the voice of God and you are hearing him tell you exactly what he wants you to know. You say, well, it's a long book. There seems to be a lot in it. Quit me of hidden faults and keep back your servant from presumptuous sins. Let them not rule over me. Then I will be blameless and I will be acquitted of great transgression.

42 · Synthesizes the five ways Scripture surpasses all else: as possession (more valuable than gold), pleasure (sweeter than honey), protector (warning us), provider (rewarding us), and purifier (keeping us from sin)

What he says Scripture is our greatest possession, more desirable than gold, our greatest pleasure, sweeter than honey, our greatest protector. It warns us, our greatest provider. It rewards us, our greatest purifier. Verses 12 and 13. It keeps you back from presumptuous sins.

43 · Identifies verse 14 as the psalm's conclusion and the proper response to Scripture's nature and effects—that the meditation of the heart and words of the mouth would be acceptable to God

And the sum of it is in verse 14. Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in your sight, O Lord, my rock and my Redeemer.

44 · Poses the interpretive question that will guide the sermon's conclusion—identifying what should occupy the believer's meditation

So what should be the meditation of our heart?

45 · Answers the question about what should be the meditation of our heart by citing Joshua 1:8—Scripture itself should be the meditation, day and night, leading to careful obedience and resulting in success

Let me close with this. Joshua 1:8. I know you know this verse. This book of the law. Scripture shall not depart from your mouth, but you shall meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do according to all that is written in it. For then you will make your way prosperous. Then you will have success.

46 · Closes the sermon with liturgical affirmation

Amen.

47 · Closes in prayer, asking God to give the congregation love for His Word, which is inseparable from love for God Himself

Father, thank you for your word. Give us a love for it, and consequently a love for you. We ask all this in the name of Christ. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we deepen our grasp of Scripture's sufficiency and transforming power by meditating on how God's Word produces salvation, shapes conviction, and calls us to love the revelation of God Himself.

Monday 2 Timothy 3:16-17

Paul declares that Scripture is "profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for training in righteousness," making the believer "complete, equipped for every good work." This sufficiency means we need not supplement God's Word with human philosophy, cultural wisdom, or supplementary revelation—the Bible alone equips us for the full scope of Christian life and ministry. Together, we can rest in the radical simplicity that God has given us everything necessary for salvation and sanctification in one book.

Tuesday Romans 1:16

Paul testifies that the gospel itself—God's Word proclaimed—is the very "power of God for salvation to everyone who believes." Not our eloquence, not our programs, not our cultural accommodation, but the Word of God carries God's own efficacious power to save and transform. This power flows from God's character, not from human deployment; thus our confidence rests entirely on the Word's inherent authority and effect, not on our skill in wielding it.

Wednesday John 20:31

John wrote his gospel "that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name." True saving faith is not mere assent to facts about Jesus—it is believing who He is, what He accomplished, and what that means for us. Understanding the meaning of Scripture is therefore not an academic luxury but the foundation of genuine conversion; superficial familiarity with biblical narratives cannot substitute for deep, comprehending faith in the gospel's truth.

Thursday Joshua 1:8

The Lord commands Joshua: "Keep this Book of the Law always on your lips; meditate on it day and night, so that you may be careful to do all that is written in it." Meditation and obedience are bound together; the goal of dwelling on Scripture is not intellectual mastery but transformed allegiance that flows naturally into obedient action. When truth grips the heart, obedience ceases to be a burden imposed from outside and becomes the glad, habitual response of a conviction that has seized the soul.

Friday Jeremiah 15:16

Jeremiah declares, "When Your words came, I ate them; they were my joy and my heart's delight." The prophet's language moves beyond duty to desire, beyond compliance to celebration—Scripture consumed as the deepest nourishment of the soul. This affection for God's Word is not sentiment but the fruit of encountering God Himself in His revelation; to love what God has said is to love the God who speaks, and such love compels us to meditate on His Word day and night, making it the meditation of our hearts and the words of our mouths.

Sunday-evening family table

What Do We Love More Than Honey?

For the parent

This prompt anchors in the sermon's image from Psalm 19:10—that God's Word is sweeter than honey. Invite your family to think about what they love so much they naturally return to it again and again, then gently ask whether Scripture fits that description in their own lives. Listen for honest answers; the goal is to surface whether Bible reading feels like treasure or obligation.

The psalm says God's Word is sweeter than honey and more desirable than gold. What's something you love so much that you naturally want to come back to it—like a favorite snack, a game, or time with a friend? Do you feel that same pull toward reading the Bible, or does it feel more like a chore? Why do you think that difference exists?
works for ages 7+ — younger children can answer with simple examples; older kids and teens can wrestle with the conviction layer
Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Psalm 19:7-9, the psalmist uses six different names for Scripture—the law, testimony, statutes, commandment, fear of the Lord, and judgments. What does this multiplicity of names suggest about how comprehensive Scripture is meant to be in our lives?
    Psalm 19:7-9
    → Can you think of an area of your life where you've only consulted Scripture partially, as if one name for God's Word were enough, rather than approaching it with this fuller understanding?
  2. The sermon emphasizes that Satan's strategy from the beginning has been to distort Scripture rather than simply deny it. How does this historical reality shape the way we should approach Bible study in our own time, and what does it mean to 'lack biblical knowledge' in MacArthur's terms?
    Ephesians 4:14
  3. MacArthur traces a progression: comprehension → conviction → affection. Where would you honestly place yourself in that progression right now, and what does movement from one stage to the next actually look like in practice?
    → What specific obstacle keeps you from moving deeper—is it time, motivation, confusion about what Scripture means, or something else?
  4. The sermon claims that 'the meaning of Scripture is the Scripture itself'—that misinterpretation is not merely intellectual error but a failure to receive God's revelation. How does that conviction change the way you approach a passage you find confusing or that seems to contradict something else you believe?
    Psalm 119:105
  5. Psalm 19:10-13 describes Scripture as 'more precious than gold' and the foundation for 'keeping back' from transgression. In your own experience, what role does love for God's Word actually play in obedience—is it something you rely on, or do you find yourself striving through willpower instead?
    Psalm 19:10-13
    → If you're relying more on willpower than on affection for Scripture, what would it take for that to shift?
  6. The sermon concludes by calling us to meditate on Scripture day and night, making it 'the meditation of our hearts and the words of our mouths.' Given everything we've discussed, what would one concrete change in your weekly rhythm look like that would move you toward that reality?
    Psalm 1
