Be Filled With the Spirit

Ephesians 5:18 G. Campbell Morgan
Thesis Being filled with the Spirit is not a privilege for the spiritually elite but a divine command for all believers, achievable through total abandonment to God and maintained through obedient abiding in Christ, manifesting not in predictable sensations but in the lived reality of Christ's Lordship.

Full transcript

0 · Morgan opens by correcting a fundamental misconception—that Spirit-filling is an elective spiritual luxury

THEY fall far short of the truth who speak of the filling of the Spirit as the privilege of believers. The word of Paul Be not drunken with wine, wherein is riot, but be filled with the Spirit, is a present imperative, being of the nature of a command, rather than a counsel of perfection. Not merely for an elect few, but for all those born of the Spirit, the will of God is that they should be filled with the Spirit.

1 · Morgan asserts the absolute necessity of Spirit-filling by claiming it is the irreplaceable prerequisite for both genuine Christian character and effective ministry

And the necessity for this filling is proved by the fact that, apart from it, there can be no full Christian life, and no powerful Christian service.

2 · Morgan grounds his argument in 1 Corinthians 12:3, establishing that genuine confession of Jesus as Lord is impossible apart from the Holy Spirit

The apostle declares that no man speaking in the 'Spirit of God saith, Jesus is anathema; and no man can say, Jesus is Lord, but in the Holy Spirit. The Lordship of Jesus is the basis of all Christian life. The Christian graces and virtues all spring from the recognition of that Lordship, and from absolute surrender thereto. It is only as man is born again of the Spirit that he can call Jesus Lord; and it is only as he is under the perfect dominion of that Spirit that he can live under the Lordship of Jesus. Not only is this true with regard to the first step in life, but also in reference to the whole subsequent course.

3 · Morgan catalogs the fruit of the Spirit from Galatians 5:22-23, positioning these qualities as the definitional marks of Christian character that distinguish believers from the world

The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law. These are the evidences of Christian character looked for in all those who profess to belong to Christ, the things that differentiate between a Christly and a worldly soul. There can be no manifestation of them save under the perpetual control of the Spirit.

4 · Morgan extends his necessity argument from character to service, asserting that apparently religious activity without the Spirit's energizing is spiritually impotent

Neither is it possible to work for God except in the energy of the Spirit. There may be a great deal of what appears to be Christian work, but it is absolutely devoid of power unless thus energized. No man can live the Christian life, and no man can serve in the Christian dispensation, save as he is filled with the Spirit.

5 · Morgan pivots from establishing necessity to promising exposition of method

It is, then, of urgent importance that there should be clear understanding of the law which governs this filling.

6 · Morgan shifts into direct pastoral address, naming the painful gap between the Spirit's fruit and the visible reality in many Christian lives

That there are scores of Christian people who are not filled with the Holy Spirit is an all too evident fact. Bring that cluster of the wonderful fruit of the Spirit side by side with the actual life and achievement of scores of professing Christians, and this fact must be at once confessed. To Christian people who really want to be such as God would have them be, who are tired of all that is merely formal and mediocre, and are anxious to live in the will of God at all costs, there is no question of more importance than that of the conditions upon which the believer, born of the Spirit, may live that life which is filled with the Spirit.

7 · Morgan unveils the sermon's central structural framework: a two-stage pathway to Spirit-filling consisting of abandonment (the initial condition) and abiding (the maintaining condition)

These conditions are of a twofold nature,—the initial, and the continuous; that by which blessing is first realized, and that by which it is maintained. The first is that of abandonment. The second is that of abiding.

8 · Morgan defends his choice of the word 'abandonment' over the more common 'consecration,' arguing that the latter has been diluted through misuse

The word abandonment is used intentionally. Consecration is a great word, but it has been so much abused that it has lost much of its deepest significance. This word abandonment is perhaps out of the ordinary run of theological terms, but it is full of force. Wherever whole-hearted, absolute, unquestioning, positive, final abandonment of the life to God obtains, the life becomes filled with the Spirit.

9 · Morgan unpacks abandonment through Romans 6:13, where Paul calls for the presentation of one's entire being to God as an instrument for His purposes

The thought is contained in Paul's words: Neither present your members unto sin as instruments of unrighteousness; but present yourselves unto God, as alive from the dead, and your members as instruments of righteousness unto God. The whole life, according to this conception, is to be handed over to the control of God, in order that, through that life, His will may be realized, His work may be done, His plans may be carried out. That is the abandoned life.

10 · Morgan signals his intention to examine two specific texts that illuminate the nature of abandonment, structuring the next section of his exposition

There are two passages which bear on this subject.

11 · Morgan introduces the first dimension of abandonment through Ephesians 4:30-31: abandonment for purification

The first reads: Grieve not the Holy Spirit of God, in Whom ye were sealed unto the day of redemption. Let all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamor, and railing, be put away from you, with all malice. This is the abandonment of the life for purification. Abandonment to God is not merely the act of enlisting as soldiers to fight battles—that is a secondary matter; it is first the abandonment of self to the Spirit of God, that He may purify and cleanse from everything that is unlike His own perfection of beauty.

12 · Morgan makes a crucial grammatical and theological distinction in Ephesians 4:31—the passive voice 'let them be put away' reveals that purification is the Spirit's work, not self-effort

The apostle did not say: Put away bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamour, and railing. The believer is not called upon to put these things out of the life: that is not the New Testament conception of purification. He said: Let these things be put away. The verse preceding explains the responsibility: Grieve not the Holy Spirit. The work of putting out of the life this unholy brood of evil things—bitterness, wrath, anger, clamour, railing—is not man's work. Man is to let Him accomplish it.

13 · Morgan transitions to his second text illustrating abandonment

The second passage is as familiar as the first:

14 · Morgan unpacks Romans 12:1 as the second dimension of abandonment: abandonment for sacrifice

I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your reasonable service. This is another aspect of abandonment. It is not merely assent to purification; it is also the presentation of the whole being to God for sacrifice. There are very many who seem to imagine that the apostle is calling Christians to sacrifice themselves to God, but he is rather calling upon them to present themselves to God as a sacrifice, which the High Priest will lay upon the altar.

15 · Morgan synthesizes his two texts into a unified definition: abandonment has two inseparable dimensions—purification (yielding to the Spirit's cleansing work) and sacrificial presentation (yielding to Christ's priestly offering of one's life to God)

The abandonment asked for is a twofold one,—first, abandonment to purification by the Spirit; and, secondly, abandonment of the whole being to Jesus Christ, that He may offer it to God.

16 · Morgan pivots from theological definition to lived reality, acknowledging the gulf between comprehending abandonment conceptually and actually practicing it

The theory seems easy. The practice is a very definite thing.

17 · Morgan defines the practical substance of abandonment: the surrender of personal plans, purposes, and hopes in exchange for God's

The life which is thus abandoned to God for the filling of the Spirit is a life that has given up its own plans, and purposes, and hopes; and has taken instead the plan, and the purpose, and the hope of God.

18 · Morgan provides a hypothetical scenario testing abandonment: when God disrupts what seemed to be His own plans for the day, the abandoned believer still embraces His will with joy rather than frustration

If God wills to alter what appear to be Divine arrangements for to-day, so that the desire and the hope of to-day are disappointed, the follower of the Master should yet be able to say: I delight to do Thy will, O my God.''

19 · Morgan exposes a common spiritual error—attempting to negotiate with God—and contrasts it with genuine abandonment where God's will is pursued without self-interested conditions attached

The will of God should be the supreme matter, beyond the doing of which the soul should have no anxiety. How often men promise God that they will do certain things if He will do something for them! —an iniquitous attempt to bargain with the Most High, which is very popular, and as old as Jacob.

20 · Morgan distills the defining difference between Spirit-filled and Spirit-empty lives into a single contrast: total abandonment versus the attempt to serve both self-interest and divine approval simultaneously

The difference between the Spirit-filled life, and the life that is not filled with the Spirit, is the difference between a life abandoned wholly to the will of God, and a life that wants to have its own way and please God too.

21 · Morgan shifts into direct pastoral confrontation, naming the universal human resistance to abandonment

Abandonment is that of which it is most easy to speak, and yet it is the one thing from which all men shrink. Men are quite prepared to sign pledges, to do any amount of work, even to sign cheques or give money, if only God will let them have their own way somewhere in their life. If He will not press this business of abandonment, if He will not bring them to the Cross, they will do anything; but they draw back from the place of death. Yet it is only in that place that the Holy Spirit is able to flow out into every part of the life and energize it, until in all conduct Jesus is crowned Lord, and the fruit of the Spirit is manifest in character.

22 · Morgan dismantles two common spiritual evasions: substituting prayer for obedience and claiming to wait for God's timing

Nothing can take the place of abandonment. Some there are who attempt to put prayer where God has put abandonment. Others profess to be waiting until God is willing to fill them. Both are wrong! While they think they are waiting for God, the fact is God is waiting for them. At any moment, if they yield to the Spirit, He will sweep through every gate and avenue and into every corner of the life.

23 · Morgan transitions from abandonment (the initial condition) to abiding (the continuous condition)

The filling of the Spirit is retained by abiding in Christ. A great deal has been said about abiding, and many have endeavoured to define the term. Some beautiful definitions have been given, mystical and poetical, and yet for the most part out of the reach of the ordinary life of the believer. It is well, where possible, to have definitions of Scripture from Scripture;

24 · Morgan grounds abiding in 1 John 3:24, distilling it to a single, stark equation: abiding equals obedience

and John gives a definition of what it is to abide in Christ: He that keepeth His commandments abideth in Him, and He in him. Nothing can be simpler. The mistake which may be made is that of trying to explain that passage until it is robbed of its simplicity. The definition is the very embodiment of clearness, and may be stated in a brief sentence: To abide is to obey.

25 · Morgan identifies the specific content of Christ's commandment from the preceding context in 1 John: belief in Jesus and love for one another

The commandments referred to are given in the preceding passage; but are spoken of there, as one commandment, having two applications: And this is His commandment, that we should believe in the name of His Son Jesus Christ, and love one another The whole law of Jesus Christ is summed up in that verse. The commandment is that of faith and love.

26 · Morgan unpacks faith as the first dimension of abiding

Faith is the absolute dependence of the soul upon Him, and the consequent life of obedience to Him. Faith in the Lord Jesus begins when a guilty soul submits itself to Him for pardon; but it does not end then. It is not by that one act of faith that men abide, but by continuing in the course begun, by making Him Lord always,—by entering into no transaction of business or of pleasure without taking Him into account; by treating Him as the ever-present King, by believing in Him; and by saying to Him, at all seasons and hours and everywhere: Master, is this Thy will?

27 · Morgan completes his exposition of faith (dependence on Christ for pardon, purity, and direction) and transitions to love as the second dimension of abiding

Faith in Him is belief on His name at the beginning for pardon, and constantly for purity and direction. Then every moment the soul lives in dependence upon Christ, and is able to sing: I dare not take one step without Thy aid. Not faith merely, but love: That we should . . . love one another. That is the life of service.

28 · Morgan synthesizes his exposition of abiding: perpetual faith and perpetual love constitute the continuous condition for maintaining Spirit-filling after the initial abandonment

He that keepeth His commandments abideth in Him. The conditions for abiding in Him are those of always believing in Him, always loving some one and serving some one. If men are filled with the Spirit by abandonment, they continue filled with the Spirit by abiding.

29 · Morgan signals a shift from theological framework (the conditions for Spirit-filling) to experiential reality (what the Spirit-filled life consciously feels like)

While it is true that there can be no full life and no powerful service apart from the filling of the Spirit, it is equally true that the Spirit-filled life must manifest the fruit of the Spirit and be powerful in service for God. These broad principles, however, are granted. The present subject is rather the conscious experience of a soul that is filled with the Spirit.

30 · Morgan issues a critical pastoral warning against the error of expecting specific emotional or physical sensations as proof of Spirit-filling

Here a word of warning is necessary. A vital mistake is made by persons who formulate a code of sensations, and wait for them as evidences of the Spirit's filling. Some expect a magnetic thrill, some an overwhelming ecstasy. These experiences may be realized, they may be utterly absent.

31 · Morgan illustrates the futility of expecting one's experience to replicate another's by referencing well-known spiritual biographies

Others wait for an experience like that of some one else. That they will never have. There are many people who have read the Lives of good men like Fletcher of Madeley, Finney, and Bowen, and who expect to realize just what these men describe. Such hopes are doomed to disappointment.

32 · Morgan asserts the irreducible individuality of Spirit-filled experience

It may safely be said that the experience of the filling of the Spirit is in no two cases exactly identical, any more than the consciousness of ordinary life can ever be the same in any two persons. There are points of resemblance, great fundamental facts which are identical; but in the light and shade there is variety. Surely, if this be true of ordinary life, it is also true of the higher spiritual blessing.

33 · Morgan reiterates the point with scriptural language borrowed from 1 Corinthians 12, affirming unity of source (the same God) alongside diversity of manifestation (different workings)

The Holy Spirit fills one and another. The realization of the one differs from that of the other. There are diversities of workings, but the same God.

34 · Morgan identifies the universal constant in Spirit-filled experience: heightened consciousness of Christ

There is, however, a common consciousness to those who are Spirit-filled. It is the consciousness of Christ. The Holy Spirit, coming in His fulness, will give men to know the Lord as they never knew Him before.

35 · Morgan develops an ecclesiological corollary to his argument about experiential diversity

The consciousness of Christ in the experience of believers will be as varied as are the saints themselves; for the full consciousness of the Head can only be realized by the whole Church. His greatness is such that He cannot give Himself wholly and utterly and finally to an individual; He needs the whole Church for the display of His perfect glory, and the unfolding of the majesty of His Person. Let no one narrow down his consciousness of the Christ to the consciousness of any single person. He is one thing to one man, He is another thing to another, but the men are united in the fact that it is the Master of Whom they are all conscious by the Spirit.

36 · Morgan details the practical outcomes of Christ-consciousness in the Spirit-filled life: participation in Christ's victory, adoption of Christ's perspective on humanity, and reproduction of Christ's self-giving passion in service

The Lordship of Jesus as a reality, is the first result of the Spirit-filled life. It follows that Christ's victory over evil will be shared by His people; His point of vision of the affairs of men and the needs of men will be theirs also; and the impulses of service which bore Him to Calvary, against all opposition, and made Him Victor in its darkest hour, will likewise be their impulse of service, so that no longer will they offer Him the service of mechanical arrangement; but in the passion of His life they will serve, even though that be a consuming passion, as it was with Him.

37 · Morgan articulates the missional dimension of Spirit-filling: believers become instrumental in revealing God to others by bearing Christ's likeness

Again, Christ's revelation of God to men will in measure be their revelation of God to men. As the Spirit fills the children of God, He will reproduce in their lives such likeness to Christ, that men, seeing them, will begin to understand Him, and be led into a clear apprehension of the glory of the Father.

38 · Morgan concludes by driving the entire sermon toward personal decision

This subject brings all to the point of personal responsibility. The whole study culminates here for the individual. That Divine Spirit Who worked in creation, who was the Spirit of revelation and of service through every age, dwells now in each believer. The individual question is whether He is indwelling in all His fulness. Or is He grieved and quenched by disloyalty to His government? If that has been the case hitherto, let the whole life be yielded to Him, that He may reproduce the Master Himself, to the glory of God, and for the good of men.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through the Spirit-filled life: from its necessity for all believers, through the dual pathway of abandonment and abiding, to the invariant reality of deepened Christ-consciousness that marks every Spirit-filled life regardless of emotional accompaniment.

Monday Romans 6:13

Paul commands us to present ourselves to God as instruments of righteousness—a summons that presupposes the Spirit's empowering filling. To remain unfilled is to withhold from God the very instrument He calls us to yield; we cannot serve His purposes with authenticity or power while reserving portions of our will for ourselves. This is why Spirit-filling is not luxury but necessity for any believer who desires to live and labor as God intends.

Tuesday Psalm 40:8

The psalmist's cry—'I delight to do Your will, O my God'—expresses the heart of abandonment: a surrender so complete that obedience becomes joy rather than burden. This delight signals the Spirit's work of purification within us; as He removes the resistance and self-centeredness that block our union with Christ, our wills increasingly align with His own, and service transforms from grim duty into willing sacrifice. In this alignment, we discover that our deepest freedom lies in Christ's bondage.

Wednesday 1 John 3:23-24

John fuses belief in Christ with love for one another as the twin conditions of abiding in the Spirit; neither can be neglected without fracturing our connection to God's indwelling presence. The Spirit-filled life is not a static achievement but an active, relational persistence in trusting Christ and loving our brothers and sisters. When we falter in either faith or love, we grieve the Spirit and diminish His work; when we maintain both, we remain open to His continuous filling and transformation.

Thursday 1 Corinthians 12:3

Only by the Spirit can anyone genuinely confess 'Jesus is Lord'—a declaration that points not to a moment of ecstatic feeling but to an inward conviction of Christ's supremacy that reshapes how we see and serve Him. While the Spirit's work manifests differently in each of us, this deepened, lived awareness of Christ's Lordship remains the constant mark; we may experience the Spirit's filling in diverse ways, yet all authentic fillings produce a sharpened vision of who Jesus is and what His reign demands of us.

Friday Ephesians 4:30-31

Paul's warning against grieving the Spirit through bitterness, wrath, and slander unveils the core of unfilled living—we cling to emotional reactivity and relational hostility while expecting to maintain a façade of Christian identity. To grieve the Spirit is to resist the very One who would purify us of these divisions and grant us the grace to love as Christ loved. This week, ask yourself: am I waiting for God, or is God waiting for me to surrender the autonomy I've been protecting? The Spirit stands ready; the question is whether we will finally abandon our resistance and receive His filling.
