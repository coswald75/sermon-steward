But it's not canceling sin with no entailment on the wrath of God. Of course, it turns aside the wrath of God and makes God propitious where he was over against us in wrath, in righteous wrath before.

76 · Carson provides his final translation of verse 25a incorporating his exegesis, showing how God presented Christ as simultaneously turning aside wrath and canceling sin through blood sacrifice received by faith, bringing together all the theological components he has established

So what does the text say? God presented Christ as a sacrifice of atonement, a sacrifice that turned aside the wrath of God. and cancel sin through the shedding of his blood, through his death, and this to be received by faith.

In other words, what Paul does is set forth the source of God's righteousness, the righteousness that we need to be acceptable before him, to make us acceptable before him on the last day. He sets forth the source of this righteousness, in God's gracious provision of Christ Jesus as the sacrifice of atonement for our sins.

78 · Carson introduces a common illustration of substitutionary atonement—a judge who steps down to take the condemned's place—admitting he himself used this illustration in the past before recognizing its theological inadequacy, setting up a critique of the metaphor's limitations

You see, I think that one of the reasons why we sometimes have trouble with this is because of the metaphors and illustrations we sometimes use to get this idea across. Have you ever explained the gospel to a friend along the following lines? I have. I don't anymore, but But I have in the past. We say it's like a judge who when he sees the thug before him pronounces guilty and pronounces the sentence and then steps down off the bench, takes off his judicial robes and takes the place of the condemned, whether to write out a check for five thousand dollars or go to jail for six months or whatever it is. He takes the place of the condemned. And so we say that's what God in Christ has done. He he pronounces the guilty and then takes the punishment himself.

79 · Carson critiques the judge illustration by showing it fails when mapped onto Western legal systems where judges are neutral administrators of law rather than the offended party, making substitution unjust since judges must treat all defendants equally—if one deserves substitution, all do

Now, the problem with this illustration is that in any judicial system in a civilized world, that really doesn't work. Because, you see, in our system, if a judge did that, he or she would be unjust. In our world, a judge is an administrator of a bigger system, namely the law. you offend against the law, or you offend against the state, or in Britain you offend against the crown. And the judge is merely an administrator, hopefully a neutral administrator, of this larger system. And because he's a neutral administrator, he's got to handle everybody the same way. If he stands in for somebody who deserves to be punished, then he needs to do it for everybody. He's supposed to be a neutral administrator of the system and even-handed in all that he does. Do you see? Which is why the illustration looks a bit silly. When you think of American law, or British law, or Canadian law, or Western law in general, it looks a bit silly.

80 · Carson identifies a second failure of the judge illustration—that judges must recuse themselves when they are the victim, making it unjust to be both offended party and judge, which further demonstrates the metaphor's inadequacy for explaining divine justice

Not only so, but the judge is supposed to recuse himself if he has any connection with the thug. Supposing the thug has been arrested because he in fact bodily attacked the judge some months earlier. When the judge recognizes that the thug is the one that attacked him, he's supposed to recuse himself because he cannot be both victim and judge. That would be unjust.

81 · Carson contrasts God's judicial role with human judges—God is always the most offended party (Psalm 51:4), never recuses Himself because His justice is perfect, and is not a neutral administrator of a system bigger than Himself but the source of law, showing why the judge metaphor fundamentally fails

But you see, God is the one who was attacked. God is the most offended party. Against you only have I sinned and done this evil in your sight, David says quite rightly in Psalm 51. God is always the most offended party. You cheat on your income tax this coming April. The most offended party is not Uncle Sam. It's God. You cheat on your spouse. You blow up at your kids with your bad temper. The most offended party is not your spouse or your kids. It's God. And God never recuses Himself. Because His justice is perfect. He never recuses Himself. And He is not only the most offended party, but He is not a neutral arbitrator in a bigger system. It's not as if he's the administrator of a law structure that is bigger than he is. He's given the law structure. He's the offended party. He's at all.

82 · Carson concludes his critique by acknowledging that human illustrations can gesture toward substitution but ultimately fail at crucial theological points, then restates the biblical truth—God as offended party stands in wrath yet provides righteousness in Christ received by faith out of grace

It's why human illustrations ultimately, though they show something of substitution, they fail. They fail at crucial points. God. is the offended party. He stands over against us in righteous wrath. But out of sheer grace, he loves men and women from every tongue and tribe and people and nation, such that the righteousness that he provides in Christ is received by faith.

And finally, Paul demonstrates Paul establishes, Paul sets forth, rather, the demonstration of the righteousness of God through the cross of Jesus Christ. 25b to 26.

Now, let me repeat that again. He sets forth the demonstration of the righteousness of God through the cross of Jesus Christ. Now, once again, there is a nest of translation challenges in this verse and a half. I think that this NIV has it right. I won't go through the debates, they would take too long, and it's just about time for a break. There will be a Q&A at the end of the second session, not this first session, but there will be a Q&A at the end of the second session. Let me tell you what I think this verse and a half means.

85 · Carson expounds verses 25b-26a, arguing that God's primary purpose in the cross was to demonstrate His own righteousness, not merely to save sinners, and that 'sins committed beforehand' refers specifically to Old Testament believers' sins that were left ultimately unpunished under the old covenant

God did this, 25b, that is, this refers to the setting forth of Christ, presenting Christ as a sacrifice of atonement. God did this, we are told, to demonstrate his righteousness. Not first and foremost to save us, though he does that too, but to demonstrate his righteousness because in his forbearance he had left the sins committed beforehand unpunished. That is, the sins to which he is referring, as far as I can see, are the sins of God's covenant people in the Old Testament. The sins committed beforehand, before the now, before the coming of Christ, before his crossword.

86 · Carson elaborates on how Old Testament punishments (exile, David's throne loss) were temporal rather than ultimate, then raises the theological question of Moses and Elijah's sins—they appear on the Mount of Transfiguration in glory, but who paid for their sins since Old Covenant sacrifices were insufficient?

You see, in some ways, the sins of the Old Covenant were met with certain kinds of punishment, people being sent off into exile, David being forced off his throne for a while and so on. But they weren't dealt with in any final way. They really weren't dealt with in any final way. Yes, Moses didn't get into the promised land, but that's not dealing with sin in the final way in which in which in which they're dealt with in scripture as a whole, in which they're dealt with under the terms of the New Covenant. In fact, the sins dealt with beforehand were left in some ultimate sense unpunished. We know that Moses and Elijah are swept into the presence of the living God. They joined Jesus on the Mount of Transfiguration, but they committed sins. Who paid for them?

87 · Carson answers his own question by citing Hebrews 10:4 to establish that animal sacrifices were insufficient, then argues that Christ's sacrifice demonstrates God's righteousness by retroactively paying for Old Testament believers' sins, vindicating God's acceptance of them despite their unpunished sins

When you recall what the New Testament says that in the last analysis, the blood of bull and goat can't take away human sin. Who pays for them? Well, the text says, One of the purposes, the central purposes of God's presentation of Christ as this sacrifice of atonement is to demonstrate his righteousness. That is the righteousness of God with respect to his people in the Old Testament who were acceptable before him, even though their sins were unpunished. Ultimately, it's Christ's sacrifice that pays for those sins committed beforehand.

88 · Carson explicates the final clause of verse 26, showing how the cross demonstrates God's righteousness at the present time by enabling God to be simultaneously just (not overlooking sin) and justifier (declaring the unjust to be just), resolving the apparent contradiction

He did this to demonstrate righteousness because in his forbearance he had left the sins committed beforehand unpunished. He did this to demonstrate his righteousness at the present time so as to be just and the one who justifies those who have faith in Jesus. To be simultaneously just. He's not pretending that the sins didn't happen. They're paid for. while declaring just those who in fact on their own accounting are not just.

89 · Carson contrasts Islamic and Christian understandings of forgiveness, arguing that Allah's forgiveness by decree without payment makes Allah corrupt from a Christian perspective, whereas the biblical God's righteousness requires sin to be addressed through payment, establishing Christianity's moral superiority in handling the sin problem

Do you know this is one of the greatest differences between Allah in Islam and Christianity? Allah forgives sins and the devout Muslim hopes that he's been good enough to earn Allah's favor. But no Muslim thinks of himself as perfect. So if Allah forgives some sinners and not other sinners, presumably because some sinners are worse than others or better than others, who nevertheless pays for those sins? The answer is Nobody. Nobody. Allah just decides by decree to forgive them. Which from a Christian point of view means that Allah is corrupt. He hasn't dealt with sin. Whereas the Bible presents the God of the Bible as so terribly righteous that sin must be addressed for any human being to be declared just and therefore acceptable in God's presence.

And that has been secured in such a way that God is shown to be just. And the one who justifies the ungodly. And that's at the heart of the cross. This is not something we could have done for ourselves. That's why we're told in verse 24, all of these people from different tongues and tribes and peoples and nations are justified freely by His grace. Sola gratia.

The answer is the cross where love and holiness meet without compromise, simultaneously demonstrating justice and justifying the ungodly, resolving the 'wretched dilemma' through shattering grace

Or to put it in poetic form, when you remember that God stands over against us both in righteous wrath and in love. Dilemma wretched. How shall holiness of brilliant light unshaded tolerate rebellions that it's slimy, not bathed in its own glory, compromised at best? Dilemma wretched. How can truth attest that God is love and not be shamed by hate and wills enslaved and bitter death, the freight of curse deserve the human rebels and mess? The cross. The cross. The sacred meeting place where, knowing neither compromise nor loss, God's love and holiness in shattering grace, the great dilemma slays. The cross. The cross. The loving God whose dear Son dies by this is just. and one who justifies.

Let us pray. Entus we beg of you, Heavenly Father, real clarity in our minds as to what the cross achieved a better understanding of your matchless holiness and spectacular redeeming love, coming together in the crosswork of Christ by which your own people have been redeemed. We bless you that the cross demonstrates your justice, that you are just and the one who justifies ungodly image bearers like us. Help us to drink deeply from the wellsprings of grace. and utterly reject any self-promotion, any of the self-righteousness of the proud in the temple who claim they are not like other people are. But grant rather that we may come before you in full recognition again and again and again and again, that we are saved by grace. For Jesus' sake. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc of God's righteousness revealed in the gospel: from the universal problem of sin that equally condemns all (Monday), through the Old Testament's preparatory witness (Tuesday), to the cross where God's wrath and love converge (Wednesday-Thursday), and finally to the assurance and freedom that flow from justification by grace alone (Friday).

Monday Romans 1:18-3:20

Paul's indictment stretches across the entire human race—Gentiles suppress God's truth in unrighteousness, Jews possess the law yet fail to keep it—establishing that the problem of sin is universal and without remedy in human effort. As we grasp the weight of this condemnation, the subsequent announcement of righteousness apart from the law becomes not merely good news but the only hope for acceptance before God. The gospel only makes sense when we see ourselves in this mirror.

Tuesday Exodus 12

God's redemption of Israel from Egyptian slavery—secured not by their strength but by His substitutionary lamb and His blood applied—establishes the pattern that runs through all Scripture: deliverance comes through grace alone, purchased by a life given. This ancient Passover was itself a prophecy in action, teaching God's people that acceptance and freedom flow from a covenant sacrifice, not from human achievement. We see in this type the very heart of the gospel to come.

Wednesday Leviticus 16

On the Day of Atonement, the blood of the offering both turned aside God's wrath and cleansed from sin—two realities that cannot be separated. Christ's sacrifice fulfills this type completely: His blood cancels our guilt and simultaneously satisfies the righteous judgment our sin provoked, making it possible for a holy God to declare ungodly sinners just. In the cross, God's wrath against sin and His love toward sinners reach their most intense expression together, not in sequence.

Thursday Psalm 51:4

David's confession—'Against You, You only, have I sinned and done what is evil in Your sight'—reminds us that God is always the most offended party, yet He alone has the authority and power to require payment and to provide it. Unlike human judges who must recuse themselves when personally wronged, God stands as both the wounded judge and the gracious provider of redemption, a paradox no earthly illustration can capture. In this lies the astounding revelation of Romans 3:26: God is just and the justifier simultaneously.

Friday 1 John 5:13

John writes to those who believe 'so that you may know that you have eternal life'—not to achieve it, but to know it as a present reality grounded in Christ's finished work. When God declares us righteous now through faith in His Son, He pronounces the very verdict that will be confirmed at the final judgment; there is no hidden indictment waiting to surprise us. The gospel compels us to embrace the assurance that is ours by grace—we are accepted, pardoned, and beloved in Christ today and forever.
