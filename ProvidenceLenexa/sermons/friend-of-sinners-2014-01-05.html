8 · The pastor provides historical-cultural background on the Roman tax system, explaining the three types of taxes and focusing on the customs tax that Levi collected—the most hated of the three

Levi, we are told, is a tax collector. Now, tax collectors were not the most popular guys in town back then, just as they are today. Not much has changed in a couple thousand years. There were 3 kinds of taxes, though, that the Jews had. Imposed upon them under the Roman Empire. There was a land tax of roughly 10% of their harvest. The second tax was a poll tax that was collected once a year, and it was roughly equivalent to about a day's wage. And then there was a third tax. It was a customs tax that was levied on goods and merchandise. So there would be collection points set up along trade routes or at entries and exits into the towns and villages. It was kind of like a toll. So if you're familiar with driving on I-70 going west towards Topeka, that's a toll road. Or if you take I-35 south down towards Wichita, that's a toll road. And if you've driven those, you every so often, every certain number of miles, you have to stop, come up to a toll booth, and give someone some money so that you can keep on progressing on the road. And that's what this tax was, this customs tax was. There would be booths along the roads where you'd have to stop and pay a tax. It wasn't exorbitant. It was typically in the 2% to 5% range. And Levi was one of these customs tax collectors who would work at these toll booths. And this is the tax that the people hated the most.

9 · The pastor explains the Roman tax-farming system in detail, showing how the upfront payment structure created strong financial incentives for tax collectors to over-collect and defraud, contextualizing Levi's profession within a corrupt system

Not only did these tax collectors take your money, they weren't necessarily nice about doing it. Or in the process of taxing you. So here's how it typically worked back then. At the time of Jesus, the Roman Empire was no longer in the business of actually collecting their own taxes. They didn't want the hassle of doing this, so they would contract it out. So the way that it worked is that an individual— it would have to be a wealthy individual or perhaps a group of individuals— would bid on collecting a tax. This customs tax, this tax in an area. And the highest bidder would win the right to collect the tax. What was different about this tax is though that when you bid on it, you had to pay Rome upfront. So it's likely that Levi was a part of an investment firm, for lack of a better word, who would bid on the right to collect taxes. So this group that Levi was a part of that was a part of might say bid $1 million to collect taxes in the area around Capernaum. And if that was the highest bid, Rome would give them the contract and then they would pay Rome upfront the $1 million. The chief tax collector of this group, of this investment firm, would now not only want to collect enough money, enough taxes to recoup their $1 million, but a little bit extra to make some profits. So maybe they would set a goal of collecting $2 million. And obviously they would have some employees who would then collect the taxes. So if there were 10 individuals working in this investment firm, each one would be given a goal of collecting around $200,000. And it's very likely that each one of these individuals not only wanted to collect their salary but might want to pad it a bit, so they could set an individual goal of $250,000. And they would go out and and spend the year trying to collect the money from individuals as they went by.

And understandably then, there were many reasons that these tax collectors were hated in the community. They were despised as well, since they were seen as being partners with the Roman Empire. So the tax collectors were viewed as sellouts. They were almost always thieves and swindlers. They could confiscate your goods if you didn't declare them properly. They could make false accusations against you and threaten you. They had the power to determine how much your goods were worth. The tax collector had all the know-how, they had all the power, and that made for a system of corruption. So this all created a great incentive for these tax collectors to be cheats, to be frauds, to steal, to be swindlers. And there was likely nothing that could be done to stop them. We say that we want to make disciples, but who of us is willing to step forward and disciple the single mom who is living in a shelter, struggling to find and keep a job, to find a place that she and her daughter can live so they don't have to move from shelter to shelter to shelter? Who will welcome that awkward couple into their care group, the one that doesn't quite understand all the social dynamics of being a part of a Christian community, who isn't mature as Christians as we perceive ourselves to be. You go out of your way to approach that person in the lobby who you know that if you ask them, "How are you doing this morning?" you're going to be there for a while.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how Jesus' mission to befriend sinners reshapes our understanding of grace, repentance, community, and the cost of following Him.

Monday Mark 1:15

Jesus summarizes His entire mission in these words: the kingdom of God has come near, and we must repent and believe. This is the gospel framework that makes sense of why Jesus called a tax collector and dined with sinners—not to celebrate their sin, but to summon them to radical turning toward God. As we follow Jesus faithfully, we inherit this same mission: to announce the kingdom's arrival and call those far from God to come home.

Tuesday Mark 1:15

The proclamation 'repent and believe the gospel' is not an afterthought to Jesus' table fellowship—it is the heart of it. When Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners, He did so precisely because they needed to hear this message of turning and faith. We honor Jesus' example when we welcome the broken and lost into our communities not by affirming their sin, but by offering them the same call to repentance and the same good news that transformed Levi.

Wednesday Mark 1:15

Notice that Jesus' message centers on believing the gospel—not on perfect doctrinal knowledge before discipleship begins. Levi rose from his tax collector's booth and followed Jesus with minimal understanding but maximum responsiveness to what he had witnessed of Jesus' character and authority. This pattern invites us to welcome others into our community at the beginning of their faith journey, trusting that the gospel itself has power to transform and teach.

Thursday Mark 1:15

The proclamation of the gospel and the summons to repent always begin with God's initiative. Jesus did not wait for Levi to clean up his life or prove his worth—He called him as he was. This is the gospel we preach to one another: we are pursued by a grace more astounding than any response we could muster, and this grace compels our repentance and faith. When we welcome sinners as Jesus did, we mirror this gracious initiative that seeks before it calls.

Friday Mark 1:15

Jesus' proclamation of repentance and belief was never sterile or distant—it came through table fellowship, presence, and genuine friendship with the despised and broken. Our faithfulness to Him means we cannot hide our gospel light behind walls of respectability or comfort; we must be willing to be seen with sinners, to extend hospitality even when it costs us reputation, and to offer both the warmth of welcome and the clarity of the gospel call. The kingdom has drawn near; let it draw near through us.

Pray together this week

A Prayer to Welcome Sinners as Jesus Did

Father, we come before You in awe of Jesus, who came not to call the righteous but sinners to repentance (Luke 5:32). We marvel at His willingness to befriend the despised, to share meals with the broken, and to see worth in those society rejected. We confess that our hearts do not easily follow His example. We are tempted to insulate ourselves from sinners, to fear contamination, to extend hospitality only to those who already look and believe like us. We hesitate to be seen with the messy and morally compromised, revealing that we do not truly trust the power of the gospel to transform lives, nor do we grasp the grace that saved us.

Yet in the gospel we have been freed from the very self-righteousness that blinded the Pharisees. Christ ate with sinners not because He condoned their sin but because He came to seek and save the lost (Luke 5:32). His mission was clear: to call us to repentance through genuine fellowship and truth spoken in love. In Him, we have tasted the grace that welcomes the unwelcome, and that same grace now flows through us to those around us.

Give us courage, O Lord, to welcome tax collectors and sinners into our midst as Jesus did. Soften our hearts toward those whom society dismisses. Grant us wisdom to speak truth while sharing genuine love, to maintain holiness while extending hospitality, to pursue the lost without fear of being seen with them. Make Providence Community Church a place where the broken, the despised, and the morally compromised feel the warmth of Christ's welcome. We commit ourselves to follow Jesus faithfully in this, knowing that His mission becomes ours, and His grace is sufficient for us. To Him be all glory and praise.
