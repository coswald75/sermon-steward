5-day reading plan

This week we walk through death's schoolhouse: how confronting mortality awakens us to reality, reorients our priorities, and fixes our hope on Christ's resurrection and our own transformation.

Monday Ecclesiastes 7:2

The preacher's assertion rests on a simple, terrible truth: a house of mourning strips away illusion. When we stand at a funeral, pretense evaporates—we cannot ignore that our days are numbered, that we will stand before God, that the trivial pursuits consuming our weeks are revealed as hollow. This is wisdom's gift, granted through sorrow.

Tuesday Luke 12:16-21

The rich fool had everything the world calls success—abundance, security, years of ease stretching ahead. Yet he never asked the one question death forces: "Then what?" He built larger barns; God required his soul that night. His folly was not wealth but blindness to brevity. We escape his ruin only by laying death's reality to heart now, while we still have days to redirect toward what endures.

Wednesday 1 Corinthians 15:53

Paul's language—"this perishable must put on the imperishable"—speaks not of annihilation but of metamorphosis. Our mortal flesh, subject to decay, will be exchanged for a body incorruptible, fitted for eternity. This is not escape from embodiment but its perfection. The gospel does not mock our horror at death; it absorbs that horror into Christ's victory and promises us a garment of glory.

Thursday Philippians 1:21-23

Paul, facing his own death, does not wring his hands. Instead, he names a paradox: remaining to serve the church is necessary and good, yet departure into Christ's presence is "far better." This is not morbidity but realism. When we consider death seriously, we begin to grasp what Paul grasps—that this life, for all its importance, is not our ultimate home. That realization steadies us and loosens the world's grip.

Friday Hebrews 12:1-2

The writer calls us to lay aside the weight that slows us—and surely mortality anxiety, if unanchored to Christ, is such a weight. But the antidote is not denial of death; it is fixing our gaze on Jesus, who has already passed through death and emerged crowned in glory. As we live "every moment on the precipice of eternity," we do not live in terror but in the glad assurance that He who conquered death walks with us, and that our race, though brief, is run in His presence.

Sunday-evening family table

What a Funeral Teaches Us

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to reflect on why funerals matter—not as sad events to avoid, but as reminders that help us live better. Listen for how your kids think about death and what matters most; use their answers to gently point them toward Jesus and eternity.

Today we heard that going to a funeral is actually better for us than going to a party. That sounds strange, right? But the preacher said funerals remind us that life doesn't go on forever—and that makes us think about what really matters. Can you think of a time you realized something was important because it wouldn't last forever? What was it, and what did you do differently because of it?
Works for ages 7+ — younger children may need help thinking of an example, but can understand the core idea that knowing something ends helps us care about it more

Ecclesiastes 7:2

It is better to go to the house of mourning than to go to the house of feasting, for this is the end of all mankind, and the living will lay it to heart.

Why this verse: This verse is the textual anchor of the sermon and encapsulates its central claim: that confronting death through the house of mourning produces wisdom by forcing us to face reality and lay eternity to heart.
