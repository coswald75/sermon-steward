Christus Victor Does Not Need Help

Paul's letter to the Colossians confronts a perennial threat to Christian freedom: the parasitic voice that says 'Christ is good, but there's a little bit more.' This scraps page widens the horizon around the sermon's central claim—that Christ's victory is complete and sufficient—by tracing the biblical, historical, and philosophical threads that intersect Paul's argument.

A scholar's companion to the sermon preached by Chris Oswald on Colossians 2:1-23, Dec 14, 2025. ← Return to the sermon

When & Where

Historical & Literary Context

Colossians was written around AD 60-62, likely from Rome during Paul's imprisonment. The letter addresses a church Paul had not personally founded, located in the Lycus Valley of Asia Minor alongside Laodicea and Hierapolis. Colossae was a declining city, once prosperous but now overshadowed by its neighbors. The church faced pressure from a syncretistic teaching that blended Jewish practices (circumcision, food laws, Sabbath observance) with pagan mysticism (worship of angels, visionary experiences, ascetic practices). This 'Colossian heresy' was not a frontal assault on Christ but a subtle parasitism—it honored Jesus while suggesting believers needed supplemental revelation and purification strategies. The cultural context was pluralistic: Jewish communities maintained strong identity markers, Greek philosophy (especially Stoicism and Platonism) shaped intellectual life, and mystery religions promised secret knowledge and spiritual transformation. Paul's letter sits at a literary hinge: chapters 1-2 establish Christ's cosmic supremacy and complete work; chapters 3-4 draw out ethical implications. Chapter 2 specifically dismantles the false teaching by asserting Christ's sufficiency in revelation (verses 2-3, 9) and purification (verses 11-15), then warning against the stoicheia—the cultural-spiritual powers that animate alternative religious systems (verses 8, 20).

Key terms

  • stoicheia — Greek for 'elements' or 'elemental principles'—refers both to the basic building blocks of reality and to the cultural-spiritual powers that govern pre-Christian religious systems. Paul uses it to describe the fundamental religious strategies humans construct to answer questions about God's nature and how to approach Him. These are both human traditions and spiritual strongholds.
  • pleroma — Greek for 'fullness'—Paul uses it in verse 9 to declare that 'in Christ all the fullness of deity dwells bodily.' This term counters Gnostic speculation that divine fullness was distributed across multiple emanations or intermediaries. Paul insists the totality of God's nature is concentrated in the incarnate Christ, requiring no supplemental revelation.
  • apekduomai — Greek for 'stripping off' or 'disarming'—used in verse 15 to describe Christ's public humiliation of the powers and authorities. The term carries the image of a victor stripping armor from defeated enemies, displaying them as conquered in his triumphal procession. It emphasizes the completeness and public nature of Christ's victory.
Across the canon

Cross-References

Genesis 3:15

God declares that the seed of the woman will crush the serpent's head, though the serpent will strike his heel.

Connection

This protoevangelium is the seed of the Christus Victor model. Paul sees Christ's work as the fulfillment of this ancient promise—Jesus, the seed of the woman, crushes Satan through his death and resurrection. The 'striking of the heel' foreshadows the cross; the 'crushing of the head' describes the decisive victory Paul celebrates in Colossians 2:15. All subsequent spiritual warfare stems from this foundational promise that God's Anointed will defeat the usurper.

Psalm 110:1-2

The LORD says to my Lord, 'Sit at my right hand until I make your enemies your footstool.'

Connection

This royal psalm shapes Paul's understanding of Christ's ongoing reign. Colossians 2 describes Christ as currently enthroned, having triumphed over his enemies on the cross. The 'footstool' image parallels Paul's assertion that Christ has disarmed the powers. The psalm's future tense ('until I make') corresponds to the already-but-not-yet tension in Paul's thought: Christ's victory is decisive, yet enemies still resist until the final subjugation. The Colossian false teachers represent forces that must be made Christ's footstool.

Ezekiel 36:25-27

God promises to sprinkle clean water, give a new heart, and put His Spirit within His people, causing them to walk in His statutes.

Connection

Paul's argument in Colossians 2:11-13 about spiritual circumcision and being made alive echoes Ezekiel's prophecy of interior transformation. Where the stoicheia operate through external purification (food laws, rituals), God's covenant promise is radical interiority—a heart transplant accomplished by the Spirit. Paul connects this to baptism, the sign of the new covenant, and declares it fulfilled in Christ. The false teachers in Colossae were reverting to the old covenant's external markers, missing that Christ had accomplished the interior reality Ezekiel foresaw.

Galatians 4:8-11

Paul warns the Galatians that returning to observance of days, months, seasons, and years is a return to slavery under the weak and worthless elementary principles (stoicheia).

Connection

This passage provides a direct parallel to Colossians 2:16-20. In both letters, Paul confronts believers who, having been freed from the stoicheia through Christ, are tempted to return to them through calendrical and dietary observance. The Galatian context clarifies that the stoicheia are not merely neutral cultural practices but spiritual powers that enslave. Paul's pastoral alarm in both letters stems from the same concern: these practices imply Christ's work was incomplete, requiring supplemental purification strategies to maintain covenant standing.

Hebrews 10:11-14

The passage contrasts the daily sacrifices of priests, which can never take away sins, with Christ's single sacrifice that has perfected forever those who are being sanctified.

Connection

The author of Hebrews shares Paul's concern in Colossians about the finality of Christ's purification work. Both writers confront the temptation to supplement Christ with ongoing purification rituals. The 'once for all' language in Hebrews matches Paul's insistence in Colossians 2:13-15 that Christ's work is complete. The Colossian ascetic practices (verse 23) and the temptation to return to Levitical sacrifices both represent the stoicheia's approach—repeated external acts to maintain cleanness. Against this, both authors declare Christ's single act has accomplished perfect and permanent purification.

2 Corinthians 10:3-5

Paul describes weapons of warfare that destroy strongholds, arguments, and every lofty opinion raised against the knowledge of God, taking every thought captive to obey Christ.

Connection

This passage illuminates Paul's understanding of the stoicheia as both human reasoning and spiritual strongholds. In Colossians 2:8, Paul warns against 'philosophy and empty deceit'—human traditions that constitute intellectual fortresses against Christ's supremacy. The 2 Corinthians passage reveals that spiritual warfare involves demolishing these thought-systems. The Colossian false teaching was precisely such a stronghold: a sophisticated religious philosophy that appeared wise (Col 2:23) but opposed the knowledge of God revealed in Christ. Paul's call to 'take every thought captive' parallels his warning in Colossians to reject any teaching that does not hold fast to Christ as the head.

Twenty centuries of witness

Voices from the Tradition

Irenaeus of Lyons · c. 130–202

Against Heresies, especially Book 5 on Christ's recapitulation and victory

Irenaeus developed the recapitulation theory, arguing that Christ reversed Adam's disobedience through his obedience, and in doing so defeated the powers that held humanity captive. He emphasized that Christ's incarnation, life, death, and resurrection constitute a complete victory over Satan and all hostile powers, restoring humanity to communion with God.

Connection

Irenaeus provides the earliest systematic articulation of themes present in Colossians 2. His insistence that Christ's victory is complete and requires no Gnostic supplements directly parallels Paul's argument. Irenaeus faced the same challenge Paul addressed: teachers who claimed Christ needed supplementing with secret knowledge or additional spiritual intermediaries. His recapitulation framework shows how Christ's work encompasses both revelation (showing us the true God) and purification (defeating the powers that enslaved us)—the two dimensions Paul defends in Colossians 2.

Athanasius of Alexandria · c. 296–373

On the Incarnation, particularly sections on Christ's defeat of death and demons

Athanasius argues that the incarnation was necessary for humanity's redemption because only God could defeat death and restore the divine image in humanity. He describes Christ's death as a cosmic victory over the devil, who held humanity in bondage through the fear of death. The resurrection is the public proof that death has been trampled and the devil's power broken.

Connection

Athanasius's work expands Paul's triumphalist language in Colossians 2:15. Where Paul declares Christ disarmed the powers and made a public spectacle of them, Athanasius explains the mechanism: the incarnate Word entered death's domain and destroyed it from within. His emphasis on the incarnation as necessary for victory resonates with Paul's insistence in Colossians 2:9 that 'in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily'—it is precisely the God-man who accomplishes the victory. Athanasius also shares Paul's concern about supplemental spiritualities, as his entire career was spent defending Christ's full deity against Arian claims that Christ was a lesser intermediary.

Martin Luther · 1483–1546

Lectures on Galatians (1535), Commentary on Colossians, and writings on Christian freedom

Luther emphasizes that Christ's righteousness is alien to us—entirely external, accomplished by Christ, and received by faith alone. He insists that any attempt to supplement Christ with human works or religious observances denies the sufficiency of Christ's satisfaction for sin. Christian freedom consists in rejecting all religious additions that claim to make us acceptable to God, resting solely in Christ's finished work.

Connection

Luther's doctrine of alien righteousness directly applies Paul's argument in Colossians 2. When Paul writes that we have been forgiven all trespasses and that God canceled the record of debt (verses 13-14), Luther hears an unconditional declaration that Christ's work is sufficient. Luther faced the same parasitic spirituality Paul confronted: medieval Catholic teaching that supplemented Christ's work with penances, indulgences, and merit-based observances. His insistence on 'sola fide' echoes Paul's warning in Colossians 2:16-23 against regulations that 'have no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.' Both Paul and Luther call believers to rest in Christ's complete victory rather than religious performance.

John Owen · 1616–1683

The Mortification of Sin and works on the Holy Spirit's role in sanctification

Owen teaches that mortification of sin is the Spirit's work applying Christ's death to the believer's remaining corruption. He warns against both passivity (waiting for sin to die naturally) and self-powered asceticism (attempting to kill sin through human effort alone). True mortification depends on faith's union with Christ's death and the Spirit's power, not on external religious practices or self-denial divorced from gospel confidence.

Connection

Owen's treatment of mortification illuminates Paul's warning in Colossians 2:20-23 that ascetic practices 'have no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.' The Colossian false teachers promoted severe treatment of the body as a purification strategy. Owen agrees with Paul that such practices fail because they operate outside union with Christ. Owen's emphasis on the Spirit's role (the same Spirit who raised Christ) connects to Paul's insistence in Colossians 2:12 that we are 'raised with him through faith.' Both writers affirm that genuine transformation comes through participating in Christ's death and resurrection, not through self-powered religious performance.

Charles Hodge · 1797–1878

Commentary on Colossians, within his Systematic Theology's treatment of Christology and sanctification

Hodge emphasizes that Colossians presents Christ as possessing all divine attributes and accomplishing complete redemption. He argues that the false teaching at Colossae represented an early form of the error that would later develop into Gnosticism—the notion that approaching God requires elaborate intermediaries and purification rituals. Hodge insists that Paul's response is to magnify Christ's person and work as entirely sufficient.

Connection

Hodge's commentary provides systematic-theological grounding for the sermon's claim that most threats to Christ are parasitic rather than competitive. He identifies the pattern in Colossians that recurs throughout church history: not outright denial of Christ, but subtle diminishment through addition. Hodge's treatment of the stoicheia as both Jewish ceremonialism and pagan philosophy helps explain how the same spiritual dynamics operate across different cultural forms. His emphasis on the Reformed understanding of Christ's threefold office (prophet, priest, king) resonates with Paul's two-dimensional concern in Colossians 2: Christ as final revelation (prophet) and final purification (priest), exercising kingly authority over all competing powers.

Outside the immediate tradition

The Great Conversation

Plato — particularly the Allegory of the Cave in Republic, Book VII

Plato's allegory depicts prisoners who mistake shadows for reality, and the philosopher's ascent from darkness to enlightenment. The Colossian false teachers promoted a similar epistemology: secret knowledge accessed through visions and mystical experiences, implying that ordinary Christian teaching was merely shadowy while they possessed true illumination. Paul's response in Colossians 2:2-3 is a direct challenge to this Platonic impulse—all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ himself, not in esoteric experiences or philosophical speculation. Where Plato located true knowledge in escape from the material world, Paul insists that 'in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily' (verse 9). The incarnation inverts Platonic metaphysics: ultimate reality has entered the material realm, and knowing Christ is knowing God.

Aristotle — Nicomachean Ethics on virtue formation through habituation

Aristotle teaches that virtue is acquired through repeated practice, habituation shaping character from outside-in through disciplined action. This framework undergirds the stoicheia's approach to purification: perform the rituals, observe the regulations, and through repetition you will become acceptable. Paul's critique in Colossians 2:20-23 challenges precisely this model—these practices 'have indeed an appearance of wisdom in promoting self-made religion and asceticism and severity to the body, but they are of no value in stopping the indulgence of the flesh.' Paul does not deny transformation through practice, but insists the power source is union with Christ's death and resurrection (verses 11-13), not autonomous habituation. Transformation is inside-out (the Spirit's work) rather than outside-in (human effort), though it manifests in embodied practice shaped by Christ's lordship.

Blaise Pascal — Pensées on the God-shaped vacuum and human restlessness

Pascal argues that humans possess an infinite abyss within them that can only be filled by God, and that all human pursuits—philosophy, pleasure, power—are diversions from this fundamental restlessness. This insight illuminates Paul's description of the stoicheia in Colossians 2:8. The 'human tradition' and 'elemental spirits' Paul warns against are not arbitrary inventions but responses to real metaphysical questions: What is God like? How can I approach Him? Pascal helps us see that these questions are universal and unavoidable—the stoicheia are humanity's collective attempts to answer them apart from Christ. Paul's claim that Christ is the complete answer (fullness of deity dwelling bodily, complete forgiveness accomplished) means the abyss Pascal describes finds its true filling only in Christ, not in supplemental religious strategies.

Alasdair MacIntyre — After Virtue on tradition-constituted inquiry and the fragmentation of moral discourse

MacIntyre argues that moral reasoning only makes sense within a tradition that provides a coherent narrative and shared practices. In a fragmented society, moral claims become arbitrary expressions of preference because they lack this grounding. This framework helps explain the Colossian situation: the Jewish and pagan stoicheia represented thick cultural traditions with coherent answers to life's fundamental questions, while early Christianity was still culturally thin—it had strong theological truth but not yet developed thick communal practices. The pressure to return to Judaism or paganism was the pressure to return to a tradition that felt substantial, that provided clear identity markers and practices. Paul's response is not to deny the need for tradition-constituted identity, but to insist that Christ himself is the true tradition's center, and that the church (verses 2, 19) is where this new tradition takes shape. The stoicheia offer false coherence; Christ offers true telos.

Translated into life

Extended Applications

Parenting and Family Discipleship

Parents often face pressure to supplement the gospel with Christian cultural markers—whether homeschooling, certain entertainment standards, political alignment, or aesthetic preferences—as if these additional observances secure their children's spiritual safety. This replicates the Colossian error. When we treat supplemental practices as quasi-sacramental (believing they make our children more acceptable to God or more likely to persevere), we functionally deny Christ's sufficiency. The application is not license—children need structure, discipline, and cultural formation—but proper ordering. Practices serve gospel formation rather than supplement gospel insufficiency. Ask: Am I presenting Christ as sufficient for my children's salvation and sanctification, or am I implicitly teaching that Christ plus [our family culture] is what actually saves? Specifically, examine how you respond to children's questions about why your family does or doesn't do certain things. If the answer is framed in terms of covenant identity in Christ rather than additional requirements for acceptance, you're applying Colossians 2 rightly.

Vocation and the Meaning of Work

Modern work culture often functions as a stoicheia—a systematic answer to fundamental questions about worth, purpose, and identity. The corporate world has its purification rituals (performance reviews, certifications, networking strategies) and revelation claims (thought leaders, productivity gurus, organizational culture). Believers can subtly treat vocational success as supplemental validation of their standing before God, or conversely, treat ministry-adjacent work as more 'spiritual' than secular employment. Colossians 2 insists that Christ's lordship extends to all work, and His declaration of your righteousness is not enhanced by vocational achievement or ministerial proximity. Practically: when you sense anxiety about career trajectory or worthiness in your field, trace whether you're seeking from work something only Christ provides. Do you need your boss's approval to feel God's pleasure? Does vocational failure threaten your sense of God's acceptance? Do you treat your work's theological significance as dependent on how 'Christian' the work appears? Christ's triumph (verse 15) means no vocational sphere remains neutral ground—all work is either submitted to His lordship or becomes idolatrous, but none adds to His finished work.

Suffering, Illness, and Physical Limitation

The body's frailty raises acute questions about God's character and our acceptability to Him. The stoicheia offer competing answers: prosperity teaching promises that sufficient faith produces healing, while health-and-wellness culture baptizes bodily optimization as spiritual discipline. Both are parasitic additions. Prosperity teaching makes physical health supplemental evidence of God's favor; wellness culture treats bodily discipline as a purification strategy. Paul's argument in Colossians 2:9-15 offers a different framework: Christ's bodily incarnation and physical death accomplished complete purification, and His resurrection body is the firstfruits of ours. Between now and then, we groan in weak bodies, but this weakness does not compromise our standing before God. Practically: chronic illness is not evidence of insufficient faith, and bodily optimization is not a higher level of sanctification. The discipline Paul commends (verse 6-7, rooted and built up in Christ) includes caring for our bodies as temples, but distinguishes this from ascetic severity that treats bodily denial as meritorious (verse 23). When facing illness or limitation, the question is not 'What am I doing wrong spiritually?' but 'How does Christ's victory over death shape my understanding of this temporary weakness?'

Church Culture and Denominational Identity

Denominations and church traditions inevitably develop thick cultures—liturgical patterns, theological emphases, aesthetic preferences, social expectations. These can serve Colossians 2:6-7 (rooted and built up in Christ) or violate Colossians 2:16-20 (imposing regulations as necessary supplements). The diagnostic question is not whether a church has culture—all churches do, and Paul affirms communal formation—but whether the culture presents itself as neutral expression of gospel conviction or as additional requirement for full standing. Consider denominational distinctives: Does your tradition present its particular emphases as faithful articulation of biblical Christianity, or as the more complete Christianity? When encountering Christians from other traditions, do you relate as fellow members of Christ's body who emphasize different biblical truths, or as incomplete Christians who need your tradition's supplemental insights? The stoicheia operate through gatekeeping—creating insider/outsider status based on observance of distinctives. Paul's standard in Colossians 2 is not lowest-common-denominator Christianity but Christ-centered maximalism: traditions that magnify Christ's sufficiency while humbly acknowledging their own partiality serve the church; traditions that claim superior access to God or fuller revelation violate Christ's supremacy.

Political Engagement and Civic Identity

Political ideologies function as modern stoicheia—comprehensive systems offering answers to fundamental questions about justice, human nature, the good society, and the meaning of history. Both progressive and conservative political visions make metaphysical claims that often compete with Christian confession. Believers face pressure to treat political alignment as supplemental evidence of authentic faith, or to derive moral authority from political effectiveness rather than Christ's lordship. Colossians 2:15's assertion that Christ disarmed the rulers and authorities includes both spiritual and earthly powers—all political systems stand under His judgment and none can claim ultimate allegiance. The application is not withdrawal from politics but proper ordering. Ask: Do I treat political victories as signs of God's kingdom advancing, or do I recognize that God's kingdom advances through the church's faithfulness regardless of political outcomes? When my political tribe contradicts biblical teaching, do I hold fast to Christ (verse 19) or conform to tribal expectations? When the opposing tribe holds a position closer to biblical teaching, can I acknowledge this without threatening my identity? Political engagement shaped by Colossians 2 treats all political options as provisional, subject to Christ's rule, and incapable of providing the righteousness or justice only Christ secures. This produces both deeper political engagement (because politics matters under Christ's lordship) and lighter political identity (because no political movement can claim to be Christ's special instrument).

Spiritual Practices and Personal Devotion

The irony of Colossians 2 is that Paul critiques religious practices while writing a letter that assumes readers will engage in prayer, Scripture meditation, corporate worship, and moral discipline. The difference is location and source. The stoicheia present devotional practices as purification strategies—things we do to make ourselves acceptable to God or maintain our standing. Gospel-shaped practices flow from acceptance, not toward it. They are means of grace that deepen our experience of Christ's finished work, not supplements to incomplete work. Practically: when you evaluate your devotional life, what narrative are you believing? If missing quiet time produces guilt that questions your standing before God, you're operating in the stoicheia. If missing quiet time produces grief over missed communion with the Father who has already declared you righteous in Christ, you're applying Colossians 2 rightly. The disciplines of prayer, fasting, Scripture memory, and solitude are valuable—Paul assumes them—but they function differently depending on the gospel they express. Spiritual practices shaped by Colossians 2 cultivate awareness of Christ's presence and deepen participation in His life, but they do not earn His favor or increase His acceptance. They are responsive, not meritorious.

Pictures that resonate

Illustrations & Resonances

The Parable of the Clear Water

observational

Imagine a person dying of thirst in the desert. A spring of clear, cold water appears before them—sufficient, pure, life-giving. The person drinks and is satisfied. But then a merchant arrives with bottles of supplements: vitamins to add to the water, minerals to enhance it, herbs to improve it. 'The water alone,' the merchant insists, 'is not quite enough. You need these additions to truly thrive.' The person, still holding the cup of clear water, begins to wonder: Perhaps the water was insufficient after all. Perhaps I need something more. This is the Colossian situation. Christ is the spring of living water, completely sufficient for life. The false teachers were merchants of supplements, not competitors with alternative water sources. Their threat was not in replacing Christ but in suggesting He needed enhancement. The tragic outcome is that the person, distracted by evaluating supplements, stops drinking from the spring that was already sufficient.

Connection

This illustrates the parasitic nature of the Colossian heresy—not replacement but supplementation—and the psychological mechanism by which such teachings gain traction even among believers who confess Christ's sufficiency.

The Horse that Spits the Bit

observational

In horsemanship, 'spitting the bit' describes a horse's refusal to accept the rider's control. The bit—a metal mouthpiece—is the primary means by which the rider communicates direction. A horse that spits the bit is rejecting external authority over its movement, insisting on autonomous direction. The sermon uses this image to describe the posture believers must take toward competing authorities. Christ's victory (Colossians 2:15) establishes His exclusive right to rule. Any teaching, practice, or spiritual power that claims supplemental authority must be 'spit'—actively rejected—even when it appears beneficial or arrives with religious credentials. This is not anti-authoritarian individualism but Christ-centered exclusivism. The believer submits completely to Christ's authority precisely by refusing all competitors. The image captures both the active nature of the rejection (spitting is deliberate) and the mouth-level intimacy of the threat (the bit touches the most sensitive part of the horse's mouth, just as false teaching targets the believer's inner assurance).

Connection

This illustrates the active, deliberate rejection of supplemental authorities that Colossians 2 demands, emphasizing that spiritual freedom requires not just believing Christ is sufficient but actively rejecting all additions.

The Coral Reef and Its Parasites

scientific

Coral reefs are ecosystems built by countless tiny organisms working in symbiosis. The reef provides structure and protection; the coral polyps provide food through photosynthesis via algae partners. But coral reefs are vulnerable to parasitic organisms that attach to the coral, appearing to be part of the ecosystem while actually draining its resources. Some parasites are obvious—crown-of-thorns starfish that visibly consume coral. Others are subtle—parasitic snails that bore into coral from within, weakening the structure while remaining nearly invisible. Marine biologists distinguish between predators (which attack from outside) and parasites (which integrate into the system while destroying it). The false teaching in Colossians functioned as a parasite, not a predator. It did not attack Christianity from outside but integrated into the community, claiming to enhance the faith while actually undermining Christ's sufficiency. The diagnostic challenge is the same for reef ecologists and for the church: identifying which organisms are symbiotic (contributing to health) and which are parasitic (draining vitality while appearing beneficial).

Connection

This scientific analogy illuminates the sermon's distinction between competitors and parasites, showing how the most dangerous threats to an ecosystem (or a church) are often those that appear to be part of it rather than external attackers.
