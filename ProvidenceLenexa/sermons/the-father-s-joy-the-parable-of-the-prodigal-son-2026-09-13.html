  1. hypothetical · unit #26 — Illustrates the older brother attitude with a detailed, concrete Sunday morning scenario. The listener physically attends church but inwardly stands outside with arms crossed, critiquing rather than worshiping. The illustration makes the abstract sin vivid and immediately recognizable.
  2. hypothetical · unit #27 — Contrasts the previous illustration with the posture of the repentant sinner who knows they are an object of mercy. Same Sunday morning, opposite heart—grateful, eager, hungry for God. The illustration makes the contrast between self-righteousness and humility emotionally and experientially tangible.
Theological claims· 6
  1. Jesus portrays God as exuberantly happy over one repentant sinner who was lost and is now found. unit #14
  2. The Father can receive repentant sinners not because sin doesn't matter, but because Jesus bore the cost of bringing sinners home to God. unit #19
  3. Neither son wants the father for Himself—one wants His gifts apart from Him, the other serves Him to get His gifts. unit #24
  4. The repentant sinner is humble and joyful because they remember the mercy that brought them into the Father's house—they see themselves as the one who has received the most mercy. unit #28
  5. God is merciful to both sons—celebratory toward the repentant rebel and entreating toward the self-righteous—giving grace appropriate to each. unit #31
  6. The father's response to sin is compassion, not anger—that is the test of whether you reflect the father or the older brother. unit #37

This week we walk through the Father's joy—from the tragedy of forgetting His goodness, through the mercy that receives repentant sinners, to the call to join His celebration by abandoning self-righteousness.

Monday Psalm 16

The psalmist says, 'In your presence there is fullness of joy; at your right hand are pleasures forevermore' (Ps 16:11). This is the Father's posture toward us—not grudging forgiveness, but exuberant delight. When the prodigal returns, the Father's joy is not restrained or measured; it overflows in a feast. We live in a world that teaches us God is distant or disappointed, but the cross-reference shows us the truth: God's deepest pleasure is in the presence of His people.

Tuesday Matthew 18:10-14

Jesus teaches that the shepherd leaves the ninety-nine to search for the one lost sheep, and 'when he has found it, he lays it on his shoulders, rejoicing' (Matt 18:13). The shepherd bears the weight of the lost one—just as Christ bore the weight of our sin on the cross. The Father's costly search for us, and His joyful reception of us, was made possible only because Jesus paid the price. Our homecoming cost Him everything.

Wednesday Psalm 16:5-6

The psalmist declares, 'The Lord is my chosen portion and my cup; you hold my fate' (Ps 16:5). Both the younger son and the older son treat the father as a means to an inheritance, not as the joy itself. But the psalm shows us the alternative: to choose the *Lord Himself* as our portion, not His gifts. True repentance means turning from 'I want what you have' to 'I want *you*'—and discovering that wanting Him is the only way to actually receive His gifts rightly.

Thursday Matthew 18:21-35

In the parable of the unforgiving servant, the king shows mercy to a servant drowning in debt, forgiving what would have been impossible to repay (Matt 18:27). But the servant then refuses mercy to a fellow servant. We are like that forgiven servant—we have been shown extravagant mercy by the Father through Christ. When we harden toward others, we show that we have not truly grasped the mercy shown to us. The test of whether we know the Father is whether our hearts grow tender or bitter toward sinners.

Friday Psalm 16:11

As we close the week, return to the source of joy the prodigal discovers: 'In your presence there is fullness of joy' (Ps 16:11). The younger son, humbled and amazed by grace, enters the feast not as a hired hand but as a beloved son. When we truly repent and remember the mercy that has brought us into God's house—not by our righteousness but by Christ's work—we discover that we are not the envious older brother, but the grateful younger one, free at last to simply be with our Father and rejoice.

Memory verse this week

Luke 15:20

And he arose and came to his father. But while he was still a long way off, his father saw him and felt compassion, and ran and embraced him and kissed him.

Why this verse: This verse captures the heart of the sermon's central claim: God joyfully receives repentant sinners with extravagant compassion, not reluctance or judgment. The father's running embrace is the visual anchor for understanding God's mercy toward all who return to Him.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. When the younger son 'came to himself' (Luke 15:17), what had changed in his thinking about his father? What was he finally remembering that he had forgotten?
    Luke 15:17
    → Where in your own life have you believed a lie about God's character that made you want to run from Him rather than toward Him?
  2. The father doesn't wait for the son to finish his prepared speech of repentance—he runs to embrace him before a single word is spoken. What does this tell us about the father's joy, and why do you think Jesus emphasizes the father's movement toward the son rather than the son's movement toward the father?
    Luke 15:20-24
  3. Jesus says there is 'joy before the angels of God over one sinner who repents' (Luke 15:10). Who is experiencing that joy, and why would Jesus want His listeners to know that His father feels this way about repentance?
    Luke 15:7, 15:10
    → How does this picture of God's joy differ from the way you've sometimes imagined God responding to your sin or the sin of others?
  4. The older son refuses to enter the feast. He says to his father, 'All these years I've served you, and you never gave me even a young goat' (Luke 15:29-30). What does his complaint reveal about why he's been serving his father? What does he actually want from his father?
    Luke 15:28-30
    → Can you identify a time when you've served God or others from a similar motive—doing the right thing in order to earn something rather than from joy in the relationship itself?
  5. The father's response to the older son is not anger but a tender entreaty: 'Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours' (Luke 15:31). How does the father's gentle correction address what the older son has actually missed—not in terms of possessions, but in terms of relationship?
    Luke 15:31-32
  6. Both sons stand outside the father's joy—one through rebellion, one through self-righteousness. As you look at your own heart this week, which son's temptation do you recognize in yourself, and what would it mean for you to turn and enter the father's joy?
    → What specific step could you take this week to remember, like the younger son did, that you are loved by the Father not because of your obedience but as an object of His mercy?
Couples · three questions over coffee

Returning to the Father's Joy

  1. Which son's posture—the younger's rebellion or the older's resentment—did you recognize in your own heart as Chris preached, and what did that recognition stir in you?
  2. Where do we slip into older-brother attitudes with each other—grumbling, judgment, keeping score—instead of remembering the mercy that brought us both into the Father's house?
  3. What specific sin or self-righteousness do you need to confess before God this week, and how can I pray for your repentance and joy in His mercy?
Sunday-evening family table

The Father Runs

For the parent

The sermon opened with the image of the Father running toward his prodigal son—a shocking detail in first-century Jewish culture where dignified fathers do not run. This prompt invites your family to sit with that image and what it tells us about how God responds to us when we return to Him. Listen for whether your kids grasp that the Father's joy is not earned but given.

In the story, when the younger son finally decided to go home, his father saw him from far away and ran to him as fast as he could. Why do you think Jesus told us that the father *ran*? What does that tell us about how God feels when someone comes back to Him?
works for ages 6+ — younger children can answer simply ('He was happy'); older children and teens will naturally move toward what the father's running reveals about God's character and mercy
