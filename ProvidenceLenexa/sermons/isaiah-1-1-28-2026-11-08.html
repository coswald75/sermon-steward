Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace God's perfect moral vision through Scripture—a vision that sees hidden victims, hidden crimes, and calls both the oppressed and the oppressor to their proper response in Christ.

Monday Exodus 3:7

When God encounters Moses at the burning bush, his first words are an affirmation of what he has *seen*: 'I have surely seen the affliction of my people.' Not heard rumors. Not accepted cultural narratives. *Seen.* This is the foundation of Isaiah's courtroom—God's vision penetrates every system, every disguise, every cultural blind spot that would hide injustice. The victims you assume are invisible to heaven are the first concern of the God who speaks.

Tuesday Psalm 68:5

The Psalmist calls God 'Father of the fatherless and protector of widows'—not because he hears their pleas, but because he *sees* their condition. This is not sentimentality; it is a declaration of God's forensic precision. He sees the widow not as society sees her (dispensable, marginal, a tax burden) but as she *is*—a precious image-bearer whose cause is his own. Where the world sees a statistical problem, God sees a person whose every tear has been catalogued.

Wednesday Exodus 22:22-24

God does not frame his response as mild correction or gentle persuasion. His judgment against those who afflict the widow and orphan is violent, personal, and final. This is the other side of God's perfect vision—he sees not only the victim's suffering but the perpetrator's guilt with equal clarity. The comfort of this passage is that no injustice will escape his reckoning, and no perpetrator will sleep soundly forever. Justice belongs to the Lord, and he will not delay.

Thursday Acts 7:60

Stephen, murdered by those who would silence the gospel, does not cry out for retribution. Instead, he prays, 'Lord, do not hold this sin against them.' This is not weakness; it is the apex of moral courage—to see your killer *as God sees him*, a soul still capable of repentance, and to intercede for that repentance before judgment falls. When you are wronged, your prayer for the wrongdoer's conversion may be the last mercy they receive before eternity.

Friday Isaiah 64:6

Isaiah's own confession—that all our righteous deeds are like filthy rags—is not despair; it is the doorway to freedom. We are never only victims awaiting vindication, nor only perpetrators awaiting judgment. We are both, held in the hands of a God whose perfect vision sees our full complexity and whose mercy in Christ covers the whole of it. This is why repentance must be mutual, corporate, and continual—why the congregation must lay down its greed for artificial peace and speak hard truth to one another, knowing we will all need that truth spoken back to us.
