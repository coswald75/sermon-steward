Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through the pattern that runs beneath Isaiah's vision: God's people exist to proclaim his glory to the nations, sin disqualifies us from that witness, and Christ's purification restores us to our proclaiming purpose.

Monday Genesis 12:1-3

Abraham's calling was never private. God blessed him so that *all the families of the earth* would be blessed through him — a people elected not for privilege but for proclamation. The same purpose runs through Israel, through the apostles at Pentecost, and through us. We are not saved to be comfortable; we are saved to make God's glory known.

Tuesday Exodus 19:5-6

God did not call Israel 'my treasure' and 'my kingdom of priests' so they could hide in the wilderness. A kingdom of priests exists to mediate — to stand between the holy God and a watching world, visibly different and visibly devoted. That priestly calling belongs to the church now. Our holiness is not private piety; it is the platform from which we speak.

Wednesday 1 Peter 2:9

Peter echoes Exodus — we are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, called to proclaim the excellencies of him who called us. But proclamation requires distinction. When God's people live as the world lives — pursuing comfort, nursing grudges, chasing status — we have nothing to proclaim. Our sin doesn't just shame us; it silences us. We become irrelevant to the very witness we were made to give.

Thursday 2 Timothy 2:21

Paul tells Timothy that a vessel purified from dishonorable use becomes useful to the Master, prepared for every good work. This is not flowery language. Holiness is *functional*. A pastor harboring bitterness cannot effectively shepherd the wounded. A parent nursing secret sin cannot call children to righteousness with authority. God's power flows through clean channels. Pursue purity not as a burden but as the prerequisite to being useful in his hands.

Friday 2 Corinthians 5:14-21

Paul's urgency to plead with others for reconciliation flows directly from his awe at being reconciled himself. He is not compelled by guilt; he is compelled by grace — by the stunning reality that Christ made him righteous, bore his sin, and now *uses* him as an ambassador. This is what happens when repentance becomes real: the sweetness of pardon becomes so overwhelming that you cannot keep it to yourself. You plead. You witness. You speak because you have tasted that the Lord is good.
