Your sins can be separated from you as far as the east as from the west because of what Christ has done for you. So what should you do? Turn from your sin, turn from your rebellion, turn from your denial of the Lord and your fear of man or whatever, and trust in Christ. Confess that you are a sinner and call on the Lord for salvation. As we saw, he will hear you, he will forgive you, and he will adopt you into his family. So if you're outside of Christ this morning, trust in Christ.

23 · Summarizes the sermon's journey from distress to rest, reiterates the relatability of David's emotions to contemporary experience, and issues a final charge to use the pattern of Psalm 4 when facing future distress

All right, to wrap things up, we've gone on quite a journey together today. We've seen David trek from distress to lament, to faith, to counsel, to contentment, and ultimately to. To rest. And we've Seen that while David's circumstances, they may have been on a grander scale than ours, with the kingdom at stake, they're not all that different from what causes us to turn in our beds or to repeat conversations in our head, or to burn in our heart. At night, when faced with distress is an hour. Sometime in the future, may we use the lessons of Psalm 4 that David exemplified for us. May we journey from that distress to a place of renewed rest in the Lord, and ultimately may we get a good night's sleep, all for God's glory and our joy.

24 · Closing prayer thanking God for his goodness and the promises of Psalm 4, asking that the sermon bear fruit in the congregation's hearts and that they remember to move toward God in future distress

Let's pray. Jesus, we just we praise you because you are a good God. You cherish us, you care for us, and you offer yourself to us. So we thank you for the promises of Psalm 4 that even in our distress we can travel from distress to rest because of who you are and what you've done for us. We pray that the words in the sermon would go deep into our hearts, that you would bear fruit through them, that if we're experiencing distress now or in the future, we remember that we can come to you and we can move towards you and we can have faith in you in whatever we're facing. Lord, we thank you. We love you. In Jesus name we pray Amen.

25 · Transitions to communion by presenting Jesus as the greater David who endured the greatest distress, quoting Hebrews 12:2-3 to establish his work on the cross as the basis for our forgiveness, adoption, and comfort, and inviting the congregation to take communion in gratitude

For communion this morning. I want to draw your attention ultimately to the greater David, to the greater King, Jesus Christ, the one who faced the greatest distress we could ever imagine. Let's look to him, the founder and perfecter of our faith, who for the joy that was set before him, endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand, seated now, listening to our prayers at the right hand of the throne of God. And let's consider him who endured from sinners such hostility against himself, so you may not grow weary or faint hearted. He is the Greater One who calls to the Lord for relief. He is the Greater One who is dishonored and shamed and lied about. He is the Greater One who taught others so wisely. And he is the greater One who took joy in the Lord more than anyone. Ultimately, he was the one whose body was broken, whose blood was shed so we could be forgiven of our sins and adopted into God's family and comforted in our distress, no matter what is going on in our lives. So come today, take communion, grateful and rejoicing that whatever is going on and whatever may come, we can take heart that God is our God, that in Christ God, the God of the universe, approves of us and that he will walk with us through any trial that we may face so in faith this morning. Come to the table.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how God's tender care for us — revealed through His sovereignty, His invitation to lament, His faithfulness in Christ, and His comfort in distress — becomes the foundation of our rest.

Monday Isaiah 6

Isaiah encounters the holiness of God—the consuming reality of divine majesty—yet finds his fear transformed into commissioned peace. Like David in Psalm 4, Isaiah discovers that meeting the true God in His Word is not paralyzing but restful; God's self-revelation in Scripture is the equipment He gives us to still our anxious hearts and know that He is God.

Tuesday Lamentations 3:22-23

The prophet Jeremiah does not suppress his grief—he names it fully—yet in the very act of lamenting before God, he discovers that mercies are new every morning. This is the pattern Jesus modeled and the pattern Psalm 4 invites: not denial of our pain, but the courageous movement of pouring it out before the God whose compassions never fail, so that we may trust again.

Wednesday Romans 8:32

Paul's logic is absolute: if God has already given us Christ—the costliest, most precious gift—how will He withhold any good thing we need? This is the gospel assurance that meets us in our sleeplessness: we are not orphans hoping an indifferent God might help, but beloved children of the Father who proved His care by emptying heaven itself for our redemption.

Thursday Hebrews 12:2-3

Jesus, our High Priest, endured the cross—infinitely greater anguish than any sleeplessness we face—and did so willingly, for joy set before Him. When we fix our eyes on Him, we are not merely recalling a historical event; we are beholding the God-man who conquered death itself and now intercedes for us, transforming our night seasons into occasions of deepened communion with Him.

Friday 1 Samuel 1:10-20

Hannah's barrenness was a wound; yet her movement was toward the temple, toward prayer, toward pouring out her soul before the Lord. Her example shows us that the way through our pain—our sleepless nights, our fears, our unmet longings—is not avoidance but this simple, countercultural act: we turn toward God, we speak our heart to Him, and in so doing, we find the peace that guarded her and that guards us still.

A Prayer for Rest in God's Sovereign Care

Father, we come before you in awe of your tender care for us. You are the God who never slumbers, who sees our distress before we cry out, and who has given us your Word to equip us for the struggles that steal our rest. In Christ, you have shown us that no circumstance—no sleepless night, no anxiety, no unanswered question—falls outside your sovereign love (Romans 8:32).

We confess that when distress comes, our first instinct is often to fix our eyes on our circumstances rather than on you. We worry about what tomorrow will bring; we rehearse our sorrows in the dark hours when sleep eludes us; we forget that you care infinitely more for our welfare than we do ourselves. Forgive us for the pride that makes us believe our anxieties are too great for your attention, and for the unbelief that questions whether you truly desire our rest and peace.

Yet the gospel tells us that you have sent your Son, the greater David, who endured the cross so that we might be forgiven, adopted into your family, and comforted in all our distress (Hebrews 12:2–3). Because Jesus has satisfied your justice and secured our redemption, we are free to bring our laments to you—our grief over broken relationships, our fear for our children's futures, our weariness in body and soul. You invite us to cry out to you with simple prayers, confident that you hear, that you care, and that you will sustain us (Psalm 4:1, 8).

We ask you to grant us the grace this week to turn toward you in our distress rather than away from you. Give us courage to voice our sorrows in prayer, knowing that you welcome our honest cries. Teach us to lie down in peace, not because our circumstances have changed, but because we rest in the finished work of Christ and your unshakeable promise to care for us in every circumstance (Lamentations 3:22–23). We commit ourselves afresh to you, the God of our salvation and our rest.
