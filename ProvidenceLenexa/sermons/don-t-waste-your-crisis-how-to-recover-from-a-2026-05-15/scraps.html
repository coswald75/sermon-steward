Don't Waste Your Crisis: How to Recover from a Self-Inflicted Wound

This sermon confronts the visceral question: what do you do when you've made a catastrophic mess of your life through your own choices? Drawing from David's response in Psalm 51, Chris Oswald argues that the crisis itself is God's severe mercy—an opportunity not primarily to restore reputation or circumstances, but to recover intimacy with the God you've wandered from. What follows widens the horizon around this claim, tracing its roots in Scripture, history, and the lived experience of suffering.

When & Where

Historical & Literary Context

Psalm 51 stands as David's confession after Nathan the prophet confronted him about adultery with Bathsheba and the murder of her husband Uriah (2 Samuel 11-12). The psalm's superscription explicitly ties it to this moment of exposure. David had enjoyed months of concealment—military success abroad, no public scandal—until God sent Nathan with a parable that unmasked the king's sin. The historical setting matters: David was Israel's anointed king, the man after God's own heart, now standing as an adulterer and murderer whose private sin had become a public crisis. The psalm reflects the interior posture of a man whose external life has collapsed but whose primary anguish is not social or political but relational—he has sinned against God. The literary structure moves from confession (vv. 1-6) to petition for cleansing and restoration (vv. 7-12) to vows of future service and worship (vv. 13-19). The placement of Psalm 51 within the Psalter marks it as a paradigmatic penitential psalm, used liturgically by Israel and the church for centuries as the pattern for post-crisis repentance.

Key terms

  • חָטָא (chata) — The Hebrew root for 'sin' in verse 4, meaning to miss the mark or fall short—David uses multiple terms (transgression, iniquity, sin) to emphasize the totality of his failure before God.
  • לֵב טָהוֹר (lev tahor) — A 'clean heart' in verse 10—David asks God to create anew, using the verb 'bara' that appears in Genesis 1:1 for divine creation, acknowledging that moral renewal is beyond human self-repair.
Across the canon

Cross-References

2 Samuel 12:1-14

Nathan confronts David with a parable about a rich man stealing a poor man's lamb, and when David condemns the rich man, Nathan declares, 'You are the man'—exposing David's adultery and murder.

Connection

This narrative provides the historical crisis behind Psalm 51. David's months of concealment ended not by his own choice but by prophetic exposure. The sermon's claim that crisis is God's severe mercy finds concrete form here: Nathan's arrival was disaster for David's reputation but rescue for David's soul. The exposure was divine intervention, not bad luck. God preferred a broken David to a successful David living in denial.

Hebrews 12:5-11

The Lord disciplines those he loves, and though discipline seems painful rather than pleasant at the time, it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who are trained by it.

Connection

This passage directly supports the sermon's central metaphor of crisis as discipline that produces harvest. The writer of Hebrews echoes Proverbs to argue that God's fatherly love is manifest precisely in correction, not in permissive indifference. The 'peaceful fruit of righteousness' is not behavioral conformity but relational restoration—the sermon's claim that the goal is gaining Christ, not recovering normalcy. The harvest depends on being 'trained by it,' which parallels the sermon's warning that crisis can be wasted by passive suffering or pragmatic behavior modification.

Luke 15:11-24

The younger son squanders his inheritance in reckless living, ends up feeding pigs, comes to his senses, and returns to his father, who runs to embrace him and celebrates his return.

Connection

The prodigal son's crisis was entirely self-inflicted—his choices led to ruin. The sermon's emphasis on seeing crisis as God bringing you back finds narrative form in the father's joy at the son's return. The son's speech ('I have sinned against heaven and before you') mirrors David's posture in Psalm 51:4, recognizing vertical offense as primary. The father's response models divine eagerness for intimacy over judgment. The son did not return to recover his inheritance but to be a servant; he received sonship instead—the sermon's distinction between recovering old life and gaining Christ.

Lamentations 3:31-33

The Lord will not cast off forever, but though he cause grief, he will have compassion according to the abundance of his steadfast love; for he does not afflict from his heart or grieve the children of men.

Connection

Jeremiah's reflection on exile—another self-inflicted national crisis—insists that God's discipline is not gleeful punishment but reluctant necessity. The phrase 'he does not afflict from his heart' supports the sermon's framing of crisis as severe mercy. God wounds to heal, not to destroy. The promise that he will not cast off forever anchors the sermon's warning against treating crisis as terminal. The abundance of steadfast love precedes and motivates the discipline, making it relational correction rather than punitive vengeance.

Hosea 2:14-15

God says he will allure Israel into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her, making the valley of trouble a door of hope, so that she will answer as in the days of her youth.

Connection

Hosea describes God's strange courtship strategy: bringing Israel into the wilderness—a place of stripping and exposure—in order to restore intimacy. The 'valley of Achor' (trouble) becomes hope's doorway. This prophetic image undergirds the sermon's claim that crisis is God personally at work to restore relationship. The goal is not Israel's geopolitical recovery but her answering God 'as in the days of her youth'—renewed first love, the sermon's emphasis on prioritizing intimacy over circumstances. The wilderness is severe mercy.

Job 42:5-6

Job declares that he had heard of God by the hearing of the ear, but now his eye sees God, and therefore he despises himself and repents in dust and ashes.

Connection

Job's suffering was not self-inflicted, but his response models what the sermon describes as the harvest of crisis: gaining greater knowledge of God. Job's earlier righteousness was real but second-hand ('hearing of the ear'); suffering brought him into unmediated encounter ('now my eye sees you'). The sermon's insistence that crisis should make the cross more glorious and necessary finds parallel in Job's discovery that knowing God firsthand surpasses all previous understanding. The goal was never restoring flocks and herds (though God did that too) but seeing God.

Twenty centuries of witness

Voices from the Tradition

Augustine of Hippo · 354-430

Confessions, especially Books 8-10

Augustine chronicles his long wandering from God through intellectual pride and sexual sin, and his eventual conversion in a Milan garden. He treats all his previous life—including his philosophical brilliance—as fundamentally restless because it was lived at a relational distance from God. His famous opening line, 'You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you,' frames every moral failure as a symptom of misplaced ultimate love.

Connection

Augustine's Confessions is the West's most famous account of crisis as divine pursuit. His emphasis on restlessness apart from God directly supports the sermon's claim that sin is fundamentally relational distance, not merely bad behavior. Augustine did not treat his conversion as behavior modification but as the return of a wandering lover to his true home. His post-conversion life focused on communion with God as the supreme good, paralleling the sermon's insistence that the goal of crisis is gaining Christ, not recovering respectability.

Martin Luther · 1483-1546

Lectures on Romans, and the broader theme of Anfechtung (spiritual trial) throughout his writings

Luther describes spiritual trial (Anfechtung) as God's strange work of crushing the sinner's self-confidence in order to drive them to Christ alone. He insists that God hides his favor beneath the appearance of wrath, and that the terrors of conscience—though they feel like abandonment—are actually God's tools for breaking the heart's idolatry and self-reliance. Luther's own monastery years were marked by intense guilt and failed efforts at self-justification until he discovered justification by faith.

Connection

Luther's theology of Anfechtung provides a Reformation lens on the sermon's claim that crisis is severe mercy. Luther would say that God's greatest kindness is to strip away the fig leaves of self-righteousness and drive the sinner to despair of their own resources, so that Christ becomes precious. The sermon's warning against pragmatizing crisis into behavior modification echoes Luther's insistence that the law's function is to kill self-trust, not to provide a ladder for climbing to God. The goal is faith clinging to Christ, not improved moral performance.

John Owen · 1616-1683

The Mortification of Sin and Of Communion with God

Owen argues that indwelling sin is never fully eradicated in this life and that the Christian's battle is not primarily against external temptations but against the heart's ongoing tendency to wander from communion with God. He insists that mortification of sin is the work of the Spirit applying the cross, not human willpower, and that the measure of spiritual health is not sinless perfection but maintaining daily fellowship with the triune God.

Connection

Owen's Puritan realism about remaining sin supports the sermon's warning in unit 29 that you will be tempted to treat old sins casually once the initial brokenness fades. Owen would say that the crisis revealed what was always there—the heart's capacity for self-deception and spiritual lethargy. His emphasis on communion with God as the barometer of spiritual health directly parallels the sermon's claim in unit 35 that talking to God throughout the day is humanity's most fundamental function. Owen treats restoration not as a one-time event but as ongoing vigilance and renewed dependence on grace.

Jonathan Edwards · 1703-1758

Religious Affections and sermons on spiritual experience

Edwards distinguishes between genuine religious affections (holy desires and delights that flow from seeing the beauty of God) and temporary emotional experiences that fade under trial. He argues that true conversion is marked not by intensity of feeling but by a new spiritual sense that finds God himself supremely satisfying, not merely his benefits. He warns against mistaking moral improvement or emotional highs for saving grace.

Connection

Edwards's category of 'religious affections' clarifies the sermon's distinction between behavior modification and relational restoration. The sermon insists that the goal is not better conduct but intimacy with God—Edwards would say that true affections have God as their object, not relief from guilt or restored reputation. His analysis of false conversions that mistake temporary conviction for regeneration supports the sermon's warning against pragmatizing crisis. The fruitful harvest is a heart that delights in God himself, not one that simply fears consequences or craves normalcy.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer · 1906-1945

Life Together and Letters and Papers from Prison

Bonhoeffer writes from Nazi imprisonment about the danger of cheap grace—grace without discipleship, forgiveness without transformation. He insists that Christian community requires confession and bearing one another's burdens, and that isolation is a tool of the enemy. In his prison letters, he reflects on suffering as the place where Christ meets his people most intimately, stripping away illusions and revealing what truly sustains.

Connection

Bonhoeffer's experience of self-inflicted crisis (his involvement in the conspiracy to assassinate Hitler led to his imprisonment and execution) lends weight to the sermon's claim that crisis should deepen love and soften you toward others rather than isolating you (unit 25). His warnings against cheap grace support the sermon's insistence that the cross must become more glorious through crisis, not less. Bonhoeffer's prison writings model what it looks like to prioritize communion with God over circumstances—he found Christ more precious in prison than in freedom, the sermon's definition of success.

Outside the immediate tradition

The Great Conversation

Aristotle — Nicomachean Ethics, Book II on habit formation

Aristotle argues that virtue is acquired through practice—we become just by doing just acts, courageous by doing courageous acts. Character is formed by repeated action until it becomes second nature. The sermon's critique of behavior modification as insufficient directly engages this classical framework. Aristotle is right that habits matter, but the Christian claim is that habit formation divorced from relational communion with God misses the root problem. David's crisis did not need better willpower but a new heart (Psalm 51:10), something beyond Aristotelian habituation. The sermon insists that character transformation flows from intimacy with God, not merely from disciplined practice, though practice matters as a fruit of that intimacy.

Blaise Pascal — Pensées, on the God-shaped void and the misery of man without God

Pascal argues that human beings are caught between greatness and wretchedness—capable of contemplating infinity yet enslaved to trivial distractions. He claims there is a God-shaped void in every heart that people try to fill with everything except God, and that all human misery stems from our inability to sit quietly in a room alone with our thoughts because we are fleeing from the knowledge of our need for God. The sermon's claim that crisis reveals relational distance from God, not merely behavioral failure, echoes Pascal's diagnosis. The self-inflicted wounds David suffered were symptoms of a deeper void—he had wandered from the love that should have satisfied him. Pascal would say that David's adultery was the attempt to fill divine-sized longing with creaturely pleasure, and that only return to God himself could address the root hunger.

Søren Kierkegaard — The Sickness Unto Death, on despair as relational alienation from God

Kierkegaard defines despair not primarily as emotional anguish but as the misrelation of the self to itself and to God—either willing defiantly to be oneself apart from God, or willing despairingly not to be oneself at all. He argues that all sin is before God, and that the intensity of sin is measured by its consciousness of being before God. The sermon's interpretation of Psalm 51:4 ('Against you, you only, have I sinned') finds philosophical articulation in Kierkegaard's claim that sin is always fundamentally a God-relationship, not merely horizontal harm. Kierkegaard would support the sermon's insistence that crisis must be seen as God bringing you back to himself—the self in despair is the self attempting autonomy, and healing requires receiving yourself as a gift from and relation to God.

Charles Taylor — A Secular Age, on the buffered self and disenchantment

Taylor traces the modern construction of the 'buffered self'—a self that sees itself as invulnerable to external spiritual forces, autonomous, and defined by its capacity for rational control and self-direction. This self contrasts with the premodern 'porous self' that experienced itself as open to God, demons, and transcendent realities. The sermon's claim that the best barometer of spiritual health is talking to God throughout the day (unit 35) directly challenges the buffered self's self-sufficiency. Taylor's analysis helps explain why modern people find the sermon's framing—crisis as divine intervention in personal relationship—so disorienting. The buffered self treats crisis as random misfortune or natural consequence, not as God's severe mercy. The sermon calls hearers back to a porous, relational ontology where walking with God in the cool of the day is humanity's most fundamental function.

Translated into life

Extended Applications

Marriage and family life

A self-inflicted crisis in marriage—whether infidelity, chronic neglect, or patterns of unkindness finally exposed—often fixates both spouses on behavioral contracts and rebuilt trust as the goal. The sermon redirects: the crisis reveals that you wandered from God, and your spouse became either an idol you demanded too much from or an obstacle you resented. The application is not primarily 'be kinder' (though you should) but to see the marriage crisis as God inviting you back to himself. When you wake each morning, the first question is not 'How do I repair my spouse's trust today?' but 'Am I walking with God today?' The practical posture: confess the relational distance from God that made your spouse either too important or not important enough. Pursue restored intimacy with God, and let renewed love for your spouse flow from that spring. Expect the marriage to look different—not a return to old normal but a new shape where both partners are learning dependence on God rather than each other.

Parenting through exposure of personal failure

When your children discover or witness your moral failure—a father's addiction exposed, a mother's affair revealed, financial dishonesty brought to light—the instinct is to minimize damage and restore their respect for you. The sermon reframes: this crisis is God's gift to your children, showing them that you are not their savior and that the Christian life is not moral superiority but ongoing repentance. The application is to resist the urge to control their perception and instead model what Psalm 51 looks like in real time. Confess to them specifically (age-appropriately) that you sinned against God, not just against family peace. Let them see you pursuing God, not just better behavior. The long-term gift is children who learn that Christianity is for sinners who cling to Christ, not for the self-sufficient. Practically: resist defensiveness when they express hurt or anger, and invite them into the slow work of rebuilding trust without demanding that they pretend the crisis didn't happen.

Vocation and work after professional failure

A self-inflicted professional crisis—financial mismanagement, ethical breach, relational toxicity that led to termination or demotion—tempts you to treat recovery as a job search and reputation repair campaign. The sermon insists the crisis is not fundamentally about your career but about your soul. Ask: was work an idol? Did you sacrifice integrity for success because you needed to be seen as competent, needed, indispensable? The application is not immediate job replacement but a season of vocational re-orientation where you examine whether your identity was in your work rather than in Christ. Practically: resist the panic to restore income and status immediately. Spend the first 60-90 days prioritizing daily communion with God—Scripture, prayer, confession—and let vocational clarity emerge from that renewed center. When you do re-enter work, the goal is not to prove yourself but to serve as one who knows they are already accepted in Christ. Expect your relationship to work to change—it becomes a sphere of stewardship, not a source of worth.

Church community and isolation after exposure

After a public moral failure, the impulse is to withdraw from church—shame makes presence unbearable, and the fear of judgment feels insurmountable. The sermon's warning against isolation (unit 25) applies directly: staying away wastes the crisis by confirming the lie that you are alone in your brokenness. The application is counter-instinctual: show up. Sit in the back if you need to, but be present in the body. Confess to a trusted elder or small group leader and ask for accountability and prayer. Resist the enemy's whisper that you've forfeited your place in the community. Practically: expect some people to be awkward or unkind—that pain is part of the harvest. But also expect the discovery that the church is full of people who have failed and been restored, and that your crisis now equips you to minister to others uniquely (unit 30). Let the crisis deepen your love for the body, not separate you from it. The goal is not to get back to where you were in church leadership or reputation but to discover a new kind of belonging—as a sinner openly dependent on grace.

Personal devotion and spiritual disciplines

Crisis often produces temporary intensity in Bible reading and prayer—panic drives you to God. The sermon warns that around 60-90 days, when acute pain fades, the old casual approach to spiritual disciplines returns (unit 29). The application is to establish sustainable rhythms during the crisis that outlast the emergency. Practically: set a daily time for Scripture and prayer that is modest enough to maintain—20 minutes is better than ambitious 90-minute sessions that collapse after two weeks. Use the crisis to build the habit of talking to God throughout the day, not just in formal devotions (unit 35). The goal is not to become a super-saint but to normalize communion with God as the baseline of your life. Expect that maintaining these rhythms will feel boring once the crisis urgency fades—do them anyway. The test of whether the crisis bore fruit is not how intensely you prayed in week two but whether you are still talking to God in month six.

Money and possessions after financial self-destruction

Financial crisis through overspending, debt, gambling, or fraudulent schemes often focuses all attention on budgets, payment plans, and recovery strategies. The sermon redirects: the financial disaster is a symptom of a deeper relational crisis with God. Ask: what were you seeking in spending, risk, or accumulation that only God can give? Security? Identity? Power? The application is to use the financial wilderness as an opportunity to learn dependence. Practically: establish basic budgets and payment plans, but do not make financial recovery your primary goal. Instead, use the enforced simplicity to discover whether you can be content with less—this is the test of whether money was an idol. Expect the temptation to panic and scramble for quick recovery; resist it. Let the season teach you to pray for daily bread rather than hoarding for security. The goal is not wealth restoration but freedom from the tyranny of money—becoming a person who holds possessions lightly because your treasure is elsewhere.

Pictures that resonate

Illustrations & Resonances

The Prodigal's Famine as Divine Kindness

literary

In Jesus's parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15), the younger son's moral failure—squandering his inheritance in reckless living—led to a famine that left him feeding pigs and longing to eat their pods. The text says he 'came to himself' in that moment of desperation. The famine was not punishment but rescue. Had the money lasted longer, the son would have continued his self-destruction in comfort. The crisis of hunger broke the spell of far-country illusion and turned his heart toward home. The father's joy at his return revealed that the famine was severe mercy—it hurt, but it saved. The son returned not to recover his inheritance but to be a servant; instead, he received a robe, a ring, and a feast.

Connection

The prodigal's famine illustrates the sermon's claim that crisis is God bringing you back to himself. The point was never the pigs or the hunger; it was the son's relational distance from his father. The crisis succeeded when it produced homecoming, not when it produced better financial planning.

Augustine's Garden Conversion

historical

In Confessions Book 8, Augustine describes his years of intellectual pride and sexual addiction as a life of restlessness and internal division. He knew Christian truth intellectually but could not break free from sin. In a Milan garden, under spiritual crisis and torment, he heard a child's voice singing 'Take up and read.' He opened Paul's letter to the Romans and encountered the call to put on Christ and make no provision for the flesh. In that moment of exposure and surrender, the chains of habitual sin broke. Augustine's conversion was not a decision to try harder but a relational return to the God he had been fleeing. His post-conversion life centered on communion with God as the soul's supreme joy, not merely on moral improvement.

Connection

Augustine's crisis illustrates the sermon's distinction between behavior modification and relational restoration. The goal was not a better Augustine managing his impulses but an Augustine who found God more satisfying than any created pleasure. The garden was his valley of Achor, the door of hope.

The Neuroscience of Habit Interruption

scientific

Neuroscience research on habit formation reveals that established patterns create neural pathways in the brain that operate below conscious decision-making. Breaking a destructive habit requires more than willpower—it requires disrupting the environmental cues and replacing the behavior with a new pattern that satisfies the same underlying need. Crisis functions neurologically as a massive disruptor: the exposure, shame, and consequences obliterate the familiar environment and routine, forcing the brain into new territory. This is why people often report that their worst moments became turning points—the neural autopilot was interrupted. But research also shows that once the initial disruption fades and familiar contexts return, the old pathways reassert themselves unless new patterns have been deeply established.

Connection

This scientific insight illuminates the sermon's warning in unit 29 that you will be tempted to treat old sins casually once initial brokenness fades. The crisis disrupted the habit loop, but sustainable change requires establishing new patterns during the disruption. The sermon's emphasis on daily communion with God is precisely the replacement pattern that must be built into the neural architecture during the crisis window.
