"Oswald applies the Peter passage to contemporary struggles with indwelling sin, arguing that many believers worship the future better version of themselves rather than Christ. He warns against this sneaky self-righteousness and supports the point with Jesus's parable of the Pharisee and the tax collector, showing that self-exaltation leads to condemnation while humility leads to justification."

Illustrations· 2
  1. The Red Thread analogy · unit #9 — Oswald illustrates the exchange of giver and gift with an extended metaphor of a young man finding a red thread in the snow. The selfish man cuts the thread and takes it; the wise man follows it to discover the beautiful woman who left it. Sin is cutting the thread — seizing God's good gifts (sex, marriage, wealth) rather than following them to their Creator. The illustration makes vivid the foolishness of exchanging giver for gift.
  2. The Rejected Son hypothetical · unit #14 — Oswald illustrates the exchange of giver and gift with two hypothetical scenarios: a parent who loves the idea of babies but not their child, and a parent who loves football but not their son. These are disgusting tragedies, yet this is how we treat God — loving His gifts but not Him. The twist: the rejected son lays down his life in love for the parent who rejected him. This is the gospel — Jesus pursues the very ones who turned their backs on Him.
Theological claims· 8
  1. Self-righteousness is the last idol rooted out of the heart and remains the primary spiritual battle throughout the Christian life until death. unit #5
  2. Both sins in Acts 10 involve worshiping a gift of God rather than God Himself — Peter worships the law, and Cornelius worships the messenger. unit #7
  3. Sin is essentially the exchange of the giver and the gift — pursuing God's good gifts while rejecting Him as the One who made them. unit #8
  4. Peter worships the law instead of the Lawgiver, and Cornelius worships Peter instead of God — both exchange the giver for the gift, worshiping wonderful things that were meant to point them to God. unit #10
  5. Sin is explicitly religious — both Peter's and Cornelius's sins are rooted in worship, and the enemies of the Lord often have the appearance of godliness while denying its power. unit #11
  6. The most glorious commonality between these sins is that both are forgiven — God is patient and steadfast, and He offers forgiveness through Jesus Christ to all who believe. unit #13
  7. God actively pursues His enemies through Jesus Christ, offering Himself up on the cross to pay for the very sins that rejected Him. unit #15
  8. Jesus is appointed by God as judge of the living and the dead, and when we compare ourselves to His sinless obedience, we see that we do not measure up. unit #16

That you were not so much into God as you were a gift that God had, or a thing God was doing, or a thing God promised to do. And the fickleness of our hearts has been revealed, if you'll think about it, to see that indeed we, we have loved the gifts more than the giver plenty of times. Now think about this. Imagine that son of yours who you decided you didn't love anymore after he stopped playing football. Imagine like he spent his whole life devoted to taking care took care of you. Imagine he like went out and got a good job and made sure that you— you got sick and couldn't work and he made sure you were taken care of. He comes over to your house all the time and makes sure that you've got the food you need and so on and so forth. What if that son who you just stopped loving because he doesn't play football anymore, what if he just laid down his life and loved you forever? That's the gospel story. The very one we jettison in favor of the stuff He makes, the very One we turn our back on when He doesn't produce the things we want, the very One we give up on, this text says in Acts 10:34-43, this is the One who pursued us. The One we got rid of, the One we jettisoned, the One that we let go of, the One we turned our back on, He pursued us.

15 · Oswald traces God's active work through Acts 10:36-43: God sends His Son to preach peace, anoints Jesus with the Spirit, raises Him from the dead, chooses witnesses, and appoints Him as judge

And you can see if you look through verses 36-43 that God is just doing all sorts of work over and over again. God is mentioned as being active in all of this. Verse 36: As for the word that he sent to Israel, what does God do when he decides to come to earth? I would picture, you know, robot destroying these terrible people kind of thing. No, he sends his Son to preach peace to his enemies. Verse 38, God anoints Jesus with the Holy Spirit and power, and Jesus goes about doing good. Why? For God was with him. Verse 39 and 40, the people killed Jesus, God raised him up. Verse 41, God chose witnesses of the resurrection. Verse 42, God appointed Jesus to be the judge of the living and the dead. So the same person, the same being who is infinitely worthy of our worship, who's given us lavishly, all amazing things. The same person we keep rejecting, keep turning our back on, keep giving up, He pursues these very people all the way to the cross, offers Himself up to pay for the very sins which consistently jettisoned Him from our hearts.

16 · Oswald focuses on Jesus as judge (Acts 10:42-43), a neglected piece of the gospel

Let's lock in on verses 42-43 for a second. We'll be done. And he commanded us to preach to the people and to testify that he is the one appointed by God to be the judge of the living and the dead. To him all the prophets bear witness that everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name. This picture of Jesus as judge, as we'll see later on when we talk about the essence of evangelism, this picture of Jesus as judge is probably one of the most neglected pieces of the gospel story. But here's what we want to do right now, Lord willing, through the power of the Spirit, power of His Word. You have felt your sin judged today. You have identified right now through this process, you have identified that when compared to Jesus Christ, you simply don't measure up. Jesus Christ did not consider equality with God something to be grasped. He didn't even presume, though it was entirely His right, He didn't even presume self-worship. He gave Himself up entirely. And of course, the whole interesting prospect of being God on earth is this idea that all of these things that you've created are now sort of hung out there like like the thread. Do you follow the thread to the Father, or do you just grab it and move on? And Jesus was tempted like we are, yet without sin, so that even in the garden, when he knew the bitter cup of the cross was coming, he's like, Lord, your will. Father, your will be done, not mine.

17 · Oswald calls the congregation to compare themselves to Jesus, the coming judge, which exposes self-righteousness and the fickleness of their love

And so you should compare yourself. This will get rid of all your self-righteousness right now. Just compare yourself to Jesus. Because that's the judgment that— that's the only judgment that matters, and that's the only judgment that's coming. And compare yourself to the judge, Jesus, and then understand this remarkable idea. And honestly, it should— it should floor you. Judge yourself against Jesus. See the fickleness of your love. See the many ways you've exchanged creation over Creator. And then realize Jesus has not done any of that, and then realize this: He is offering to give you His righteousness. He's offering to give you His love for the Father that never got sidetracked or awash in idolatry. He will give that to you for free, and He will take your terrible track record and forgive it. Understand this moment, just even right now on an average January Sunday morning, this moment is the kind of moment in which people are born again simply because sin has been judged and Jesus has made his offer. I will give you my righteousness and take your sin away. That's beautiful. That's, that's extraordinary.

18 · Oswald closes with a prayer of worship, acknowledging the clarity of sin revealed in the sermon and the fickleness of the congregation's hearts toward God

Let's take a moment to pray and worship the Lord who made this incredible offer. Lord, we just bow our hearts before you right now, and we just say The clarity of the sinfulness of our sin, Lord, has been put on display today to some degree. We have seen the fickleness of our hearts toward you. We have seen the tendency to love and make our lives about things you've made.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the essence of sin—the worship of God's gifts rather than God Himself—from its roots in self-righteousness through its religious disguise, to the glorious exchange Christ offers at the cross.

Monday Luke 18:10-14

The Pharisee's prayer reveals what the sermon calls 'the sneaky self-righteousness'—we worship our own moral performance and future improved selves rather than Christ. Notice how the tax collector's plea for mercy, not his self-improvement, is what justifies him; we too are invited to abandon confidence in our own righteousness and grip only the mercy of God in the gospel.

Tuesday Romans 1

Paul traces idolatry to its root: humanity 'exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images' (1:23), worshiping the creation rather than the Creator. This is precisely the pattern the sermon identifies in both Peter and Cornelius—each exchanges reverence for God Himself in favor of what He has given (the law, the messenger). We see here that sin is fundamentally this inversion of worship.

Wednesday 2 Timothy 3:1-7

Paul warns of those who have 'the appearance of godliness, but deny its power' (3:5)—religious language and pious behavior masking hearts that serve themselves. Peter's scrupulosity about the law and Cornelius's honor paid to Peter both wear the clothes of devotion while they worship the gift rather than the Giver. We must ask ourselves where our own religious practices mask a subtle exchange.

Thursday Philippians 2:6

Christ, though 'equal with God,' emptied Himself and humbled Himself unto death on the cross (2:6-8)—the ultimate reversal of all idolatry. While we were still exchanging the Creator for His gifts, Christ descended to bear our sin and restore us to true worship. This is the stunning pursuit of grace: the very One we reject pursues us to reconciliation.

Friday Acts 10:34-43

In Peter's proclamation to Cornelius's household, the gospel invitation stands clear: 'Everyone who believes in him receives forgiveness of sins through his name' (10:43). The exchange has been made—Christ's perfect obedience for our sin, His righteousness for our idolatry. This announcement transforms both Peter and Cornelius from worshipers of gifts into worshipers of the Giver, born anew into the family of God.

Pray together this week

The Exchange of Gifts for the Giver

Father, we come before You in worship, acknowledging that You alone are worthy of our affection and allegiance. You are the Giver of every good gift, and yet we confess that we so often exchange You for the gifts You have made. Like Peter, we cling to our own righteousness and the law itself rather than loving the Lawgiver; like Cornelius, we bow before the messenger instead of worshiping the One who sent him (Acts 10:25-26). We are prone to worship the future, better version of ourselves — a subtle idolatry that masquerades as godliness while denying its power. We exchange the Creator for creation, clothing our self-worship in religious language and calling it obedience. Forgive us for this essential sin that clings to us even now.

Yet we glory in this: Jesus Christ, in His immeasurable grace, has pursued us as His enemies and offered Himself on the cross to pay the very debt our worship of gifts has incurred (Acts 10:42-43). He stands as the Judge of all the living and the dead, and in His sinless obedience, He has accomplished what we never could. In the gospel, we are offered the most glorious exchange — He gives us His perfect righteousness, and He takes our sin upon Himself. We are born again through this substitution, this radical grace that humbles us and fills us with gratitude.

Grant us grace, O God, to recognize the idols we worship in the quiet places of our hearts. Teach us to love the Giver, not merely His gifts. Make us a people compelled by the gospel to turn from every false worship and to bow before Christ alone. Give us courage to confess these things to one another, knowing that we are forgiven and accepted in Him. We commit ourselves afresh to the glad pursuit of Christlikeness, resting in the finished work of our Savior. To Jesus Christ, who has taken our sin and given us His righteousness, be glory and dominion forever and ever.
