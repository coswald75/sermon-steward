Theological claims· 11
  1. A six-stage pattern governs how leaders bring people through God-driven change in Scripture and in all leadership contexts. unit #1
  2. The central leadership problem of our age is not that leaders face crises of confidence, but that they fail to pass through them successfully. unit #7
  3. The six-stage pattern of call, change, conflict, cost, complaint, and crisis is universal to all leadership that follows God's vision. unit #15
  4. Leaders bear ultimate responsibility not for experiencing a crisis of confidence but for yielding to the anxious crowd rather than obeying God. unit #21
  5. The crisis of confidence is inevitable in godly leadership; the crucial question is how the leader responds when it comes. unit #27
  6. The first step in overcoming the crisis of confidence is to pray, even if the prayer is imperfect. unit #29
  7. Prayer is the act of promising to give God the last word instead of letting human anxiety determine the outcome. unit #30
  8. Prayer functions as a decompression chamber, preventing the leader from making impulsive, anxiety-driven decisions. unit #31
  9. Leaders must distinguish between love for the people and love for the people's approval—the latter is a sinful motive that will compromise leadership. unit #39
  10. If the leader's motive is to be loved by the people rather than to love them, he will compromise when approval is withdrawn. unit #41
  11. The doctrine of trials teaches that opposition and suffering are normal when following God, and complaints from followers are to be expected under hardship. unit #56

You need to get your mind set in the right direction with prayer. Get your eyes off of yourself. Get your eyes off the problem. Get your eyes off the people. And pray. Now you also need to get your heart settled on the right devotion.

39 · Oswald introduces the second remedy: getting the heart settled on right devotion by distinguishing between two similar but morally distinct motivations—love for the people versus love for being loved by the people

Well, what do we mean by the right devotion? Well, it is hard enough to lead people when your motives are pure. We said a moment ago that the crisis of leadership is magnified in some sense by the leader's love for the people. He hates to see them suffer for something he's initiated. True enough. But the heart of the leader is never pure. It's never pure. Only Christ's heart in leadership is pure. So no doubt, all of us have to deal with a second motivation. And it looks like the other one. And that is, in addition to being motivated by a love for the people, another sinful motivation could be creeping in. And that is, I am motivated by the people's love for me.

40 · Oswald illustrates the distinction between the two loves with a hypothetical scenario of a young husband who compromises discipline because he loves the feeling of being his wife's hero

And these two things don't appear to be very different. When you're a young man and you just got married, you would not believe how much of a temptation this is to be your wife's hero and let all kinds of things slide. Because it's not because you're motivated by love for her. That's what it feels like. But what you're really motivated for by is you love to feel loved by her.

41 · Oswald asserts that leaders must confront and expunge the sinful motive of needing to be loved

So in addition to turning our hearts to God and getting our minds right with prayer, we've got to sort this out. We've got to learn the difference between I'm doing this because I love you or I'm doing this or not doing this because I want you to love me. Because you can't have that sometimes as a leader. Sometimes that's taken off the table. You don't get to feel loved by the people you're leading. Now, if your love for the person is pure, you'll go talk to God and you'll get fortified in confidence in the plan and God and your calling and you'll stay faithful. But if you don't get this darker motive expunged, it's not going to work.

42 · Oswald provides a contemporary example of the people-pleasing motive in action—pastors compromising biblical teaching on sexuality and gender because they love human approval more than God's

Where did all the pastors go over the last 15 years on the controversial issues of our day? Why have so many abandoned God's clear biblical teaching about human sexuality and gender? Why are so few pulpits focused on the sins of the people in the room? Why has the rainbow mafia been so effective in moving previously faithful pastors, churches, and entire denominations off of the clear teaching of God's word? John 12, 43 has the answer. For they love the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God.

43 · Oswald applies the John 12:43 diagnosis directly to fathers in the room, arguing that if pastors fall prey to people-pleasing, fathers certainly do—especially in parenting, where discipline is often compromised by the desire to be liked

Now, friends, if that's true, if you can see that clearly in the pulpits in America, boy, friends, you don't think that's true at your dinner table? You don't think you secretly want to be the hero and get manipulated out of doing the right thing? You don't think that many of your discipline issues with your own children come down to what you have broadly communicated to them in a language they don't speak, and you don't speak, but that language is, I really want you to like me. My discipline is informed by that. By my desire for you to like me.

44 · Oswald returns to Exodus 4:29-31, showing the moment when Moses and Aaron are received with adoration by the elders—a moment ripe for the temptation to love being loved

Back in Exodus 4, 29, remember this scene. Then Moses and Aaron went and gathered together all the elders. This is before all the bad stuff happens. Moses and Aaron went and gathered together all the elders of the people of Israel. Aaron spoke all the words that the Lord had spoken to Moses and did the signs in the sight of the people, and the people believed. And when they heard that the Lord had visited the people of Israel and that he had seen their affliction, they bowed their heads and worshiped. Would anyone be surprised if in that moment, Moses thought, well, now I could get used to this. He appears on the scene, having been absent for quite some time, walks into the elders of Israel, shares the good news of God's promised deliverance, and the people look at him as the champion they had been waiting for. And they bow their heads and worship in gratitude to God for sending this man to accomplish the impossible. You don't think there was a little bit of temptation to love that feeling?

45 · Oswald acknowledges from personal experience that the temptation to love being loved is persistent in leadership

Well, I've been leading things for a long time, and it's always a temptation. It's always a temptation.

46 · Oswald contrasts the adoration of Exodus 4 with the hostility of Exodus 5:20-21, showing how quickly approval can turn to condemnation

But in the next chapter, in verse 20 of chapter 5, all of their goodwill toward Moses has completely reversed. They met Moses and Aaron who were waiting for them as they came out from Pharaoh, and they said, the Lord look on you and judge because you have made us stink in the sight of Pharaoh and his servants and have put a sword in their hand to kill us. And to be honest, that is going to be what Moses experiences pretty much every day until he dies.

47 · Oswald assures the congregation that they will not face Moses' level of opposition constantly, but they will face it sometimes

That is not what you in the new covenant will experience as you lead your brothers and sisters. You will not experience this level consistently. But you'll experience it sometimes. The pastor comes in and says, I have a novel idea. Let's preach against the sins in the congregation. Well, there are people in that congregation who have a lot invested in the status quo. And they will create a stink. And that stink will affect other people's opinions so that now all the people are questioning, is this guy really the guy? So now there's grumbling. And that grumbling in various ways reaches the pastor. And the pastor begins to have a crisis of confidence. And he's like, well, maybe I'm not the guy. Maybe I'm not doing this right. This happens all the time, friends.

48 · Oswald applies the two remedies to the contemporary pastoral scenario: turn to God in prayer, and purify the heart's motives

So what do we do? We turn our eyes to God. We say, Lord, you're going to get the last word. Not my complaints, not their complaints. You're going to get the last word. We double down and make sure that in our leadership there's not a motivation that is really, and by the way, there will be. This crisis of confidence is where this darker motive goes to die. Or, it's where it goes to be in charge. But, in good cases, it's where it goes to die. We double down and we're like, Lord, I absolutely love these people, or this person that I'm leading, but I have to absolutely insist that I not be motivated by seeking their approval. I have to seek your approval, Lord. I can't love the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God.

49 · Oswald transitions to the third remedy: filling the soul with right doctrine

And number three, fill your soul with the right doctrine. Fill your soul with the right doctrine. Set your mind in the right direction in prayer. Set your heart up right. Get rid of that darker motive. And now, let's say, let's fill our soul with right doctrine.

50 · Oswald returns to Exodus 5:22-23 and identifies Moses' theological error: accusing God of doing evil

Look back at verse 22. Then Moses turned to the Lord and said, O Lord, why have you done evil to this people? Why did you ever send me? For since I came to Pharaoh to speak in your name, he has done evil to this people, and you have not delivered your people at all. Let's focus in on this phrase. O Lord, why have you done evil to this people? Well, this is just plain wrong. This is bad doctrine. James 1.13 says, Let no one say when he is tempted, I am being tempted by God. For God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one. Moses' theology isn't as sharp or refined as ours can be with the new covenant. He is asking why.

51 · Oswald notes that New Testament leaders do not ask "why" when suffering comes—they have a better theological framework

By the time we see the spiritual leaders in the New Testament dealing with this issue, they're not asking why. Jesus has made it real clear to them that why is a dumb question. The right question, by the way, isn't why, it's how long. That's the biblical question. But we'll get to that in a moment, maybe. Okay, so by the time we get to the New Testament, all of our spiritual leaders are not asking why anymore. They're not provoked in this state, at least not publicly like Moses is. They're saying other things.

52 · Oswald cites 1 Peter 4:12 to show that Peter teaches believers not to be surprised by trials—a more mature theology than Moses' surprise and accusation

For instance, in 1 Peter, Peter is able to say, Beloved, this is in 1 Peter 4, Beloved, do not be surprised at the fiery trial when it comes upon you to test you as though something strange were happening to you. Don't be surprised by this. Moses was surprised. We don't have to be surprised.

53 · Oswald reads 1 Peter 5:8-10, extracting the doctrine of trials: trials are not strange, they are universal among believers, they are temporary, and God will restore the sufferer

In chapter 5, be sober-minded, be watchful. Your adversary, the devil, prowls around like a roaring lion seeking someone to devour. Resist him, firm in your faith, knowing that the same kinds of suffering are being experienced by your brotherhood throughout the world. And after you've suffered a little while, the God of all grace who has called you to his eternal glory in Christ will restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you. To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen. Don't be surprised. This isn't strange. That's the basic doctrine I'm talking about. You need a doctrine of trials. And the first piece of the doctrine is they're not strange. They're not novel. These kinds of things are experienced by all the brothers all over the world. No need to ask why. If you want to ask how long, the answer will always be in a little while.

54 · Oswald cites 2 Corinthians 4:17 to show Paul's teaching that trials are light and momentary compared to eternal glory, reinforcing the doctrine that suffering is temporary

which is honestly totally true. In 2 Corinthians 4, Paul says that these trials, these sufferings are light and momentary compared to the eternal weight of glory prepared for us. The answer is always in a little while, which is always true at some level and usually true practically. These don't last as long as we think they will.

55 · Oswald shows that the "little while" proved practically true for the Israelites—the plagues came quickly, shutting down Pharaoh's economy and ending the crisis sooner than expected

The Hebrews did not have to endure this deplorable state much longer at all. God sort of starts shutting down the Egyptian economy pretty quickly after this. There's no more work. Can't work when there's no light. Can't work when there's flies everywhere. He shuts it all down pretty quick. You can't make bricks when you don't have water and all you've got is blood. So they didn't actually have to wait that long. Moses' nerve didn't have to hold too long actually.

56 · Oswald reviews the six-stage pattern, then introduces the three remedies (prayer, purified motives, right doctrine), and then expounds the doctrine of trials: trials are normal, opposition is inevitable when following God, and complaints are to be expected

So let's just review the call. God gives you some clarity that in some way or another the people that the thing you're leading needs to adjust to be more in conformity with God's will. The change, you introduce the change to the thing you're leading. A conflict emerges because there are forces settled in the status quo and those forces could be Satan, it could be the world, or they could just be the flesh of the people you're leading. A cost emerges, the followers begin to feel friction, there are complaints, and then we have a crisis of confidence at which time we turn to the Lord, we get our hearts settled on like real love and not using people to feel good about ourselves. Not using our kids to feel good about ourselves. Guys, come on. And then we fill our souls with right doctrine and we process what God has said explicitly about trials. Trials are a part of the Christian life. When we follow God, we find opposition. All who desire to live a godly life will be persecuted, 1 Timothy 3.12. Through many tribulations, we must enter the kingdom of God, Acts 14.22. If you were of the world, the world would love you as its own, but because you are not of the world, but I chose you out of the world, therefore the world hates you. Remember the world, the word that I said to you, a servant is not greater than his master. If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you. If they keep my word, they will also keep yours. John 15.19. Sometimes we'll upset the world, sometimes we'll upset the flesh of our followers, sometimes we'll step on Satan's tail and remind him that his time is short. In all these ways, a conflict and a cost emerge and to ask people not to complain under that is asking a lot. It can happen, but we shouldn't expect it to happen. We should expect people to complain who are going through hardship. Hopefully they grow, but that's really not up to us in the immediate term. And when we lead people through godly change, there's always going to be a cost.

57 · Oswald returns to Piper's diagnosis of late-20th-century culture: the virtues of endurance and perseverance are absent, replaced by self-esteem and self-realization

Now back to this thing that Piper was talking about in this biography of Charles Simeon. He goes on, when historians list the character traits of the last third of the 20th century, commitment, constancy, tenacity, endurance, patience, resolve, and perseverance will not be on that list. The list will begin with an all-consuming interest in self-esteem. It will be followed by the subheadings of self-assertiveness and self-enhancement and self-realization. And if you think that you are not at all a child of your times, just test yourself to see how you respond in the ministry or leadership when people reject your ideas. We need help here, Piper says.

58 · Piper introduces Charles Simeon as the historical example of endurance—a pastor who served faithfully for 49 years under fierce opposition by learning to settle his confidence in the Lord

When you are surrounded by a society of emotionally fragile quitters, and when you see a good bit of this ethos in yourself, you need to spend time with people, whether dead or alive, whose lives prove there is another way to live. And so he introduces a pastor named Charles Simeon from 1830s. Pastor Simeon served 49 years in the ministry and almost all of it was marked by fierce opposition. He remained faithful and joyful in large part because he had learned how to live in this crisis of confidence, peace, and always wind up settling down in the Lord.

59 · Simeon's famous quotation is delivered: suffering for Christ is bearable because Christ has already passed through—our head has made it through the hedge, and we follow him

He was asked by a friend, how did you endure for so long so well? And he said, my dear brother, we must not mind a little suffering for Christ's sake. My dear brother, we must not mind a little suffering for Christ's sake. When I am getting through a hedge, if my head and shoulders are safely through, I can bear prickling my legs. Let us rejoice in remembrance that our holy head has surmounted all his suffering and triumphed over death. Let us follow him patiently, we shall soon be partakers of his victory.

60 · Oswald unpacks Simeon's hedge metaphor: the hedge represents the painful passage from one state to another

So the word picture is a little localized to England where Simeon served. These hedges are thorny and very thick brush and they use them to separate one piece of land from another. And to walk through a hedge, you're going to scrape your face, you're going to scrape your arms, you're going to scrape your legs. They're not meant to walk through. They're meant to keep animals from passing through. So if you were to walk through a hedge, it's a very uncomfortable situation. The image that Simeon's presenting is of moving from one land to another, of moving from the status quo into the thing that you're called to lead. And he says, you know, as long as you can get your face through, that's the hard part, get your head through. You can see the other side. And he's like, there's still a lot of scrapes to endure, but you've gotten your head through and you can see where you're going and you're kind of almost as good as through, even though you're not. And then he reminds us that it is actually Christ who has stuck his head out. It is actually Christ who has, in his own obedience to the Father, crossed from this land into the next. And he's the first fruits of the resurrection. Christ, our head, has already entered into the place we will join him in. He said, yeah, there's still some of the body to push through the thorns, but our head is through.

61 · Oswald introduces God's response to Moses by framing prayer as the best deal ever—bad prayers receive great promises in return

So how does God respond to Moses' very imperfect prayer? He provides a very perfect promise. And this is the deal with prayer. It's such a great deal. You guys love getting good deals. Prayer's the best one ever. You give a really terrible prayer and in return, God gives a really great promise. It's a great exchange rate.

62 · Oswald reads Exodus 6:6-8, highlighting the threefold "I will" structure as evidence of a perfect promise—God's response to Moses' imperfect prayer is redemption, deliverance, and covenant relationship

Moses comes with his rather terrible prayer and God responds with literally in the Hebrew, this would be a literal idea of perfection. He responds with a perfect promise. Threefold repetitions are perfect. A lot of you know that. So in chapter 6, verse 6, he responds to Moses, say therefore to the people of Israel, I am the Lord. I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians. I will deliver you from slavery to them and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great acts of judgment and I will take you to be my people. I will be your God and you shall know that I am the Lord who has brought you out from the burdens of the Egyptians. I will deliver you. I will redeem you. I will make you my people.

63 · Oswald reads Exodus 6:9 and makes the critical observation: the promise did not restore the people's confidence, but it restored the leader's confidence

Now, as I mentioned already, these words of assurance were not enough for the people. Verse 9, Moses spoke thus to the people of Israel, but they did not listen to Moses because of their broken spirit and harsh slavery. Moses didn't pray a prayer and find everybody suddenly on his side again. He prayed a prayer and suddenly found himself on God's side again. Promises weren't enough for the people, but they were enough for the leader. And so the crisis of confidence is navigated successfully. Humanly speaking, the whole Exodus project depended on whether Moses would or not trust the Lord in his leadership task.

64 · Oswald applies the sermon directly to the leaders in the room, acknowledging the difficulty and the need for community and prayer, while insisting that much depends on their ability to navigate the crisis successfully

Leaders in the room today, husbands, fathers, it's not easy. It's not easy. It's not. You're going to need each other. You're going to need to spend much time in prayer. But I want to be clear. Humanly speaking, much depends on you being able to navigate these things. of giving the Lord the last word in times of anxiousness, of getting your own heart sorted out and doing the difference of I love you and I love to be loved by you, of seeing that trials are just a part of the story when we choose to follow God.

65 · Oswald concludes by echoing Simeon's phrase ("we must not mind a little suffering") and quoting 1 Peter 5:10 as the final word—suffering is temporary, and God will restore and establish the faithful leader

Brothers, leaders, we must not mind a little suffering when we lead into the light. When we lead into the light, the dark will always react. We must not be surprised by the fiery trials that follow our turn toward the Lord. We must just simply say that after we have suffered a little while, the God of all grace who has called us to his eternal glory in Christ will himself restore, confirm, strengthen, and establish you. To him be the dominion forever and ever. Amen.

66 · Oswald prays for leaders in the congregation to understand that trouble follows obedience, to turn to God when the crisis comes, to confess sins of people-pleasing, and to emerge from the service with renewed confidence in God

Let me pray. Oh, Father God, we pray that you would use what we see in your word to see just how clearly you care about leadership, how important it is to you, how you choose to work through leaders, and then, Lord, in whatever capacity we have leaders in this room today, we're focusing on fathers because it's Father's Day. We have women who are leading many things here as well. Just pray, God, that you would help every person who is called to lead something, no matter how big or small, understand that when they lead their thing toward you, there's trouble on the horizon. That they are too, when they encounter that trouble, turn to you and ask you to do the work on their hearts necessary for them to remain faithful and ask them, Lord, to just, and help them to see that this is just a part of the process of loving people with leadership. So, Lord, I pray your blessings. If anyone here is in a current crisis of confidence, Lord, help them emerge even from the sanctuary today with renewed confidence in you. if anyone here needs to confess sins of getting mixed up in the love dynamic and beginning to confuse one kind of love with the other and essentially seeing that sometimes their actions are dictated by their desire to please rather than their desire to be a blessing. Lord, would you just give them grace to sort through those issues or do you even just heal those wounds and put health in those spots in their heart? God, would you just build us together as a group of people who are not surprised by fire trials but just double down in our trust of you? We love you, Lord. In Jesus' name we pray. Amen.

67 · Oswald transitions to communion by contrasting human love (always mixed with need) with Christ's love (entirely pure and requiring nothing in return)

For communion, I just invite you to come and remember the one person who ever loved you perfectly who never did it because he was needing you to love him back. Jesus entered this world full of the Father's love. He was not looking for some insecurity to be resolved. He did not trust himself to man for he knew what was in the heart of man. And so this great God is really the only person you'll ever meet who did something for you with literally just absolutely no need on the other end. What a beautiful thing it is to be loved by this great God and we celebrate his love when we partake at the table.

68 · Oswald recites the words of institution from 1 Corinthians 11 and invites the congregation to partake of communion as a reminder of Christ's perfect love

For I received from the Lord what I also delivered to you that the Lord Jesus on the night when he was betrayed took bread and when he'd given thanks he broke it and said this is my body which is for you do this in remembrance of me. In the same way also he took the cup after supper saying this cup is the new covenant in my blood do this as often as you drink it in remembrance of me. for as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes. Would you now come and partake of this table that is meant to instruct you week after week the great love of your Lord and Savior Jesus Christ. Amen.
