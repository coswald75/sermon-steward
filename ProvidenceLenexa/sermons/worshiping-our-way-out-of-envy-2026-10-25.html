  1. analogy · unit #23 — Lakemacher uses Minnesota ice fishing as an extended analogy for Psalm 73's depiction of the wicked. Theological claims· 5
  1. Believers cannot control their circumstances, but they can control their response; Asaph's envy arose from trying to understand the world without God rather than from the fact of the wicked's prosperity itself. unit #11
  2. Every struggle in the human heart, including Asaph's envy of the wicked, is fundamentally a struggle with God's character—his goodness, justice, and omniscience—making all inner battles worship battles. unit #16
  3. Christian transformation happens not by looking inward (the world's approach) but by looking away from self to God, making the primary counseling task bringing struggling people into God's presence. unit #18
  4. Christians have greater confidence than Asaph to approach God in struggle because Christ's sacrifice grants direct access to the Father without the Old Testament sacrificial system's barriers. unit #19
  5. God reveals truth through worship without changing circumstances, and that truth reshapes the worshiper's response; additionally, the worshiper meets God himself and finds contentment and satisfaction in him. unit #27

and we church whatever you come in here this morning struggling with you can come into the house of god the presence of god because of the finished work of jesus christ and you can find truth revealed that's going to reorder you when the world seems disoriented it's going to combat lies but most importantly you're just going to get a glimpse of god himself who will begin to change your affections and your desires and reorder your worship and you can worship your way out of whatever you are struggling with

let me pray well father thank you so much for the good news of your word and the gospel thank you that we can humbly come into the presence into your presence because of the finished work of jesus christ on the cross thank you for the gospel thank you that you promised to give us help and grace in our time of need and thank you that you are constantly reordering our disordered worship so that we can follow you more faithfully have more joy and contentment in you for the glory of your great name in christ's name we pray amen

6 questions for your group this week

  1. Asaph begins Psalm 73 by affirming that God is good to Israel, yet by verse 2 he admits his feet had almost slipped. What does this reveal about the gap between what we know to be true about God and what we actually feel or believe in moments of struggle?
    Psalm 73:1-2
    → Can you think of a time when you affirmed something true about God intellectually but found yourself acting as though it weren't true? What was happening in your heart?
  2. In verses 4-12, Asaph catalogs the prosperity and apparent ease of the wicked in great detail. Why do you think he lingers on this description rather than moving past it quickly? What does his dwelling on these details suggest about how envy works in us?
    Psalm 73:4-12
  3. Asaph confesses in verse 13 that he has kept his heart pure and washed his hands in innocence—yet still suffers. What false assumption about how God operates is embedded in this complaint, and what does it reveal about what Asaph is actually worshiping in that moment?
    Psalm 73:13-15
    → What are the ways we similarly try to negotiate with God—'I've done X, so I deserve Y'—rather than worshiping him for who he is?
  4. The turning point comes when Asaph enters the sanctuary (verse 17). The sermon emphasizes that the circumstances don't change—the wicked are still prospering, Asaph still suffers—but *his perception* transforms through worship. How does encountering God's presence reorder what matters to us without removing the difficulty itself?
    Psalm 73:16-17
  5. Read Hebrews 4:14-16 aloud together. The sermon notes that Christians now have greater confidence than Asaph to approach God in struggle because of Christ's sacrifice. How does knowing you can bring your envy, your confusion, and your complaints directly into God's presence change the way you approach him when you're struggling?
    Hebrews 4:14-16
    → What feels risky or wrong about bringing raw struggle into worship rather than waiting until you've 'figured it out' first?
  6. By the end of the psalm, Asaph declares that God himself is his portion and his heart's desire (verses 25-26). The sermon emphasizes that transformation happens not by looking inward but by looking away to God. In your own life this week, where are you tempted to compare your portion with someone else's, and how might turning your gaze directly to God reshape that?
    Psalm 73:25-26

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through the transformation Asaph experienced—from envy rooted in lost sight of God, through humble entrance into his presence, to the reordering of his heart by encountering God's truth and finding his supreme satisfaction in God himself rather than in worldly gain.

Monday Hebrews 4:14-16

Asaph had to enter the sanctuary with ritual and distance; we enter the throne of grace directly through Christ's blood. The barriers have fallen. When envy grips us, when we cannot understand why the wicked prosper, we do not have to wait or qualify—we come boldly, with confidence, into God's presence itself. This is our inheritance through Christ.

Tuesday Matthew 22:37-39

Asaph's envy was not really about the wicked's wealth; it was a question about God's justice, goodness, and love. When we envy, we are asking whether God truly deserves our complete love and allegiance—or whether we should love the world's treasures instead. The Great Commandment shows us the heart of the matter: our envy reveals that our worship has been divided, and our primary work is to return our love wholly to God.

Wednesday Psalm 22:1

Even the Messiah cried out in darkness, feeling abandoned. Yet he did not abandon his Father. Asaph's confusion and pain, like Christ's cry in Gethsemane, did not disqualify him from approaching God—they qualified him to do so. Our circumstances may perplex us, but our response is always available to us: we can turn toward God or away. The one who turns toward him finds restoration.

Thursday 1 Chronicles 15

David gathered Israel not to analyze their feelings but to bring them into God's presence through worship and music. Asaph, as one of David's chief musicians, understood that the cure for a divided heart was not introspection but doxology—turning the whole self toward God in song and praise. When we gather to worship, we are participating in the same ancient remedy: we look away from our confusion and toward the God who is good and faithful.

Friday Matthew 7:13-14

The broad road that looks prosperous and easy leads to destruction; the narrow road that looks difficult and narrow leads to life. Asaph's envy ended not when the wicked fell (though they would) but when he saw clearly which road each was on. We need not wait for vindication in this life; worship opens our eyes to see the end from the beginning. God himself becomes our portion, and that satisfaction transcends any earthly gain.

Sunday-evening family table

When Someone Else's Good Feels Like Your Bad

For the parent

This prompt invites kids to name a real experience of wanting what someone else has—a toy, a skill, attention, or opportunity. The goal is to help them see that envy clouds their vision of what they actually have and that looking toward God (not toward the person with the thing) is what brings back joy.

Tell us about a time when you really wanted something because someone else had it. When you were looking at what they had, did it make the things you already have feel like they didn't matter anymore? What happened when you stopped looking at theirs and looked at something good in your own life instead?
works for ages 6+; younger kids may need help naming the feeling, but the experience is universal
