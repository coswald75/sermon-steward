Christ in Isaiah

December 10, 2023 Pastor Dr. James White
Thesis Isaiah 9:6-7 reveals Jesus as the incarnate Yahweh who exercises divine authority as Creator and brings true peace through his atoning work, and this prophetic text must be understood within the canonical context of Isaiah 6-11 where New Testament writers consistently identify the coming Messiah with Yahweh himself.

Illustrations· 3
  1. personal story · unit #17 — Dr. White uses a painting of the Emmaus road he keeps in his RV to illustrate the disciples' amazement at Jesus' exposition of the Old Testament, embedding the illustration within a personal anecdote about traveling ministry.
  2. historical example · unit #24 — Dr. White illustrates the Christological importance of affirming Jesus' true human birth by recounting Gnostic denial (Protevangelium of James, docetism) and tracing its influence into Roman Catholic Mariology, arguing that denying natural birth threatens the reality of the incarnation, crucifixion, and resurrection.
  3. historical example · unit #32 — Dr. White digresses to correct popular misconceptions about the nativity setting, explaining ancient Palestinian domestic architecture and arguing Jesus was likely born in the animal area of a relative's home rather than a separate stable.
Theological claims· 6
  1. New Testament writers apply Old Testament texts that speak uniquely of Yahweh—including attributes like immutability that belong to God alone—directly to Jesus, establishing his identity as Yahweh incarnate. unit #6
  2. Apologetic readiness flows from living under Christ's lordship (recognizing him as Yahweh), which transforms believers' responses to adversity in ways that prompt unbelievers to ask for reasons. unit #12
  3. Isaiah 9:6-7 must be interpreted within the canonical context of Isaiah 6-11, a section from which New Testament writers repeatedly drew to establish Christ's identity, likely reflecting Jesus' own post-resurrection instruction to the apostles. unit #16
  4. Observing how New Testament authors use Old Testament texts gives us access to Jesus' post-resurrection teaching and enables proper contextualization of familiar passages like Isaiah 9. unit #18
  5. The world and human political powers fear Christ not as an infant but as the one declared King of Kings and Lord of Lords who demands the submission of all earthly authority. unit #33
  6. The incarnation must be preached in light of its necessary end—the cross—because Jesus was born in the shadow of the cross, and he brings peace not merely by his birth but by the giving of himself in atoning death. unit #35

5-day reading plan

This week we trace how the New Testament writers found Jesus hidden throughout Isaiah, showing us that Christ's identity as Yahweh incarnate—and his mission to bring peace through the cross—was the consistent message of Scripture and the risen Lord's own teaching to his apostles.

Monday Hebrews 1:10-12

The writer of Hebrews quotes Psalm 102—a psalm addressed to Yahweh alone—and applies it entirely to the Son. Notice the bold claim: Jesus, like God the Father, is immutable and eternal, the one who laid the foundation of the earth and will never change. This is not metaphor or honorific language; it is direct identification of Jesus with the very nature and prerogatives of Yahweh.

Tuesday John 12:37-41

John tells us that Isaiah saw Christ's glory in the temple (Isaiah 6:1-3) and spoke about him. This means that what Isaiah saw—the thrice-holy God seated on his throne—was a vision of Christ. Jesus himself, in his post-resurrection appearances, must have opened the apostles' eyes to see him throughout Isaiah's prophecies, beginning with the throne-room vision in chapter 6.

Wednesday Acts 2:14-36

Peter's Pentecost sermon weaves together multiple Old Testament texts—Joel, Psalm 16, Psalm 110—to prove that Jesus is Lord and Messiah. This concatenation of cross-references wasn't Peter's innovation; it reflects how the risen Jesus taught the apostles to read Scripture. We inherit that same apostolic method when we learn to follow the New Testament's lead in finding Christ throughout the Old Testament.

Thursday 1 Peter 3:14-15

Peter commands us to sanctify Christ as Lord in our hearts and be ready to give a reason for the hope within us. Notice the sequence: *lordship* precedes *apologetics*. When we genuinely acknowledge Jesus as Yahweh—the King who demands all authority—our calm in suffering and joyful confidence become so countercultural that the world cannot ignore them. Our life becomes the question that prompts inquiry.

Friday Isaiah 53:4-6

Every prophecy of Christ's coming in Isaiah—his birth, his titles, his reign—culminates in the suffering servant who bears our griefs and carries our sorrows. The peace of Isaiah 9:7 is not a political arrangement or sentimental comfort, but the deep reconciliation won when he was pierced for our transgressions. This Christmas, we proclaim not an infant in a manger, but the Mighty God who came to die.

Pray together this week

Prayer: Christ the Mighty God and Prince of Peace

Father, we come before you in awe of the Christ revealed in Isaiah's prophecy—the one whose name declares him Mighty God, Eternal Father, and Prince of Peace. We marvel that the New Testament writers, following the risen Jesus himself, saw in these ancient words the very identity of your Son as Yahweh incarnate, the one to whom belong the attributes of immutability and eternal dominion that belong to you alone (Hebrews 1:10–12; Colossians 1:15–17). We worship him as the one before whom every earthly power must bow.

Yet we confess our weakness in grasping the fullness of what Christ's reign demands of us. We are content, as the world is, to sentimentalize Jesus in the manger—to admire his birth without submitting to his lordship. We struggle to live as those who truly recognize him as King of Kings and Lord of Lords, and we often fail to let that recognition transform our response to adversity and opposition. None of us perfectly demonstrates the readiness to give reasons for our hope that comes from living under Christ's absolute authority.

We thank you that the incarnation was never separate from the cross. Jesus was born in the shadow of his atoning death, and he brings us genuine peace—not through sentiment, but through the giving of himself in substitutionary sacrifice (Isaiah 53:4–6). In the gospel, we have access to his post-resurrection teaching through the very texts the apostles used, and we are invited to see in Isaiah 6–11 the whole arc of his identity and work. The peace he achieved is the foundation of all hope.

Grant us grace, we pray, to preach and proclaim the whole message of Advent—not a Jesus who remains safely in the manger, but the Christ who came to die and rise, who now reigns and will reign forever. Give us apostolic boldness to connect the titles of Isaiah 9:6–7 to the Jesus who demands our allegiance and transformation. Make us a people compelled by the recognition of his lordship to live in readiness, that our glad submission to Christ's reign becomes winsome testimony to a world that has not yet bowed the knee. To him be glory and dominion forever. Amen.
