Thesis Trials, not spiritual experiences or good works, reveal the tested genuineness of our faith by showing whether we love God for himself or merely use him to get what we want.

Theological claims· 8
  1. The names Samuel and Saul form a divine joke: Samuel represents God's faithful provision when we trust him; Saul represents God giving us what we demand even when it harms us. unit #2
  2. A king like all the other nations means a king who treats God transactionally — using religion for personal benefit rather than covenant relationship. unit #3
  3. Saul's story forces us to examine whether our faith is genuine — both in our own lives and in those around us in the church. unit #5
  4. Spiritual experiences — even tasting the Holy Spirit and being enlightened — cannot distinguish genuine faith from false faith, because God can grant such experiences to the non-elect who will ultimately fall away. unit #16
  5. Neither seeking God for blessings, nor good works, nor spiritual experiences can serve as reliable tests of genuine faith. unit #17
  6. Trials — moments when obedience is costly and God's ways make no sense — are the God-given test that reveals whether faith is genuine or transactional. unit #19
  7. By the time of Isaiah, Israel has become institutionally what Saul was individually — religious in form but empty of genuine devotion, like a haunted house. unit #24
  8. Saul of Tarsus was a New Testament recapitulation of Old Testament Saul, but God's grace transformed him into Paul — offering hope that religious hypocrites can be genuinely saved. unit #25

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

The story of Saul teaches us that genuine faith is revealed not by spiritual experiences or good works, but by how we respond when God's will costs us what we want — a truth that runs from the Old Testament king through the apostle Paul, and into our own trials today.

Monday 1 Peter 1:6-7

Peter writes that trials test the genuineness of our faith the way fire tests gold, proving its worth. Saul's life was marked by spiritual experiences and outward obedience, yet his faith crumbled the moment God required something he did not want to give. Our trials work the same way: they strip away every other reason to follow Jesus and leave only one question — do we love him for himself, or only for what he gives us?

Tuesday Matthew 7:21-23

Jesus warns that many will come to him saying 'Lord, Lord,' having even prophesied and cast out demons in his name, yet he will tell them he never knew them. Saul experienced the Spirit's power — he prophesied among the prophets, he was anointed king, he led Israel to victory — yet none of this saved him. A life full of religious activity and even supernatural signs proves nothing about the state of our hearts before God.

Wednesday Hebrews 6:4-6

The writer of Hebrews describes people who have been enlightened, tasted the heavenly gift, and shared in the Holy Spirit, yet fell away — impossibly difficult to renew to repentance. Saul had all these experiences. He had tasted God's power. He had done good works as judge and general. But his faith was always transactional: he loved God's provision, not God himself. When the provision stopped — when God refused his way — Saul's faith revealed itself as hollow.

Thursday Acts 9

The apostle Paul began as Saul of Tarsus, a man zealous for God's law, doing what he believed was God's work by persecuting the church — just as Old Testament Saul kept the forms of religious obedience while his heart remained transactional. But on the Damascus Road, Christ met him with grace that transformed him completely. If God can turn a religious hypocrite like Saul into a genuine lover of Jesus, there is hope for any of us whose faith is still tangled up with what we want rather than who God is.

Friday 1 Timothy 1:12-17

Paul testifies that he was the foremost of sinners — a religious man who persecuted the church — yet received mercy because his encounter with Christ was real and transforming. Your trials are not punishment but God's invitation to discover whether your faith is genuine. And if you find yourself still clinging to religious forms while your heart pursues other things, Paul's testimony reminds you that grace can break through even the hardest religious exterior and make you new.

What Do We Love God For?

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to think about the difference between loving God himself versus loving what God gives us. The sermon showed how Saul used God to get what he wanted rather than loving God for who he is—and trials are what reveal the difference.
