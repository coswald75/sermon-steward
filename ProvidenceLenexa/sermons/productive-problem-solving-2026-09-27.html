Thesis When God's people solve problems without remembering His faithfulness, fearing Him appropriately, and acting from their identity as His chosen people, they create destructive consequences that can last for generations, but Jesus Christ remains the ultimate solution to all our failed problem-solving.

Theological claims· 13
  1. The first essential question in biblical problem-solving is whether we have correctly identified and understood the actual problem. unit #6
  2. Humility requires asking whether we are the person authorized to solve a problem, resisting both the temptation of ego gratification and the assumption that visibility equals responsibility. unit #7
  3. Many problems do not require immediate action but rather careful monitoring and trust in God's providence to work without our hasty intervention. unit #8
  4. A secure identity in Christ as God's adopted child must govern how we approach and solve problems, distinguishing Christian problem-solving from worldly approaches. unit #9
  5. Biblical problem-solving requires not just intellectual memory of God's past faithfulness but an embodied, emotionally felt confidence that reshapes our current disposition toward the problem. unit #10
  6. Biblical problem-solving requires approaching decisions with appropriate fear of God. unit #11
  7. Leadership failure through personal sin or failure to raise godly children is the dominant recurring problem throughout the Old Testament narrative. unit #14
  8. Without a secure, conscious awareness of our identity in Christ, we will instinctively default to solving problems exactly as the world does, which will fail because God has designed a unique path for His people. unit #19
  9. God is repulsed by ingratitude not merely for attitudinal reasons but because we need active remembrance of His faithfulness to guide us rightly in future decisions, and He tracks our forgetfulness. unit #26
  10. God may discipline disobedient problem-solving by granting exactly what we demand, meaning that getting what we want may be judgment rather than blessing. unit #28
  11. Fear of God paradoxically displaces fear of everything else, so that persistent panic about problems reveals insufficient fear of God rather than excessive fear. unit #31
  12. The foundational question for biblical problem-solving is whether we are consulting and obeying what God has already said in Scripture about our situation. unit #43
  13. Regardless of the quality of our decision-making, the ultimate resolution to all our failures is always Jesus Christ. unit #46

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the anatomy of destructive problem-solving: how Israel forgot God's faithfulness, acted without proper fear, and demanded a solution that became judgment—and how Jesus Christ redeems even our worst decisions.

Monday 1 Samuel 1 (Hannah's barrenness and prayer)

Hannah faced what seemed like an unsolvable problem—barrenness in a culture where it meant shame and exclusion. But instead of demanding a solution from her community or grasping for a worldly fix, she brought her desperation to the Lord and believed He could act. Her confidence was not abstract knowledge that God could help; it was a gut-level certainty that moved her to prayer rather than panic. This is the kind of remembered faithfulness we need when we face our own crises.

Tuesday 1 Samuel 2 (Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas)

Eli's sons were corrupt—they abused their priestly office and despised the Lord—yet Eli failed to restrain them decisively. His passivity as a father created a spiritual crisis that rippled through a generation. When Samuel's own sons later became corrupt judges, the pattern repeated: the problem was not merely the corruption itself, but the leaders who should have prevented it through godly discipline and training. We inherit or create these crises through our choices about who we raise and how we lead.

Wednesday Deuteronomy 17:14-17

Long before Israel demanded a king, God had already given them instructions about how a king should operate if they ever asked for one—he must be from among the Lord's brothers, must copy the law, must not multiply horses or wives or gold. Israel had the answer to their crisis written down, but they never consulted it. Instead, they panicked and demanded a king like the nations, ignoring what God had already provided. Our first move in any crisis is not to innovate a solution, but to ask: what has God already said about this?

Thursday 1 Kings 12 (Rehoboam and the division of the kingdom)

Israel got their king—and for a while, Saul and David were genuine provisions from God. But the pattern of demanding solutions rather than trusting God's timing eventually led to Rehoboam's foolish choice to increase taxes, which split the kingdom in two and created centuries of civil war and exile. What seemed like the solution became the seed of catastrophe. Every generation that forgot why the kingship existed in the first place made worse decisions, until the kingdom itself was destroyed. Getting what we insist on can be mercy's opposite.

Friday Isaiah 9:6-7

The prophet foretells a Child whose government will have no end and whose kingdom will endure forever—the true King who Israel rejected and we all fail to follow. Jesus is not angry at us for our poor problem-solving; He is the answer to every crisis our bad decisions create. He came not to condemn the wise and foolish alike, but to bear the weight of both our sins and our failures, and to establish a kingdom that cannot be shaken. When we feel the weight of our own destructive choices, we turn not to self-recrimination but to Him.
