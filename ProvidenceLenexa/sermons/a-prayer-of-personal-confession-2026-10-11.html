  2. analogy · unit #5 — Applies the hourglass/river pattern to parenting. Parents raise children in a godly environment, but each child must have their own reckoning moment with God, and the fruit of that conversion flows back into the family. The pattern recurs throughout Scripture.

  4. analogy · unit #23 — Applies the principle to parenting and leadership using John Piper's concept of "gravity and gladness." Taking sin seriously in gravity enables one to feel the joy of grace deeply. The pattern holds: the church showed David both his sin and God's salvation.
  5. historical example · unit #26 — Tells John Newton's conversion as the paradigmatic river story. Newton — profane slave ship captain — cried out to God during a storm (narrow moment). He knew to call out because his mother had filled his head with Scripture and hymns (wide river preparation). After conversion, he pastored, wrote Amazing Grace, and counseled Wilberforce (wide river fruit).
  6. historical example · unit #27 — Completes the Newton story with his deathbed testimony — memory gone, but two truths remained: "I am a great sinner and Christ is a great savior." Demonstrates that the church's work endured to the end. The people of God equipped him before conversion and benefited from his ministry after conversion.
Theological claims· 6
  1. The church is the unsung hero of Psalm 51, and everything that happens in David's repentance occurs because God is conducting his redemptive business through his people. unit #2
  2. Every person must do individual business with God to be saved, yet that individual encounter is always connected to God's larger work among his people. unit #3
  3. David's individual repentance was only possible because God worked through the corporate body — sustaining Nathan, sending Nathan, and enabling Nathan to confront David. unit #15
  4. The secular culture, represented by voices like Marlene Winel, views biblical confrontation of sin as spiritual abuse and doctrines of sin and judgment as psychologically traumatic. unit #16
  5. David knew God's forgiving character because he grew up in a covenant community that communicated both law and gospel, and that is the church's charter — to be the community that shows people both their sin and their Savior. unit #22
  6. The secular world misunderstands biblical confrontation because they are spiritually blind to the gospel, and the church's challenge is to be faithful in proclaiming both sin and the greater Savior. unit #24

John Newton was a sailor, a sailor so profane that even other sailors complained about him, and he became the captain of the Atlantic slave trade, the captain of a ship in the Atlantic slave trade. In 1748, his ship nearly went down in the North Atlantic storm, and alone he cried out to God for mercy. You're like, that's the narrow moment. He's at sea. He's all alone. It's just him and God. Doesn't matter what kind of family he was raised in, or any kind of thing in that sense. But how does John know who to call out to? How does John even know that there is a God who would offer salvation? Well, because John's mother filled his head with scriptures and Isaac Watts hymns prior to her death. And so this adult male who was the most profane of all profanes, grabbing hold for his dear life, had something in his head put there by a faithful covenant community that he could call out and say, Lord, save me. And the Lord did save him. And after the storm, the river widened again. And he was ordained and pastored a little parish of Olney for 16 years. And the song Amazing Grace, he wrote it just for his little church to sing. And in London, he counseled a young man named William Wilberforce to stay in parliament and to fight the slave trade and to do so prayerfully.

27 · Completes the Newton story with his deathbed testimony — memory gone, but two truths remained: "I am a great sinner and Christ is a great savior

So the near end of his life, Newton's 82, he really couldn't talk anymore, not very much. And he told a friend, my memory is nearly gone. He was having trouble just understanding where he was like it was. He was losing it. And he told a friend, my memory is nearly gone. But two things I do remember that I am a great sinner and that Christ is a great savior. The people of God had done their job. They had been there in advance well before he was ready to repent and told him that there is a fountain filled with blood. Told him that there is a great God ready to redeem. Told him, John, you're a sinner. And then the people of God were there on the other side to benefit from yet another glorious example of Jesus seeking and saving the lost. So that after that narrow moment on that ship where he did his business with God, he became a servant of God and served his people.

28 · Synthesizes the sermon's argument into a doxological claim about the church's 2,000-year mission

And this is what the church has been doing for 2,000 years. Connecting wide river moments with narrow river moments. Wide river moments with narrow river moments. Over and over again, link by link, God is building his church.

So I'm going to pray for us and then we're going to have I think Dara is our first testimony for baptism today. We're going to have three short testimonies. And you'll hear the river story in their testimonies as well. But let me pray. Father God, we praise your holy name for all you've done for us. Thank you God for blessing us spiritually and physically and taking care of us as a church for so long. Lord, we are grateful that today we get to be reminded that it's our job to talk about sin and our great savior. And Lord, we're really encouraged that we get to hear three examples of three people who have gone through this very thing. Lord, we pray that you bless them. Help them not be too nervous. We love them. Definitely are safe here. We pray you bless them in their testimonies. In Jesus' name, amen. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through the claims that made David's repentance possible: God's sovereignty over the church, the church's dual charter to show sin and Savior, and our calling to carry that witness into a hostile culture.

Monday 2 Samuel 12:1-7

Nathan did not arrive at David's door by accident. God sustained him, sent him, and enabled him to speak truth that David's own conscience could not yet name. We often think repentance happens in isolation—a person alone with God—but Scripture shows us that God uses his church as the instrument of confrontation and restoration. The church exists to be the means by which God brings his people to see themselves as they truly are.

Tuesday Galatians 6:1

Paul's instruction to restore the fallen one carries the same spirit Nathan brought: not judgment from a distance, but gentle correction from one who knows he too is tempted. The word "restore" suggests a craftsman carefully reassembling what has been broken. When we confront sin in our brothers and sisters, we are not performing condemnation—we are participating in the church's charter to restore people to wholeness and to God.

Wednesday Romans 5:20

Grace abounded where sin abounded. David had been taught the law since childhood; he knew his transgression was grave. But he also knew a God whose mercy was greater than his sin because his community had shown him both. The church's essential task is to hold law and gospel together—not to soften either one, but to let each magnify the other. Without the law, grace means nothing. Without the gospel, the law crushes.

Thursday 2 Corinthians 4:4

The god of this age blinds people to the beauty of Christ. When our culture rejects the church's confrontation of sin, calling it abuse and trauma, they are not reasoning clearly—they are seeing through a darkened lens that cannot perceive that truth spoken in love is salvation, not violence. We do not soften our witness to accommodate blindness. We speak truth and trust that the Spirit opens eyes.

Friday Psalm 51:18

David's restored life overflowed into the building up of God's house and God's people. Likewise, our freedom from debt is not an end in itself—it is a means. We are now positioned to be the community that shows people both their sin and their Savior, to confront gently, to welcome home, and to restore. The resources God has entrusted to us are meant to multiply these moments of individual repentance that strengthen the whole body of Christ.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. David opens Psalm 51 by asking God to blot out his transgressions and wash him thoroughly from his iniquity. What do you think David understood about God's character that gave him the courage to ask for something so radical — and where did that understanding come from?
    Psalm 51:1-2
    → How is David's confidence in God's mercy different from someone who simply hopes they might be forgiven?
  2. The sermon emphasizes that Nathan's confrontation was essential to David's repentance — that the church was the unsung hero of this moment. Can you think of a time when someone in your life (or in this church) spoke truth to you about sin when it would have been easier to stay silent?
    2 Samuel 12:1-7; Galatians 6:1
    → What made that confrontation different from mere judgment or condemnation?
  3. David confesses not just his external actions but his internal condition: 'Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me' (Psalm 51:5). What is David claiming about the nature of his sin — is he blaming his mother, or is he making a larger point about human depravity?
    Psalm 51:5
  4. The sermon names a tension: the secular culture views biblical confrontation of sin as 'spiritual abuse,' while the church's calling is to show people both their sin and their Savior. How do we hold both truths — genuine confrontation *and* genuine compassion — without softening either one?
    → What would it look like in our church community to confront sin the way Nathan did — with the goal of restoration, not destruction?
  5. David says to God, 'Create in me a clean heart, and renew a right spirit within me' (Psalm 51:10). This is not David asking for forgiveness alone — he's asking to be remade. What does David's request tell us about what genuine repentance requires?
    Psalm 51:10
  6. The sermon connects David's personal repentance to his future ministry: 'Then I will teach transgressors your ways, and sinners will return to you' (Psalm 51:13). How does David's own encounter with mercy and restoration position him to shepherd others? How does this apply to our own calling as a church?
    Psalm 51:13
    → Where in your own life has God's forgiveness equipped you to help someone else find their way back to him?
