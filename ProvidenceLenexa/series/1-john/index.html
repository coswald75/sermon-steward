Part One

The Letter Itself

1. Why did John write this letter?

John wrote 1 John to solve a problem that has plagued God's people from the beginning: imposter leaders and false teachers circulating among believers, leading many astray. He's not writing a calm theological treatise — he's writing a pastoral field manual for discernment. The need was urgent enough in the first century that all three of John's letters address some form of it: 3 John deals with a man who has hijacked a house church; 2 John warns about teachers who deny that Jesus came in the flesh; 1 John lays out the general pattern. John believed he was living “in the last hour,” and the proliferation of deceivers was the evidence.

Source: 1 John — Introduction (1 John 1:1–4)

2. What is John's central method for sorting true from false Christianity?

He uses what we might call the logic of family resemblance: if God is X, those truly born of God will be X. The whole letter is built around two propositions about God — “God is light” (1:5–3:10) and “God is love” (3:11–5:17) — and John keeps applying the same logic in both halves: if God is light, true believers won't walk in darkness; if God is love, true believers will love one another. This is what we might call ontological ethics — what you do proceeds from what you are. If you are born of God, you will act like God.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

3. So is 1 John a how-to guide for spotting heretics?

Not exactly. John doesn't give a catalog of every possible deception. He does something deeper: he points readers back to a deep, enduring knowledge of Jesus Christ himself. The solution to a discernment crisis is not memorizing every error — it's knowing the real Christ so well that counterfeits stand out. John grounds his authority not in cleverness but in eyewitness testimony: he saw, heard, and touched the incarnate Word. Knowing the true Jesus is what makes discernment possible.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

4. Why does John keep repeating himself?

He's using a Semitic literary style called amplification — circling back to the same ideas from different angles. The repetition isn't sloppy editing. It's purposeful: John is teaching us to differentiate between true and false Christians, and each loop tightens the distinction. If a passage feels like you've already read it, you probably have — and you're meant to.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

Part Two

Knowing the True Jesus

5. Why does John make such a big deal about who Jesus is?

Because anyone who can change your view of Jesus can change your entire life, trajectory, and capacity to discern truth from error. John's argument is that the most dangerous scoffers are not the ones who deny Christianity outright — they're the ones who tamper with the truth about who Jesus is. Most attempts to distort Jesus are motivated by a desire to make him safe around particular sins: pulling the claws and roar out of the mighty Christ, domesticating him for human purposes.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

6. What are some of the predictable ways false teachers reshape Jesus?

A few patterns recur across history: selective quotation that leaves out everything inconvenient; Jesus reduced to a social-justice revolutionary; Jesus as a self-help guru; Jesus as a spiritual mascot for a political tribe; Jesus conveniently silent on whatever sin the teacher does not want to repent of; Jesus severed from the God of the Old Testament. The common thread is always a tamer Jesus — one who blesses what we already wanted to do.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

7. How does this affect how I read Christian books or listen to podcasts?

Be careful. Even popular and well-intentioned authors can selectively portray Jesus in ways that serve their agenda rather than reflecting the biblical Jesus. Only knowledge of the true Christ enables genuine discernment. The remedy is not paranoia, and not a refusal to read widely — it's deeper familiarity with the actual Jesus of Scripture, so that distortion registers when you encounter it.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

8. What does it mean to “not grow bored with what we have had from the beginning”?

This is one of John's most pastoral concerns. Boredom with the basics — with the gospel, with Christ himself — is the gateway through which deceivers enter. People rarely defect from orthodoxy because of an argument; they defect because the foundational truths about Jesus stopped feeling alive. John insists there is always more to learn, love, and live out in response to Jesus than we have yet apprehended. Familiarity is not the same as exhaustion.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

Part Three

God Is Light — Holiness and Sin

9. What does John mean when he says “God is light”?

Light, in John, is shorthand for moral purity and self-disclosing truth — God hides nothing and tolerates no darkness in himself. The pastoral payoff is the family-resemblance argument: if the Father is light, his children will not make a settled practice of walking in darkness. This is not a claim about sinless perfection — John insists we will sin (1 John 2:1). It is a claim about direction. Children of light walk toward the light.

Source: 1 John — Introduction

10. If I'm saved by faith, does my behavior really matter?

Yes — but not in the way you might fear. You are not saved by your behavior; you are saved by Jesus' finished work, received by faith. But your behavior matters as evidence of whether the gospel has actually taken root in you. The apostolic pattern across the New Testament is consistent: present the pure gospel, then call believers to practical holiness, then focus that holiness specifically on personal relationships. The gospel rightly preached will provoke the flesh to think license has been granted, which is exactly why the apostles keep following gospel proclamation with calls to holiness.

Source: 1 John 3:1–18 Revisited

11. What is antinomianism, and why does it keep coming up?

Antinomianism (literally “against the law”) is the move that divorces creed from conduct — claiming that because we are saved by faith alone, our behavior is irrelevant to our salvation. Three forms tend to show up in real life: practical antinomianism (habitual disobedience despite orthodox belief), particular antinomianism (selective tolerance of certain pet sins despite general agreement that holiness matters), and passive antinomianism (obedience to the prohibitions while ignoring the positive commands — don't do that, but never do this for them). John is writing against all three at once.

Source: 1 John 3:1–18 Revisited

12. Aren't holiness and love different things?

No. To be holy is to be loving. Love is the fundamental expression of holiness in Scripture. This is why the three forms of antinomianism all show up as failures to love: divorcing theology from love (practical), loving selectively (particular), and never moving past avoidance into pursuit (passive). All sanctification should be reframed as the pursuit of love, because love is the virtue most antithetical to the flesh and the most diagnostic of genuine conversion.

Source: 1 John 3:1–18 Revisited

Part Four

God Is Love — What Love Actually Is

13. What kind of love is John talking about?

When John writes “see what kind of love the Father has given us” (1 John 3:1), he's pointing at something unique in the universe: God loves believers not with a generic, charitable love, but with the same pure love the Father has for the Son. We are brought into the filial love of Christ. That love requires no forbearance and no overlooking — it is pure because it was earned by Jesus, not by us. It is otherworldly. It should provoke marvel.

Source: See What Kind of Love (1 John 3:1)

14. How do I receive this love if I haven't?

By faith in Jesus Christ — humble acceptance of an undeserved offer. Salvation is earned by the works of Jesus, who suffered the consequences of our sins under the Father's wrath, and it is received by faith alone, not by our own works. If that sounds too simple, that's the point. Anyone can come; no one has to qualify.

Source: See What Kind of Love

15. What does Jesus' kind of love actually look like, day to day?

It is loyal, committed, and long-suffering — the way Christ loves us. It is not primarily a feeling; it is observable and testable. It does not require doctrinal compromise. It is not the same as being universally liked. It does not co-exist with unrepentant sin. John writes against four common myths about love at once: that love can coexist with hidden sin, that love guarantees likability, that love is primarily a feeling, and that loving well requires doctrinal compromise.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

16. Is “love” without theological content still love?

No. Sentimental acceptance without theological content is not love — diluting doctrine actually disfigures the very definition of love. The cultural temptation is to define love as endorsement, but biblical love wills the good for the other, and the good has to be defined by God, not by the person being loved or by the culture around them.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

Part Five

Worldliness

17. Is “don't love the world” a call to withdraw from culture or stop enjoying things?

No. Jesus himself enjoyed the world's legitimate goods. The command against worldliness is not asceticism — it is the discipline of choosing eternal goods over temporal goods when the two compete. Worldliness, in John's sense, is the elevation of temporal preferences to ultimate loyalty. It's the move from “I like this” to “I will not be inconvenienced for the sake of a person.” The world's goods aren't the problem; ranking them above God and people is.

Source: 1 John 2 — Love of This World

18. What does worldliness look like in ordinary life?

It looks like a preference-first, people-last orientation that quietly distorts every relational decision. The worldly Christian is loyal to their preferences and willing to abandon people when the two conflict. Jesus was the opposite — more loyal to the Father and to us than to his own preferences. Worldliness is detected not in what you enjoy but in what you refuse to give up when love requires it.

Source: 1 John 2 — Love of This World

19. I feel spiritually dry. Is that because of unconfessed worldliness?

It might be. One of the more sobering claims in this part of the letter is that spiritual dryness can be a relational consequence — God withholding the felt enjoyment of his presence because selfishness and lovelessness grieve the Holy Spirit. That's not a guaranteed diagnosis (sometimes God's apparent silence has other causes), but it is worth asking honestly: is there a person I am willing to harm to protect a preference? That question, faithfully answered, often locates the dryness.

Source: 1 John 2 — Love of This World

Part Six

Antichrist

20. Who is the antichrist?

Not a single future figure. John uses the language of antichrist to describe a recurring spirit or ethos of deception that has been operating inside the church since the apostolic age. Focusing on identifying a single end-times Antichrist individual is actually pastoral malpractice — the text calls for a general strategy to resist a recurring spirit, not for speculation about a specific person.

Source: Many Antichrists Have Come (1 John 2:18–27)

21. How does this antichrist spirit actually operate?

Parasitically, from within — it lives off Christian presuppositions rather than attacking from outside. After Satan's external opposition through persecution largely failed, this became his more effective strategy: internal corruption rather than external pressure. The pattern is consistent: false teachers position themselves as the necessary addition to Christ.

Source: Many Antichrists Have Come

22. What's the one lie at the heart of every antichrist movement?

“Jesus plus something else is necessary for salvation.” Whether the “plus” is ceremonial law, secret knowledge, sacraments, free will, a particular spiritual experience, a political program, or anything else — the move is identical. It diminishes Christ's sufficiency and adds requirements Scripture does not impose. Whenever you hear that formula, you are listening to a recurring spirit, not a novel insight.

Source: Many Antichrists Have Come

23. How do believers defeat this?

Not by speculation or conspiracy-watching, but by simple, visible loyalty to Christ — especially in seasons of spiritual struggle. The church defeats deception not by seeking novel teachings but by remaining rooted in what it had from the beginning. Refusing anyone who offers you “Jesus plus” is the practical work.

Source: Many Antichrists Have Come

Part Seven

Assurance and the Day of Judgment

24. Will I really stand before God one day?

Yes. The most important eschatological issue is not the debatable timelines of end-times prophecy — it is the certainty that every individual will stand before God and face the exposure of all things. Faithful pastoral ministry has to prepare people for that day, when self-deception and pretense will be removed.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

25. Is believing in Jesus enough to be saved?

Belief is enough for salvation — but not necessarily enough for assurance. Assurance requires observable evidence of transformation. Self-deception is real: people can sincerely believe they are saved when they are not. This is why John spends so much of the letter pointing to observable markers — particularly Christ-like love — as evidence that the new birth has actually happened.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

26. Why is love specifically the marker John keeps returning to?

Because Christ-like love is extremely difficult to counterfeit over time. Doctrinal claims can be parroted; outward morality can be performed; even fervor can be faked. But sustained, sacrificial, long-suffering love — across years and across people who don't repay you — that is hard to fake, which is why John treats it as a reliable indicator of genuine regeneration.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

27. On the day of judgment, will I be evaluated by what I believed or by what I did?

Neither, in isolation. Belief alone won't prepare you; works alone won't either. The question on that day will be what you are — whether you have been born again. Belief and works are both expressions of that prior reality. The right question isn't “have I checked the right boxes?” but “has God made me new?”

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

28. I'm not sure I've been born again. What do I do?

You cannot choose to be born again — but God has promised to answer the desperate, faith-filled prayer of anyone who asks him to make them new. The first practical step is usually to confess your need to other people, breaking the power of the fear of man that keeps so many believers stuck in private. You don't have to perform; you have to ask.

Source: Love, Assurance, and the Coming Exposure

Part Eight

Faith as Victory

29. What does John mean by calling faith our “victory”?

John ends the letter by reframing faith as the weapon of Christian warfare. The Christian life is not a defensive crouch; the incarnation was an invasion, and believers are soldiers in an advancing kingdom. Every conversion, every act of discipleship, every step toward holiness is territory seized from Satan. Counter-attacks aren't surprising — they're predictable responses to our offensive victories.

Source: Faith as Victory (1 John 5)

30. Where does the devil attack most often?

In two predictable places: (1) the object of your faith — distorting who Christ is by presenting false versions of him; and (2) your understanding of faith itself — reducing faith from active, ongoing trust into mere intellectual assent. Either move neutralizes faith without ever having to deny it outright.

Source: Faith as Victory

31. Isn't “faith” just believing certain things are true?

The Western church has largely treated it that way, and John would say that's the problem. In Greek, faith is a verb (active trust exercised) at least as much as a noun (beliefs held). John overwhelmingly uses the present active participle — “the one who is believing” — which is dynamic, ongoing trust. The devil has hijacked the word “belief” to mean creedal assent, which creates the appearance of unity among professing Christians when in reality some have living faith (verb) and others have dead assent (noun).

Source: Faith as Victory

32. So how does the devil attack my view of Jesus today?

Often not through false teaching directly, but through painful circumstances — especially childhood trauma, tragedies, unanswered prayers — designed to make you doubt God's goodness. We need a kind of healthy paranoia: recognizing that strategic attacks on our view of God's character are often hidden inside suffering, not just inside heresy. Identifying where you are being tempted to redraw God's character is part of the work of faith.

Source: Faith as Victory

33. Is the antichrist Jesus of today the same as the ancient heresies?

The packaging is different; the strategy is identical. In contemporary Western culture, the dominant false Christ is a hyper-permissive, passive Jesus who never exercises authority, never says no, never disrupts the customer's preferences. The ancient heresies denied that Christ came in the flesh; the modern one denies that Christ has any binding authority over the flesh. Same satanic playbook, new costume.

Source: Faith as Victory

34. How do I tell whether my faith is verb or noun?

Ask whether your faith is active. Persistent unrepented sin, spiritual passivity, lack of zeal for others — these are signs that faith has slid into mere assent. The remedy isn't anxious self-scrutiny; it's resumed exercise. Faith is sustained by being used. Even the “shield of faith” metaphor in Ephesians 6 implies advance, not retreat.

Source: Faith as Victory

Part Nine

A Few Practical Threads

35. What does this letter say about leadership in the home, the church, or the workplace?

Every Christian leads in some sphere of influence — parents, mentors, employers, neighbors — and 1 John 2:1–5 models how. Christian leadership has three components: affection for those you lead (a portion of your joy legitimately depends on their faithfulness to Christ), a biblical agenda (the “good” you will for them is defined by Scripture, not by your ambition or their felt needs or the culture), and an approach that consistently points them to Jesus Christ as both Lord (the standard to follow) and Savior (the propitiation when they fail). The modern church often emphasizes Christ as Savior while neglecting Christ as the standard of righteousness. John holds both together.

Source: John as an Example Leader (1 John 2:1–5)

36. What's one sentence I should walk away with from this series?

The aim of 1 John is to leave you knowing the real Jesus so deeply that counterfeits stand out, loving like him so consistently that your love can't be counterfeited, and trusting him so actively that the devil's two strategies — false Christs and dead faith — find no purchase in you.

Source: Synthesis across the series
