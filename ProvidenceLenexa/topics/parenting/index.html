The Long Game: What Parenting Actually Requires

You already know this is hard. Here's an honest account of what's at stake — and what you actually have to work with.

Name What You're Actually Carrying

If you found this page, you're probably not looking for another listicle. You're worried — about your kids' faith, about the pull of their phones, about whether what you're doing is actually working. And the weight of that is real. Parents have what one pastor describes as 'a ton of immediate demands in raising children.' [2] That's not an exaggeration for effect. That is your Tuesday morning, your Sunday afternoon, your 10 p.m. when you're not sure the conversation went the way it needed to go. The demands are immediate. The results are slow. And the gap between those two things is where most parental anxiety lives.

Here is something worth naming before anything else: you cannot will your children into faith. 'We cannot teach our children the scriptures, but we cannot will them into conversion.' [10] That is hard to hear, and it is also, for most parents, a relief — because it means the pressure you feel to manufacture an outcome that only God can produce is a pressure you were never meant to carry alone. What you can do matters. But what you can do is not everything.

The Actual Goal

So what are you actually aiming at? There is a verse in 2 Timothy 3:14 that, if you sit with it, gives you the clearest possible target: 'continue in what you have learned and firmly believed.' The aim is to be able to say that — with sincerity and conviction — to the young man or young woman you've raised. Not 'I hope you remember some of this.' Not 'good luck out there.' But: *continue in what you have learned and firmly believed.* [10]

Notice the word 'firmly.' The goal isn't just that your children learn the content of your faith. It's that they themselves move from knowing it to trusting it — that it becomes theirs. 'We aren't content as parents with merely communicating our faith. We want to transmit our faith.' [10] That distinction is everything. Communication can be one-directional. Transmission requires something living to pass through — a faith that is genuinely, visibly real in you before it ever gets to them.

What Your Children Are Watching

The uncomfortable part of parenting is that your children are not primarily listening to your instructions — they are reading your life. 'Fathers and mothers, your children are listening to you, watching you, learning from you. What are you teaching them? Intentionally and not so intentionally.' [6] That question — *not so intentionally* — is the one most parents would rather not sit with. But it is the honest one.

One concrete example worth putting on the table: it is possible to want well-behaved, successful children for reasons that have more to do with how you look than with who they become. 'I want to look good by having successful, well-behaved children. Well, what am I worshiping there? Am I worshiping the Lord God? No. My worship of self is driving how I'm interacting with my kids.' [5] That's not a condemnation — it's a category. A useful one. Because once you name it, you can actually do something about it. The question every parent has to keep asking is whether what drives daily parenting decisions is God's glory or self-protection.

The flip side is also true: a faith that is genuinely grateful and joyful is contagious. 'A grateful, joyful heart is the key to a sincere faith. It's the expression of a sincere faith.' [9] Children know the difference between a parent who believes God has been good to them and a parent who is going through religious motions. One of those is transferable.

You Were Not Designed to Do This Alone

Here is something most parenting resources skip over: 'the day will come when you need a whole community of faith to help stand guard over your child's life and doctrine.' [2] Not might come. Will come. Your influence over your children is enormous, and it is also finite — and the window of it being the dominant influence in their lives is shorter than it feels when they are small.

The practical implication is worth being direct about: 'pick a church, stick with it, if possible put down roots, plant relational seeds, become known, and get to know others. So that when your children get older, they will be surrounded by people who know them, who love them, and who can do their little part in helping launch your child into the next chapter of their Christian journey.' [9] The friendships built now become something more than friendships later. 'The friendships you build in the church today will become gatekeepers for your child's heart tomorrow.' [2] Children need to know, concretely, what virtue actually looks like in a person's life — and parents can lean on that. You can ask your kids who they know that's generous, who they know that's faithful, and let them start naming names of real people who have been present in their world. [8]

This also matters for your children's understanding of who is in authority over them. They need to see both parents operating with unified weight. 'Children, obey your parents' — plural. [7] The household isn't a solo act, and the people surrounding that household aren't optional accessories. They are part of the structure.

The Subversive Act Nobody Talks About

'Making and raising children into fear and admonition of the Lord is one of the most subversive things you can do in this current day.' [4] That framing is worth sitting with — not because parenting is a culture war, but because it clarifies what is actually at stake. You are not simply managing small people toward functional adulthood. You are raising image-bearers who will either know and love God or not, and that matters beyond your family and beyond your lifetime.

Part of that means bringing your children into the real weight of your family's decisions, not just handing them outcomes. One account worth noting: when a family was praying about a difficult relocation, they 'took the kids aside and brought them into that process, let them know... we felt God might be leading us... we brought them into that. We prayed with them. We talked with them. We let them share their concerns, their fears. And they could see that it wasn't just God moving mom and dad, it was God moving an entire family.' [13] Children who see faith operating under actual pressure know it's real in a way they never could from watching it operate only when nothing costs anything.

And regardless of the educational path chosen — homeschool, Christian school, public school — 'all of those families have the responsibility to steward their children well, to teach their children about God's truth, and to live for Him and to be an example and to educate their children in the ways of righteousness.' [12] The method is a real decision. The responsibility doesn't vary by method.

The Vow Underneath Everything

If there is a stake in the ground underneath all of this, it is this: your children are not ultimately yours. At a child dedication, parents are asked: 'Do you now dedicate your child to the Lord who gave them to you, surrendering all worldly claims upon their lives in the hope that they will belong wholly to Him?' [14] That is not a ceremony. That is a reckoning. 'It's a recognition that not even our own lives belong to Jesus, but even those of our children.' [14]

And the God to whom you are surrendering them is not indifferent. 'God does not merely tend to us and care for us like a shepherd does his sheep. He tends to us and cares for us and makes sure that we have everything we need from a fatherly affection and from a fatherly inclination to bless his children.' [11] The long game is hard. But you are not playing it without a Father who is playing it with you.

Start with the honest question from source [5]: what am I actually worshiping in how I interact with my kids today? That question, taken seriously, will surface more than most parenting books will. Then take seriously the long game — not just the immediate demands, but the allies, the community, the roots that will matter when you no longer have daily access to your child's heart. [9] You were not made to do this alone, and the God who gave you these children is not watching from a distance.
