The Keller Signature

Soteriology leads — but not by the typical margin. The near-equal weight of Christology and Hamartiology reflects Keller's approach: sin is always diagnosed before grace is applied, and grace is always anchored in the person of Christ. Anthropology ranking #5 (unusual) reveals his consistent diagnosis of secular identity formation — he's as interested in how people construct selfhood as in how they're redeemed.

Structural Note

Theological Claims lead exposition units 240 to 210 — reversed from most expository preachers, where textual exposition precedes doctrinal assertion. The Dual Tone

Every single sermon registers both Pastoral and Didactic simultaneously — not a single outlier. This is Keller's hallmark: he never teaches without pastoring, never pastors without teaching. The Manhattan congregation demanded intellectual rigor; the gospel demanded pastoral warmth. He never chose between them.

Illustration Profile

Cultural reference leads at 28% — the inverse of most evangelical preachers, where personal story dominates. Keller's Manhattan context produced a distinct hermeneutic: the secular world's films, novels, and philosophers become unwitting witnesses to the gospel's truth. Personal stories (20%) are notably low for a major preacher, consistent with his stated philosophy that the sermon's authority should not rest on the preacher's biography.

Personal Story
A college freshman who had given up her faith "around Thanksgiving" — she cited intellectual objections, but a minister's questions revealed she had moved in with a boyfriend that same semester. Keller's point: irreligion and skepticism can themselves be fig leaves, covering not philosophical conviction but the desire to escape the gaze of God. Her arguments were real, but they were also serving a function.
Nakedness & the Holiness of God · Genesis 3:7–13
Cultural Reference
Draws on Freud's Civilization and its Discontents — the original German title uses "Unbehagen" (deep unease, malaise) rather than mere "discontent." Freud argued that civilization's price is pervasive guilt: the ego must repress its egocentric desires, producing a life-pervading anxiety. "Freud believed our lives were pervaded with guilt… we feel uneasy about ourselves, we feel uneasy about life." Used to ground the gospel's address to universal condemnation.
Reconciled and at Peace · Hebrews 9:11–14
Hypothetical
Develops the scenario of a judge who has one son — a murderer and fugitive who shows up at her door asking to be hidden. On one hand: a mother's love is strongest when a child is in trouble. On the other: "I'm a judge. I cannot take you… I stand for the law. I uphold the law." The hypothetical holds subjective revulsion and legal barrier in tension simultaneously — the exact double problem the atonement resolves.
Reconciled and at Peace · Hebrews 9:11–14
Personal Story
A physically beautiful woman in Keller's Virginia congregation had been taught from childhood that her looks were her "calling card" — and could not shake the internal voice: "you better go with him honey because if you don't have a man who loves you you're nothing." A counselor told her to build a career instead; Keller pointed to Colossians 3:3 — "your life is hid with God in Christ." Two months later, transformed: "You're not my life. Christ is my life."
Nakedness & the Holiness of God · Genesis 3:7–13
Cultural Reference
Jean-Paul Sartre's concept of "The Look" from Being and Nothingness: to be seen by another is to be turned into an object, diminished, dehumanized. Sartre applied this to God — "because God can see me and I can't see him, I am dehumanized." Keller uses Sartre as the secular world's own witness to the terror of God's holiness when sin has not been dealt with, then pivots: in Christ, God's gaze becomes acceptance, not exposure.
Nakedness & the Holiness of God · Genesis 3:7–13
Historical Example
Early Christians in the Antonine Plague: "Most Christians showed unbounded love and loyalty, never sparing themselves and only thinking of others. Heedless of danger, they took charge of the sick, attended to their every need… many departed their life serenely happy." Contrasted with contemporaries who abandoned the dying and threw the half-dead into the streets. Used to demonstrate how eschatological hope produces this-worldly fearlessness.
Healing the Sick · Matthew 8:5–17
Hypothetical
The divorce law thought experiment: Richard Rorty says keep religion out of public discourse — but divorce law depends on the purpose of marriage, which depends on views of human flourishing, which are rooted in deep metaphysical commitments. "If you say, keep your religion out of the public realm, what you really are meaning is, my Enlightenment Western individualistic faith assumptions about human nature are privileged over yours." The secular is never value-neutral.
Exclusivity: How Can There Be Just One True Religion? · 1 John 4:1–10
Analogy
Uses addiction to map the dynamics of sin: disintegration (you need more for less satisfaction), isolation (you have to lie, defend, blame others), and denial (increasingly out of touch with reality). "Everybody in this room says, I know. First hand or second hand, at the most third hand, the devastation of those poor people who get addicted to substances." The punchline: this is every human heart's relationship to any idol — not just substances.
Hell: Isn't the God of Christianity an Angry Judge? · Luke 16:19–31
Personal Story
Peter Kreeft's neighbor — a woman who had resolved her fear of death through secular "death is natural" philosophy — tells her seven-year-old son whose cousin has just died: "Your cousin has gone back to the earth… when you see the earth putting forth new flowers next spring, you can know that your cousin's life is fertilizing those flowers." The boy screamed, "I don't want him to be a fertilizer!" and ran out. The natural human intuition: death is not natural. It's an intrusion.
Hope in the Face of Death · 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18
Analogy
Every person who sits on their porch after a relational rupture and says "they started it — I shouldn't have to go to them first" is mirrored by the other party on their porch saying the exact same thing. "The whole world is sinking into hell because everybody's sitting on their porch waiting for the other person to make the first move." The prodigal's father does the opposite — he runs. He doesn't wait. Used to illuminate the scandal of divine initiative.
And Kissed Him · Luke 15:11–24
Cultural Reference
Aldous Huxley from Ends and Means: "I realize I didn't want there to be a God. I wanted atheism to be true. I wanted life to be meaningless because then I could do whatever I wanted." Keller deploys this as the secular world's most honest witness — Huxley's confession that atheism often functions not as the conclusion of dispassionate inquiry but as moral liberation. The intellectual objection and the moral desire are not always separable.
Out of Egypt · Matthew 2:13–23
Historical Example
Albert Camus on the cross: "Christ, the God-man, suffers too. Evil and death can no longer be entirely imputed to him since he suffers and dies. The night on Golgotha is so important because the divinity ostensibly abandoned its traditional privilege and lived through to the end, despair included, the agony of death." Camus — atheist, existentialist — identifies the crucifixion as the moment that changes the problem of suffering philosophically. Used to show the cross is where atheism's best objection runs aground.

Intellectual Lineage Note

C.S. Lewis appears 23 times — more than all other external sources combined. This is not incidental. Keller uses Lewis as a culturally legible bridge: a literary giant, a former atheist, a philosopher comfortable in secular idiom who nonetheless reached orthodox Christianity. The second most cited figure is Augustine, whose analysis of disordered love in Confessions undergirds Keller's entire doctrine of idolatry. Unusually, Sartre, Camus, Huxley, and Freud appear as witnesses — not enemies. Keller's practice: let the secular world's most honest thinkers testify against their own system.

What This Reveals

Fulfillment dominates (33%) — Keller's reflexive move is to show how a text, figure, or theme finds its completion in Christ. This is not a Christo-centric add-on; it is the structural argument. Every passage is preached as pointing to something it cannot itself supply.

Typology ranks second (26%) — Keller reads Old Testament persons and institutions as patterns that anticipate Christ's person and work. The Ethiopian eunuch, the Passover lamb, the temple priesthood: all rehearsals for what was to come.

Contrast appears third (12%): a move where the text's situation is inverted by the gospel. The Prodigal's shame becomes the father's running embrace. The slave becomes a son. The condemned becomes the justified. This is the "gospel reversal" move — the last thing you expect the text to do.

The Gospel Third

Keller's distinctive: every sermon concludes with explicit gospel application regardless of the text. A sermon on work ends in Christ's labor. A sermon on idolatry ends at the cross. The BT moves are the mechanism — fulfillment and typology are the structural devices that make the pivot unavoidable.
