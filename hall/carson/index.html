He is the premier diagnostician of contemporary evangelical drift in the Hall, combining NT scholarship with cultural critique.

Historical Example
So biblical theology is interested not only in the particular formulations and biblical contributions of particular books, so people can speak of a Johannine theology. That is the kind of emphasis that John's writings make, but also how they fit into the sequence of God's ongoing revelation until the whole canon is complete with a high mark in Jesus Christ himself, the word made flesh. Now, to do that, in other words, you must not only know a lot of text, you must know something of their historical sequence. And Calvin managed in the commentaries not only to be a good dealer in text. a faithful interpreter of text, but to preserve the historical sequencing of things that we call biblical theology today and would not be pushed into reading the Bible anachronistically in order to buttress his systematic theology. Now, I've given you an overview of what I want to say. Now, let me unpack it just a wee bit. You see, it's possible to make some huge mistakes this way. What is the relationship between Calvin's commentaries and his institutes? One person writes. The institutes contain all of Calvin's theology, whereas his commentaries contain the application of such theology. Huge mistake. Because what that does is elevate the institutes to the primary place and the commentaries become the application, whereas in Calvin's own view, the commentator is first in taking that which is God revealed. The institutes are not God revealed. The scripture is God revealed and the commentator is commentating on scripture. Now, insofar as the commentator is right, he is re revealing all that God has said in the first instance. But necessarily, the institutes are a second order thing because they are bringing things together from from many, many other parts of the Bible, rather than the text that is being commented on at a particular point. This is one of the arguments, as Joel Beakey said last night, that Calvin had with Butzer. Butcher wrote these long, long, long commentaries, all right, but not only were they long and verbose and difficult, he really followed an Aristotelian model of how you go about creating arguments. What he looked for in each text that he was expounding was what he called the locus, the chief point. And these loci, these things he then expounded at length so that he began with biblical text and then went at great length to unpack that point. And thus, in the unpacking of that point, which he found to be so central, pretty soon he was bringing on all of the systematic theology. So in Butzer, as you have alleged commentary on text, what you actually have is systematic theology imported into the text, at least those parts of the text that are in Butzer's view, the locus, the most important point. Whereas what Calvin does is say it's wrong on two points. It's wrong, first, because Bucer does not actually comment on everything in the text. He only comments on the low side. He only comments on the points that he thinks are are the most important. And as a result, there are many, many things in the text that Bucer doesn't even look at. He just comments on the things that he thinks are the most important. Whereas a commentator has got to comment on everything. So in one sense, although, although in his commentaries, Calvin is much shorter than then. He is also more comprehensive. He doesn't want to let anything slip by so that you read Calvin's commentary. And he's briefly reflecting on introductory comments and the language of quotation and historical details. And so he's pretty comprehensive in his view, because all of it is God's word. You cannot afford simply to leap from text immediately to systematic theology. You have to see the flow of the whole thing, do you see? And then, secondly, he doesn't like what Butzer is doing, not only because it is so long and complicated that the ordinary people can't understand it. He doesn't like what Butzer is doing also because he is becoming anachronistic. That is, he's developing the entire systematic theology and putting it back in the text and losing the historical specificity of what is actually in the text.

Personal Story
For a while, my sister and her husband served as missionaries in Papua New Guinea in a tribe that was pre-Stone Age in its technology. That is to say, they did not even use stone on their arrowheads. They used a hardwood like ebony on bamboo shafts, ebony or teak. So, from a technological point of view, this was a very primitive people. Suppose, for argument's sake, one of those people came out, one of those tribe's people came to your hometown, and you buckle down with that tribal person and learn the language. You spent five years at it, and you learned to speak the language fluently. And now let us suppose that you have the assignment to go back into that tribal area, and without using any visual aids, explain to the tribals exactly what electricity is, using only their language and no visual aids. How will you proceed? Well, you will begin in their language by saying something like this, I am here to tell you about... well, you don't have a word for it, we'll make one up, in your language we shall call it electricity. Electricity is like a powerful spirit that runs along hard things like vines. Only these vines are not something that grow, they're something that we make. And we string them from tree to tree. Actually, we cut down the trees and take off all the branches and plant them in the ground again, but then we string these vine-like things, and we pump in a whole lot of this powerful spirit at one end, and then this vine comes into our mud huts. And it goes into square things that we also make with round bits on top. And this spirit-like thing, electricity, goes around so fast, it makes something like a fire, so that you can boil water without actually having any smoke in your mud hut. And then it goes into other round things near the top of your thatch roof that makes many suns. so that you can stay up late at night, if you wanted to. Now how am I doing in my explanation of electricity? You see, so far I have not mentioned the molecular nature of matter. I have certainly not introduced anything about atomic weights. I haven't introduced alternating and direct current. I haven't introduced microchips. I haven't introduced storage facilities, generating capacity. I haven't introduced measures, ohms, watts, volts and so forth. Now what's the trouble? Are these people stupid? No, of course not. If they emigrate to Canada, their children will probably beat ours. They're not stupid. The children of immigrants often beat the Westerners for a while. It takes us two or three generations to slow them down. Immigrants often try harder. So what's the problem then? The problem is their complete want of experience. They have no categories to understand these things.

Hypothetical
If you read in this area, read, for example, Calvin's Institutes, who thought about these things a great deal, his section on accommodation. He speaks of God lisping in our language. Let me put it this way. Imagine an eternity past. How did the Father and the Son communicate? Did they speak in Hebrew? Did they speak in any sense the way we speak? See, all of our notions of speaking in any language entail passage of time and sequence. What does God look like as the Father communicates with the Son in eternity past? And don't give me the answer that a lot of Christians give at this juncture and say, well, to God, everything is eternally present. How do you know that? Present is a temporal category. I don't know that everything is eternally present to God. I don't know what it looks like to God. I think it's far better to admit what we don't know. But we don't understand what he's talking about. And he must do so in a language that we understand. All of that is accommodation. Not because God could not communicate intratrinitarianly in eternity past without all of our limitations. But because God is now, to use Calvin's language, lisping our language.
