What the sermon argues

The shape of the message

This sermon examines Romans 3:21-26, which Luther called the central point of the Bible, exploring how God's righteousness is revealed in the gospel apart from the law covenant. Carson demonstrates that justification by grace through faith addresses the universal problem of human sin—equally condemning Jews and Gentiles—and establishes that God's righteousness is available to all without racial distinction but only through faith in Christ. The exposition shows how Christ's sacrifice of atonement simultaneously demonstrates God's justice in dealing with sin and His love in justifying the ungodly, resolving the dilemma of how a holy God can accept sinful image-bearers. The cross reveals God to be both just and the justifier, canceling sin while turning aside divine wrath, thereby vindicating His righteousness in forgiving sins committed under the old covenant and securing the justification of all who trust in Christ.

Small-group leader brief

Questions for midweek

  1. Romans 3:21-26 has been called the theological center of Scripture. As you listen to this passage read aloud, what strikes you about how many times the word 'righteousness' (or related forms) appears in these six verses, and what does this repetition suggest Paul wants us to understand? Romans 3:21-26
    When Paul says God's righteousness is revealed 'apart from the law,' what do you think he means—that the law is irrelevant, or something else?
  2. The sermon emphasizes that Romans 3:21-26 makes no sense without understanding Romans 1:18–3:20, where Paul establishes the universal problem of sin. Why would Paul need to establish that all people—Jew and Gentile alike—stand condemned before he announces the solution of justification by grace? Romans 1:18–3:20
  3. According to the sermon, at the cross God's wrath against sin and God's love for sinners converge simultaneously rather than one replacing the other. How does this change the way you understand what happened when Christ died, compared to thinking of His death as simply demonstrating God's love or simply satisfying God's justice?
    Can you think of a time when you've sensed both God's sorrow over sin and His love for you at the same moment?
  4. The sermon discusses the Day of Atonement imagery (Leviticus 16), where blood was applied to the mercy seat to both turn aside God's wrath and cleanse from sin. What does this Old Testament pattern suggest about whether Christ's sacrifice should be understood as only canceling our guilt, or as doing something more? Leviticus 16
  5. Romans 3:22-23 establishes that God's righteousness is available 'to all who believe' without any racial distinction, yet the sermon notes this creates a fundamental challenge to contemporary culture. What is it about the exclusivity of faith in Christ (that this is the only way to be made right with God) that provokes resistance, and how does understanding the universal guilt of sin shape how we present this claim? John 14:6; Acts 4:12
    How might our witness be different if we first helped people see their need to be right with God before offering Christ as the answer?
  6. The sermon shows that justification—God's declaration that we are righteous—happens now through faith, even though the final judgment hasn't yet occurred. What confidence does this give you about your standing before God today, and how should this shape the way you live this week? 1 John 5:13
    If assurance of present justification is inseparable from this doctrine, what might be hindering your own sense of assurance, and how does the gospel address that?
Daily readings

Five-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc of God's righteousness revealed in the gospel: from the universal human problem of sin, through the exclusivity of Christ's sacrifice, to the simultaneous display of God's wrath and love at the cross, and finally to the assurance that flows from justification by grace alone.

Monday
Theological solutions are unintelligible apart from the problems they solve; sin equally condemns Jews and Gentiles.Romans 1:18–3:20
Before we can grasp the marvel of God's righteousness revealed in the gospel, we must understand the universal condemnation that precedes it. This passage establishes that all of us—regardless of background or moral achievement—stand under God's wrath because we have suppressed the truth of His authority and asserted our own autonomy. The problem is not circumstantial but constitutional: we are sinners by nature and choice, which is why grace alone can justify us.
Tuesday
Christ's sacrifice accomplishes both expiation (canceling sin) and propitiation (turning aside wrath), typified in the Day of Atonement.Leviticus 16
The Day of Atonement reveals that God's approach to sin never separates justice from mercy—blood must be shed to both cleanse from sin and turn aside divine wrath. When we understand Christ as our great High Priest whose sacrifice fulfills this pattern, we see that His death is not merely therapeutic removal of guilt but the satisfaction of God's righteous demand against sin. In the cross, God's wrath does not evaporate; it falls fully on Christ, the Substitute.
Wednesday
The Old Testament abundantly testifies to God's grace and foreshadows the insufficiency of animal sacrifice apart from Christ's perfect offering.Hebrews 10:4
Animal sacrifices under the old covenant could never permanently remove sin—they pointed always forward to a better sacrifice, Christ Himself. This reality does not diminish God's grace in the old covenant but reveals His long-suffering patience and His sovereign purpose to consummate redemption through His Son. We are the beneficiaries of that fulfilled hope, recipients of a righteousness that no ritual could ever produce, offered to us freely by grace.
Thursday
The exclusive claims of Christ—that He alone provides access to God—are essential to the gospel's substance and cannot be removed without destroying it.John 14:6; Acts 4:12
Contemporary culture recoils from exclusivity, yet the gospel cannot be diluted into pluralism without ceasing to be good news at all. Christ is not one option among many; He is the unique God-man whose sacrifice alone bears the weight of human sin and satisfies divine justice. This exclusivity is not an unfortunate imposition but the foundation of the gospel's power and certainty—if salvation could be achieved through human effort or multiple paths, grace would vanish and we would be left to our own hopeless devices.
Friday
Present justification by grace alone logically entails present assurance that we are accepted before God, a doctrine that flows from justification as inaugurated eschatology.1 John 5:13
Because God has already pronounced us just through faith in Christ, we may know now—not merely hope—that we are His children and that our sins are forgiven. This assurance is not presumption but the rational fruit of justification: the verdict that belongs to the final judgment is declared over us today through grace. As we embrace this truth together, we are liberated from the anxiety that plagues every human heart—the fear of rejection before God—and compelled to gratitude that moves us to worship and witness.
Weekly prayer

Grace Alone: Prayer for Justification

Father, we come before You in awe of Your righteousness revealed in the gospel. You are just and holy, abhorring sin, yet in love You have provided what we could never achieve—justification through grace alone. We worship You for the way the cross displays simultaneously Your wrath against our rebellion and Your immeasurable love toward the ungodly (Romans 3:21-26).

We confess that we often suppose we might earn or merit Your favor through our own righteousness. We are prone to hide behind the idols of our culture—authenticity, self-determination, personal fulfillment—that suppress the truth of the creator-creature distinction and our desperate need for Your acceptance. We fail to grasp the gravity of our sin or the urgency of the question that runs through all Scripture: How shall we be right with God? Our hearts are too easily satisfied with therapeutic answers about happiness rather than the fundamental reality of justification before the holy God (Romans 1:18–3:20).

Yet in the gospel we have been declared just. Christ, our substitute, bore the full weight of Your righteous wrath and purchased our redemption through His blood (Romans 3:24-25a). You have pronounced over us now the verdict that belongs to the final judgment, graciously providing what we cannot earn. This justification by faith alone—not by race, not by works, not by any human distinction—is available to all who believe and trust in Christ's finished work (Romans 3:22-23).

Grant us grace this week to live in the power of this truth. Awaken us to the scandal and beauty of the cross, where Your justice and mercy converge. Give us boldness to raise the question of acceptance before God with those around us, knowing that only the gospel's answer—grace alone through faith alone in Christ alone—addresses the deepest need of every human heart. Compel us by this grace to rejoice in our justification and to offer it freely to others without racial distinction, just as You have offered it to us.

We commit ourselves to You as those who are declared just, made alive by the Spirit, and eternally secure in Christ. To You alone—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—be glory and honor forever.

Memorize

Romans 3:21-22

“But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from the law, although the Law and the Prophets bear witness to it— the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe.”
This verse encapsulates the sermon's central thesis: God's righteousness is revealed through the cross and received by faith alone, universally available to all believers regardless of background. It distills the essential claim that justification comes through grace, not works, and binds together the themes of God's righteousness, the gospel apart from the law, and faith as the necessary condition for all who believe.
Family table

The Cross Where Love and Justice Meet

One question for the table: Today we heard that the cross shows us God being both angry at sin and loving toward sinners at the exact same moment. If you had to explain to a friend why Jesus had to die instead of God just forgiving everyone without it, what would you say? What does the cross show us about how serious God takes sin, and how much He loves us?

works for ages 8+ — younger children can listen and offer simple answers; older kids and teens will naturally engage the theological depth

For parents: In the sermon, Carson highlighted how the cross is the stunning point where God's holy anger at sin and His loving grace converge—not as opposites, but as perfectly unified. This prompt invites your family to grapple with how Jesus' death accomplishes something both sides of that tension require.

Couples

Righteousness and Grace in Our Marriage

  1. What aspect of God's righteousness displayed at the cross—His justice, His love, or both together—most stirred your heart this morning, and why?
  2. How does the gospel's message that we are justified by grace alone through faith alone challenge any ways we've been earning acceptance from each other or keeping score in our marriage?
  3. What is one specific way you want to grow in receiving and extending the same grace toward your spouse that Christ has extended to you, and how can we pray for that together?
