0 · Opens the sermon by naming the cultural confusion around dual citizenship (earthly and heavenly) and previews the sermon's purpose: to rescue citizenship from evangelical confusion

We live in confusing times when it comes to citizenship. As Christians in America, we're constantly being pulled in different directions about what it means to be both citizens of this country and citizens of heaven. This morning, I want to talk about rescuing citizenship—rescuing it from the confusion that has plagued American evangelicalism for too long.

1 · Direct pastoral address preparing the congregation for potentially challenging content, preemptively addressing defensiveness while clarifying the sermon's non-antagonistic intent

Before we dive into our text, I need to say something clearly: this sermon will challenge some deeply held beliefs about America, patriotism, and Christian identity. If you find yourself getting defensive, I ask you to pause and listen. My goal is not to attack your love of country, but to help us all see what Scripture actually says about where our ultimate allegiance belongs.

2 · Reads and introduces the primary text, highlighting its radical nature and scope of application to citizenship, politics, and national identity

Turn with me to Philippians 3:20-21. Paul writes: 'But our citizenship is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power that enables him even to subject all things to himself.' This is our text for this morning, and these two verses contain a radical claim that should reorient everything about how we think about citizenship, politics, and national identity.

3 · Unpacks the Greek word politeuma and its original Roman colonial context to illuminate Paul's argument about the believer's citizenship being relocated to heaven

The word Paul uses here for 'citizenship' is the Greek word politeuma. In the Roman world, this was a technical term with precise meaning. Philippi was a Roman colony, which meant its citizens held Roman citizenship even though they lived hundreds of miles from Rome. They were expected to live according to Roman law, participate in Roman civic life, and maintain loyalty to Caesar—even while living in a distant outpost of the empire. Paul takes this familiar concept and turns it on its head. He says our politeuma—our citizenship, our colony, our true home—is in heaven.

4 · Derives the doctrinal implication from the exposition: believers' primary identity is heavenly citizenship, which relativizes all earthly national identities and establishes the believer as an exile

Here's what this means: your primary identity is not American, or Mexican, or Korean, or whatever passport you hold. Your primary identity is as a citizen of heaven. This is not your home. You are an exile, an alien, a sojourner passing through. And this changes everything about how you relate to earthly citizenship.

5 · Brings in 1 Peter 2:11 to reinforce the exile identity, unpacking the Greek term paroikos to describe the believer's posture of engaged-but-distanced participation in culture

Peter picks up this same theme in 1 Peter 2:11 when he addresses believers as 'sojourners and exiles.' The word Peter uses for 'exile' is paroikos—someone who lives alongside, but not fully integrated into, the dominant culture. You're here, but you don't fully belong. You participate, but you maintain a critical distance. You're engaged, but not absorbed.

6 · Uses the contemporary analogy of international students to make the exile posture concrete and accessible, showing what engaged-but-not-absorbed participation looks like

Think about how international students live in this country. They're here, they go to school, they work, they pay taxes, they might even love many things about America. But they never forget they're not citizens. They hold a different passport. When graduation comes, they go home. They live with one foot here and one foot there. That's the posture of the Christian—fully present in this world, but never forgetting that our true home is elsewhere.

7 · Introduces Jeremiah 29:7 to establish the biblical model of engaged exile—seeking the earthly city's welfare without confusion about ultimate allegiance—as the redemptive-historical pattern for the church

Now, here's where this gets really important. Exile status doesn't mean withdrawal. In Jeremiah 29:7, God commands the Jewish exiles in Babylon to 'seek the welfare of the city where I have sent you into exile, and pray to the Lord on its behalf, for in its welfare you will find your welfare.' Notice: they are to seek the city's good even though it's not their home. They are to pray for Babylon even though Babylon destroyed Jerusalem. They are to work for flourishing even in exile. This is the Christian posture: engaged for the good of the earthly city, but never confused about which city is ultimate.

8 · Examines the verb tense in Philippians 3:20 to establish the present reality of heavenly citizenship, arguing that the identity is already operative rather than merely future or potential

Look again at verse 20: 'Our citizenship is in heaven.' The verb tense here is present, not future. Paul is not saying 'Our citizenship will be in heaven' or 'Our citizenship used to be in heaven.' He says it is. Right now. Already. Not just when we die. Not just when Jesus returns. Now. Your citizenship has already been transferred. You already hold a heavenly passport. The question is whether you're living like it.

9 · Applies the exposition through diagnostic questions that expose functional allegiance, challenging the congregation to examine whether their emotional and rhetorical loyalty patterns align with heavenly or earthly citizenship

So let me ask you directly: Are you living like your citizenship is in heaven? Or have you functionally given your ultimate allegiance to an earthly nation, an earthly political party, an earthly cultural identity? When political debates arise, do you find yourself more passionate about defending America than defending the gospel? When someone criticizes this country, do you feel more offended than when someone criticizes Jesus? These are diagnostic questions that reveal where your true citizenship lies.

10 · Recapitulates the sermon's purpose (rescuing citizenship from confusion), restates the core identity claim (heavenly citizenship is primary), and issues a final charge to live as engaged exiles before transitioning to prayer

Church, we have to rescue citizenship from the confusion that has captured American evangelicalism. We must remember that our first identity, our controlling identity, our ultimate identity is not American—it is Christian. We are citizens of heaven who happen to reside in America for a season. Let us live like the exiles we are: fully engaged in seeking the good of this earthly city, but never confused about which city is our true home. Let us pray.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we meditate on what it means to hold heavenly citizenship as our primary identity while faithfully stewarding earthly citizenship—a posture of exile that transforms our engagement with culture, politics, and community.

Monday 1 Peter 2:11

Peter calls us "sojourners and exiles"—not because we've withdrawn from our cities, but because our ultimate allegiance belongs to a kingdom not of this world. This posture frees us from the false god of national identity that demands our ultimate loyalty. When we accept that we are first and foremost citizens of heaven, earthly citizenship takes its proper place: important, but not ultimate.

Tuesday Jeremiah 29:7

God commanded the exiles in Babylon to "seek the welfare of the city" even while captive in a foreign land. Our heavenly citizenship doesn't make us indifferent to our earthly communities; it makes us free to love them without demanding they become our redemption. We work for justice, peace, and flourishing in our cities not because America saves us, but because Christ does.

Wednesday Philippians 3:20

Paul reminds us that our "citizenship is in heaven." This isn't escapism—it's the most grounded truth we can build on. When we stop expecting our nation or political party to save us, we're freed from despair when it fails. Our hope rests on Christ's return and the establishment of His kingdom, not on the next election cycle or policy victory.

Thursday Philippians 3:21

Christ will "transform our lowly bodies" and "subject all things to himself." This future hope changes how we see our present work in schools, governments, neighborhoods, and families. We labor not to build a perfect earthly kingdom, but to reflect Christ's coming kingdom now. Every act of justice, mercy, and faithfulness is a sign of the restoration Christ will complete.

Friday 1 Peter 2:11

Peter's call to abstain from worldly desires doesn't mean withdrawing from our neighborhoods—it means refusing to let the world's value system (power, status, revenge, fear) shape our hearts. This week, ask yourself: Where am I tempted to seek ultimate meaning in earthly citizenship rather than heavenly? Where can I serve my actual city *as* a heavenly citizen, free from the pressure to save it?

Prayer for Heavenly Citizens

Father, we come before you grateful for the clarity of your Word, which names our true home. You have made us citizens of heaven, a citizenship that transcends every earthly nation, every political boundary, every flag we might be tempted to worship. We adore you for this radical repositioning of our identity—that we are aliens and strangers in this world, yet never without a King, never without a kingdom, never without a Father who has already secured our future (Philippians 3:20).

We confess that we have often lost this clarity. We have wrapped our faith so tightly around our earthly citizenship that we have confused allegiance to nation with allegiance to Christ. We have treated earthly political outcomes as though they were ultimate, and we have let the anxiety of this age convince us that our security depends on which party holds power, which law passes, which border stands or falls. We have forgotten that we are exiles, and in forgetting, we have lost our way.

But here is the good news: our Lord Jesus has already conquered. He sits at the right hand of the Father, and he will transform our lowly bodies to be like his glorious body (Philippians 3:21). This is not a call to withdraw from our earthly cities. Rather, it is a call to seek their welfare from the posture of those who belong to another kingdom (Jeremiah 29:7). We pray that you would give us the grace to be faithful citizens here—to work justly, to speak truth, to care for the vulnerable—while holding all of it lightly, knowing that our true nationality is in heaven.

Grant us wisdom to navigate the difference between earthly citizenship and heavenly citizenship. Free us from the idolatry of the nation-state. Give us courage to say no to the false messiahs of this age and yes to the Messiah who has already won. Let us live as a people secure in our ultimate allegiance, and therefore free to love our earthly neighbors without demanding that they save us. We commit ourselves to you and to this reorientation, trusting that you alone are the source of our identity and our hope. Amen.

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Philippians 3:20, Paul writes that 'our citizenship is in heaven.' Before we unpack what that means, what does earthly citizenship typically demand from us—what claims does it make on our loyalty, our time, our identity?
    → How have you felt those claims most acutely in your own life—at work, at home, in conversations with neighbors or family?
  2. Read 1 Peter 2:11 together. Peter calls believers 'exiles and sojourners.' What's the difference between being an exile and being a citizen? How does that distinction help us understand what Paul means by 'heavenly citizenship'?
    1 Peter 2:11
  3. The sermon suggests that American evangelicalism has sometimes confused earthly citizenship and heavenly citizenship. Where do you see that confusion showing up—in how Christians talk about politics, patriotism, national identity, or the church's role in society?
    → What does that confusion cost us spiritually?
  4. If heavenly citizenship is primary and earthly citizenship is secondary, how should that reshape the way we engage with our earthly cities and nations? Read Jeremiah 29:7 and discuss what 'seeking the welfare of the city' looks like for someone whose true home is in heaven.
    Jeremiah 29:7
    → Does believing heaven is your primary home make you more involved or less involved in earthly politics and community? Why?
  5. Philippians 3:20-21 promises that Christ 'will transform our lowly body to be like his glorious body.' How does this future hope—the certainty that Christ is remaking all things—change how you think about your citizenship today? What does it free you from? What does it call you toward?
    Philippians 3:20-21
  6. As we close, name one specific area of your life—work, neighborhood, family conversation, media consumption—where you need to realign your primary loyalty toward heaven rather than earth. How can this group pray for and encourage you in that realignment this week?
