Invitations to the Great Banquet

Luke 14:12-24 September 13, 2026 Pastor Jim Donohue
Thesis Because God passionately desires to fill His house and there is still room at the great banquet, we must urgently and sacrificially compel people to come in—crossing social barriers, risking rejection, and refusing to give up—knowing that our evangelistic labor will be rewarded in heaven even when it costs us now.

Theological claims· 18
  1. Jesus uses the banquet metaphor to reveal that God joyfully invites those with low social status—the very people the world overlooks—into his family. unit #3
  2. Jesus is forming a new Israel—a new family open to every kind of person—and God's desire to fill his house demands that we compel people to come in. unit #4
  3. You should invite non-Christians to the banquet because God will reward you at the resurrection for reaching out to those who cannot repay you now. unit #6
  4. Jesus calls us to pursue heavenly reward over earthly repayment by serving those who cannot reciprocate. unit #9
  5. Though evangelism often yields no earthly return and seems like a bad investment by worldly standards, in God's economy it is a great investment because we will be rewarded at the resurrection. unit #12
  6. Nothing in life matters more than being invited to and present at the marriage supper of the Lamb. unit #19
  7. Jesus's anger at rejection of the invitation is just, and the Jews who reject the Messiah will be judged and cast into outer darkness. unit #22
  8. Christ's heart naturally bends toward blessing and love, not judgment; we must correct distorted views of God that see him primarily as a wrathful judge. unit #23
  9. Extended quotation from Dane Ortlund supporting the claim that God's natural disposition is blessing, not wrath—what leaps out of God most freely is the desire to bless and bring joy. unit #24
  10. The poor and needy are often more receptive to the gospel than the well-off because they are more aware of their need and less distracted by earthly things. unit #26
  11. We should invite non-Christians to the banquet because God has called us to, and some will come—even when we don't expect them to. unit #28
  12. Christianity is unique among all religions in that salvation is by grace, not works—you don't have to earn your seat at the banquet, but you must come through repentance and faith. unit #32
  13. Lost people are accelerating toward eternal separation from God in hell, and we are commanded in Proverbs 24:11 to rescue those being taken away to death. unit #36
  14. People reject the gospel invitation because they prioritize earthly distractions over eternal realities—and we risk doing the same if we lose sight of the urgency. unit #43
  15. God holds us accountable for our failure to rescue those stumbling to the slaughter; we cannot plead ignorance, and we will be repaid according to our work. unit #45
  16. The phrase 'that my house may be filled' reveals the heart of God—he desires to save and bless, not to condemn, and he commands us to fill his house. unit #49
  17. Jesus took our place in the line to judgment, absorbing the wrath and death we deserve, so that we could be brought into the banquet—this is the substitutionary atonement that makes salvation possible. unit #50
  18. Jesus pursued us when we were running from God, and now he calls us to join him in pursuing others to fill his house. unit #51

We have to step in. We have to hold them back. There is a tremendous urgency when it comes to evangelism. It is life and death.

That's why verse 22, the master says to compel them to come in. The servants of the master. And the master has called us to go out with great urgency, and he's called us to compel people. Look at verse 23. The master said, go out to the highways and hedges and compel people to come in.

40 · Acknowledges the cultural resistance to 'compelling' evangelism—it feels aggressive and offensive—but insists the biblical mandate is clear and must be followed despite cultural discomfort

We should compel people to come in. Now it can be very easy in this increasingly hostile environment to just, to keep the gospel to ourselves. The idea of compelling people seems offensive, doesn't it? It seems too aggressive. But we are clearly called to compel people to come in.

41 · Diagnoses the problem: people need to be compelled because they are not interested, not aware of their need, and not aware of the coming judgment

Now, why do they need to be compelled? Why won't they come? Well, Jesus highlights a significant problem. Most people aren't interested in God's family. They're not interested in the banquet. They don't wanna come. They aren't aware of their need to be at the banquet and the consequences that will come if they ignore the invitation. They don't know that a storm is coming. The master has provided a great banquet and it seemed like people were interested. They had already RSVP'd. But when the second invite goes out, letting people know everything is ready, they don't wanna come. They're not fighting for seats at this banquet. They're making excuses, lame excuses.

42 · Humorous personal story about Aunt Wendy making excuses to avoid babysitting—used to illustrate the absurdity of the excuses in Luke 14 and the pattern of people caught off guard by the invitation

This reminds me of my Aunt Wendy when my brother and I, I have an identical twin brother, and when we were younger, my mom used to call my Aunt Wendy to babysit and she hated to babysit. We would just torture her. We'd pretend we were the other person, switch on her. And so she would always try to make these excuses. But the problem was, and I know this is gonna be hard to understand for some of you, When my mom would call on the phone, back in those days, the phone was attached to the wall. And there was actually a cord that attached you to the— so you couldn't go that far. And this is the other thing. You actually didn't know who was calling you. I know it's hard to comprehend this. It was a surprise. So my mom would call and my Aunt Wendy would answer. She'd ask if she could babysit. And my Aunt Wendy'd be like, just that, you know, she just couldn't do it. And that's exactly what's happening right here. Like these people are getting caught off guard. It's like, I bought this field and I'm gonna go look at it again. Or I just got, what did I get? 5 yoke of oxen and I'm going to inspect them again. Or the other person's like, I just got married. So what? It's free food. You can both come. Your expenses are only going up from here. Free meal. What's the problem?

43 · Diagnoses the root problem in the parable and applies it: people reject the invitation because they prioritize earthly things over eternal things

These people don't want to come. The banquet is not attractive to them. Why? Because they've put second things first. They're distracted by earthly things. The things in this world are more important to them than the things in the world to come. They don't think about eternity. They put second things first. Do we put second things first? A better way of asking this is, what second things are we putting first? It's easy to do, isn't it? This world provides plenty of distractions. Like the Pharisees, are we being distracted from the things that really matter? Do we realize that time is running out? Do we see the lost people around us that are staggering to the slaughter? Is there an urgency to rescue them and bring them into the banquet?

44 · Defines what 'compelling' looks like in practice: persuasion, apologetics, challenging false beliefs, risking offense and rejection

When was the last time you tried to compel someone? I'm not just talking about sharing the gospel with someone or inviting them to church. Are you compelling people to repent and believe? Well, what would that look like? Well, it sounds to me like not taking no for an answer. I think compelling looks like trying to persuade someone. Good arguments, pushing back on wrong worldviews, using apologetics to challenge false beliefs and ideologies, not being afraid to bring the truth. Listen, it is very easy to sacrifice truth on the altar of grace because we don't want to ruffle any feathers. But compelling someone means we have to risk offending. We have to risk being rejected. We have to open our mouths and challenge someone. Brothers and sisters, we have the truth. We have a worldview that makes the most sense out of the world we live in. We understand the power of the gospel. We have been forgiven. We know what we believe. We have no excuse. We have to compel people to come in.

45 · Cites Proverbs 24:10-12 to argue that God holds us accountable for failing to rescue the lost—pleading ignorance will not excuse us, and God will repay according to our work

Here are the surrounding verses to Proverbs 24:11. Listen to what this says. If you faint in the day of adversity, your strength is small. Rescue those who are being taken away to death. Hold back. Those who are stumbling to the slaughter. If you say, behold, we did not know this, does not he who weighs the heart perceive it? Does not he who keeps watch over your soul know it? And will he not repay man according to his work?

46 · Transitions to the fifth and final reason to evangelize, drawn from the servant's report in Luke 14:22 that 'there is still room

Number 5, last point. Number 5, invite them because there is still time.

47 · Expounds Luke 14:22—'still there is room'—and interprets the highways and hedges as an allusion to the gospel going to the Gentiles, people outside Israel who will fill the house

And verse 22, I love how it says it. The servant said, sir, what you commanded has been done and still there is room. So the great banquet starts to fill up, but there's still room. And the master says, go out of the city now to the highways and the hedges. This is alluding to the gospel going to the Gentiles, people who are far from God, people of different ethnicities. They will fill his house.

48 · Personal reflection on preaching in churches with empty seats, interpreting those empty seats as a sign that there is still room for the lost to be saved

Now there were 2 phrases that deeply affected me. The first was, still there is room. Now I've had the privilege of preaching in many Sovereign Grace churches and lots of other venues and contexts, both in the US and around the world, and I often look out and I see empty seats, sometimes lots of empty seats, and you know what I think? There is still room. There is still room for people to be forgiven of their sins. There is still room for people, men and women, to come to know Christ. There is still room for the lost and dying. There is still room for the condemned and guilty. There is still room for the addicted. There is still room for the self-righteous. Is still room. Brothers and sisters, we are not done. Our job is not finished. Still there is room.

49 · Interprets 'that my house may be filled' as capturing the heart of God—his desire for his house to be full is the driving force behind the Great Commission and the church's evangelistic mission

Now when the servant pointed out that there was still room, the master responded with the other phrase that stood out to me. He said, that my house may be filled. And this phrase captures the heart of God and the heart of Christ. He is not a God that desires anyone to perish. He desires to bless, to forgive, to save. He desires that his house may be filled. And God is saying to us this morning, fill my house. Cross of grace, fill my house. Fill my house. Fill my house.

50 · Returns to the Schindler's List imagery to make the gospel vivid: we were in the line to slaughter, but Jesus took our place

Now let's go back to that image in Schindler's List of standing in line. Do you remember when you were standing in that line? Do you remember when you were staggering towards your own death, stumbling to the slaughter? We had no way to escape. We were doomed until Jesus tapped us on the shoulder and took our place in that line. He exchanged places with us. He took up the cross, our cross. He placed it on his shoulders and he staggered forward. He stumbled up the hill called Calvary, taking the death that we deserve, taking the hell that we deserve, absorbing the wrath and fury that we deserve. He gave his life because it was the only way he could fill his house. He had to die so that we could be brought into the banquet.

51 · Interprets the servant's mission to the highways and byways as an image of Christ pursuing us when we were running from God—and now calls us to join him in that same pursuit

And then he pursued us. I love the picture of, the servant running out to the highways and the byways. This is what Jesus did for us because we were running full speed away from God. We were far from God, but he went further. He ran us down to bring us in to fill his house. And now he calls us to join him in this glorious task.

52 · Extended historical illustration from Hacksaw Ridge—Desmond Doss rescuing 70 men one at a time through the night, praying 'Lord, give me one more' after each rescue

Let me close with this story. Some of you might have seen the movie Hacksaw Ridge. It was based on a true story. A private named Desmond Doss was in Okinawa during World War II, and he refused to fight. He was a believer and he was a pacifist. He even refused to carry a gun, and he was persecuted terribly for this. But in the battle for Hacksaw Ridge, which was up this huge cliff, it was like, I don't know, like 60 or 70 feet up. The American soldiers would have to climb up this netting and then they would battle the Japanese. Well, in this one battle, they pushed the Japanese back, but the Japanese counterattacked in the late afternoon and just cut the Americans down. The Americans were called to retreat at around 6:00 PM and men just flew down this netting to get away. But Desmond Doss refused to leave. And this is where his work of rescuing men and women began. He crawled around hiding from the Japanese. He crawled around among the bodies and he found men with shattered legs and bandaged them up. And then he took them back to the cliff and he lowered them down with ropes. And every time he would rescue a man, he would say, Lord, give me one more. And then he'd go back and he found men choking on their own blood. He cleared their airways. He dragged them back and lowered them down and say, Lord, give me one more. He found men riddled with shrapnel, men that had lost limbs. He used tourniquets, many tourniquets. He'd pick them up, carry them back, and lower them down and say, Lord, give me one more. And as night grew, he had to hide from the Japanese soldiers who were coming through, killing everyone that was still alive. There were even men that were crazy stumbling toward the Japanese line. He would grab them, drag them down, drag them back, and lower them back down. And every time he would say, Lord, give me one more. And all through that night in utter darkness, exhaustion and dehydration, he dragged one dying man after another back to that cliff and lowered them down until over 70 men were rescued. Lord, give me just one more.

53 · Concluding charge to the congregation: adopt Desmond Doss's prayer as your evangelistic posture—'Lord, give me one more

Brothers and sisters, that needs to be our prayer. Lord, give me just one more. Who are the people in your life that are far away that you can reach out to, that you can invite to the banquet? Remember that person that invited you. Don't give up. Don't stop compelling men and women to come in. Let's, by God's grace, fill his house. Amen.

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Luke 14:21, the master tells his servant to bring in 'the poor and crippled and blind and lame'—those with the lowest social status. What does Jesus reveal about God's heart through his choice to fill the banquet with these particular people rather than the wealthy and influential?
    Luke 14:21
    → How does this challenge any assumptions we might hold about who God is most eager to save?
  2. The invited guests in Luke 14:18-20 all make excuses—land, oxen, marriage—that are not sinful in themselves. What does their pattern of refusal tell us about what people prioritize over accepting God's invitation to salvation?
    Luke 14:18-20
  3. In verse 23, the master commands his servant to 'compel people to come in, that my house may be filled.' The word 'compel' suggests urgency and persistence. What does this urgency reflect about God's desire, and how should it shape the way we approach evangelism in our own lives?
    Luke 14:23
    → When have you experienced someone's persistent invitation to faith? How did their urgency affect you?
  4. According to Luke 14:13-14, Jesus instructs us to invite those who 'cannot repay you' because we will be 'repaid at the resurrection of the just.' Why do you think Jesus contrasts earthly repayment with heavenly reward, and what does this reveal about how we should evaluate the 'success' of our evangelistic efforts?
    Luke 14:13-14
    → What earthly returns do we naturally expect when we invest time in sharing the gospel, and how might heavenly reward reshape those expectations?
  5. Proverbs 24:11-12 says, 'Rescue those being taken away to death…if you say, "Behold, we did not know this," does not he who weighs the heart perceive it?' Given this command and the urgency in the banquet parable, what does God hold us accountable for regarding those around us who have not yet heard or responded to the gospel?
    Proverbs 24:11-12

How does grasping that Christ absorbed the judgment we deserve change our motivation to urgently invite others to come in, and what does it say about the gospel's power to transform both us and those we reach?

5-day reading plan

This week we meditate on God's passionate desire to fill His house with every kind of person, the urgency of our evangelistic calling, the substitutionary work of Christ that makes our invitation possible, and the eternal reward awaiting those who compel the lost to come in.

Monday Revelation 19:6-9

John's vision of the wedding supper anchors our entire evangelistic urgency: this is the banquet to which we compel people—the very throne room of heaven itself. When we grasp that every soul we reach will either feast eternally with Christ or be cast into darkness, our invitations become weighted with the gravity of eternity. We cannot treat evangelism as optional when we understand that presence at the Lamb's supper is the culmination of all God's saving work.

Tuesday Proverbs 24:11

Solomon's proverb echoes the master's command to compel people into the banquet: rescue those stumbling toward the slaughter. This is not gentle suggestion but divine imperative, grounded in the reality that people without Christ are indeed being led away to judgment. Our silence in the face of such urgency is itself a form of complicity—we cannot claim ignorance or neutrality when Scripture so plainly commands us to intervene with the gospel.

Wednesday Proverbs 24:10-12

The fuller proverb warns against pleading weakness or ignorance—God sees our hesitation and will judge our restraint. Yet this accountability is not burdensome; it is grace-soaked: God promises to repay us according to our labor, even when our evangelistic efforts yield no earthly return. The One who knows our hearts will reward every sacrifice we make to compel the lost to come in, transforming what the world calls foolishness into eternal gain.

Thursday Revelation 19:6-9

The joy resounding from heaven in John's vision—'Hallelujah! For the Lord our God the Almighty reigns'—celebrates not merely creation but redemption, Christ's triumph over death and judgment. We can compel others to the banquet only because Jesus absorbed the wrath we deserved, securing our place and opening the door for every kind of person. Our evangelistic labor participates in His redemptive work, extending to others the substitutionary grace we ourselves have received.

Friday Proverbs 24:10-12

Solomon reminds us that the One who judges the heart sees every costly act of evangelism—every conversation with someone who turns away, every bridge crossed to reach the overlooked, every risk taken when reciprocal blessing seems impossible. God's ledger is kept in heaven, not on earth, and He rewards us according to our faithfulness, not our visible results. This week, as we sit with these truths, we are invited to join Christ in His passionate pursuit of the lost, trusting that our labor will not be in vain when we stand before Him at the resurrection.
