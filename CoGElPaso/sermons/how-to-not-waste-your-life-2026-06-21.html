How to (Not) Waste Your Life

Titus 3:8-15 June 21, 2026 Pastor Ricky Alcantar
Thesis To avoid wasting your life, you must avoid distraction from the gospel, embrace discomfort for Christ's kingdom over personal comfort, and devote yourself fully to good works that advance and adorn the gospel.

Illustrations· 5
  1. personal story · unit #3 — The pastor tells the story of a church member facing a life transition whose deepest concern was not career, relationships, or experiences, but whether he would waste his life. This story illustrates that beneath surface-level worries about achievements, possessions, and experiences lies a universal human question about whether life will be wasted. The illustration sets up the sermon's central question.
  2. personal story · unit #10 — The pastor tells a story of a conversation with Chuck Mosley, one of the church's earliest pastors, about how the early leaders avoided theological distraction. When asked how they stayed focused, Mosley revealed they never strategically planned to avoid distractions—they simply had their hands full with God's saving work, church restoration to Acts design, and belief that time was short. The illustration demonstrates that being consumed with the right things naturally crowds out distractions.
  3. historical example · unit #14 — The pastor vividly illustrates the discomfort of every person mentioned in Titus 3:12-13: Artemis and Tychicus sent to 'Pirate Island' Crete rather than a desirable location, Titus told to leave his settled life and family to meet Paul, Apollos the gifted orator sent to an unglamorous location instead of Rome, and Zenas the lawyer choosing discomfort over the comfortable life his profession could provide. The illustration establishes that none of these early Christians were comfortable—all were being sent or sending others.
  4. analogy · unit #19 — The pastor illustrates the concept of vocational devotion using escalating levels of basketball involvement: casual viewing, dedicated fan loyalty, recreational playing, rec league participation, and finally Division I player whose entire life revolves around the sport. The illustration maps casual-to-vocational relationship with basketball onto Paul's call for vocational devotion to good works.
  5. personal story · unit #22 — The pastor illustrates fruitfulness with a personal story of planting cacti in his front yard. Most grow vigorously; one barely shows life, prompting him to keep checking if it's alive or dead. The illustration maps onto Christian lives: Paul doesn't want people looking at our lives wondering if there's any life there. The unit clarifies that fruitfulness doesn't mean perfection—sometimes it starts with repentance—but over time worthy work should visibly shine and grow.
Theological claims· 9
  1. The more subtle and deadly danger facing Christians is not denial of the faith but distraction from it through unprofitable controversies. unit #4
  2. For most Christians, the greatest danger is not denial of the faith but slow, week-by-week distraction from the essentials—Satan entangles believers through quarrelsome disputes. unit #6
  3. People who neglect their families to engage in constant theological disputes become warped, sinful, and self-condemned—do not associate with them. unit #8
  4. Paul's test for avoiding distraction is simple: is it profitable and worthwhile—do not give your best energy to what is profitless and worthless. unit #9
  5. The greatest danger American Christians face is valuing comfort over Christ and his kingdom—sending and being sent only happen when we value Christ above comfort. unit #13
  6. Comfort is the path to a wasted life; discomfort for the sake of Christ and the kingdom is the path to an unwasted life. unit #15
  7. Christians are called to vocational devotion to good works that advance or adorn the gospel, using every opportunity God puts in front of them rather than waiting for dramatic future assignments. unit #20
  8. Crete mattered to God because people made in his image lived there who did not know Christ—unglamorous places matter to God when image-bearers need the gospel. unit #25
  9. This church exists and we have heard the gospel because first-century believers refused distraction and lived sent and sending others—we are called to continue this mission. unit #27

5-day reading plan

This week we meditate on Paul's closing charge to Titus: how to build a life that matters for Christ by refusing distraction, choosing discomfort for the kingdom, and devoting ourselves fully to good works that advance the gospel.

Monday Titus 1:1-4

Paul opens his letter to Titus by anchoring all ministry in the gospel itself: the knowledge of the truth that accords with godliness, the promise of eternal life. Before Paul gives Titus any instruction, he reminds him *why* the instruction matters. An unwasted life begins here—not with busyness, but with clarity about what we're actually sent to do: advance the gospel and make disciples. Everything else is commentary.

Tuesday Proverbs 1:25-33

Proverbs warns that those who reject wisdom and despise her reproof will eat the fruit of their own way—they ignore what matters and reap loss. This mirrors Paul's warning in Titus 3:9: avoid unprofitable disputes, which entangle believers slowly, week by week. The danger is not that we deny Christ; it's that we get caught in endless quarrels while the mission waits. We refuse the distraction by asking Paul's simple test: Is this profitable? Is this worthwhile? If not, we let it go.

Wednesday Acts 20:28-32

Paul warns the Ephesian elders that after his departure, false teachers will arise and draw followers away. But the remedy is not debate—it's devotion to the word of God and to prayer. The early church advanced not by winning every argument but by staying laser-focused on the gospel and the care of God's flock. We inherit their work today. The question each of us must answer: Will we be distracted by the next controversy, or will we be sent like they were sent?

Thursday Titus 1:12-14

Paul cites the Cretan poet: 'Cretans are always liars.' Titus is sent to a hard place, a place of entrenched sin, where the work will be uncomfortable. Paul doesn't apologize for the hardship—he emphasizes it. The call to good works, to advance the gospel, to build the church in Crete means Titus cannot choose comfort. An unwasted life is never the comfortable life. It's the life willing to go where Christ sends us, even when it costs.

Friday Titus 3:14

Paul's final charge: 'Let our people learn to devote themselves to good works, so as to help cases of genuine need, and not be unfruitful.' This is not a call to dramatic, distant assignments—it's a call to see the work *right in front of you* and give it everything. Your family, your neighborhood, your workplace, your church community: these are not distractions from the gospel mission. They *are* the gospel mission. An unwasted life is built by being faithfully present and devotedly useful in the place God has already put you.
