Theological claims· 4
  1. When betrayed, the proper Christian response is to run to God in prayer rather than to despair, other people, or one's own feelings—a pattern David models and Calvin articulates. unit #8
  2. To withhold our groaning from God in distress is proof of lack of faith; true faith brings the distress to God in prayer rather than attempting self-reliance, which is as futile as plastic toy armor against real danger. unit #13
  3. David's ability to sleep in mortal danger was not psychological denial but genuine trust in God's sustaining power—a trust Calvin distinguishes from the false peace of the wicked. unit #17
  4. David's physical sleep and waking sustained by God is a type of Christian resurrection—through Christ we can die and rise again both spiritually and eternally because of God's sustaining grace. unit #28

Instead, David's example is to look to the faithfulness of God in Scripture, but also in his own life. So my question for us today is, where have you seen God keep His promises in your life? Where have you seen God at work in the specifics of your life? One of my friends, Todd, another pastor here, one time I was like, how do you notice the Holy Spirit's work in your life? And he goes, you notice it. Sometimes we think the small things of the Lord are not of the Lord. We look at little things that happen and go, oh, that just happened. Well, how did it happen? Is the Lord not the sustainer of all life? Is he not sovereign over all things? So when the thing. When we look back at things in our life that where the Lord has been faithful, it would be a right response to say, lord, you were faithful in that, even in that little thing. Maybe he was faithful in a really gigantic thing. Maybe in the moment you didn't feel like he was faithful. But as you look back, you go, oh, you totally were faithful. This is what David encourages to do in our own songs. Might I encourage you to make it a regular rhythm in your own life to remember the specifics of God's faithfulness to his people and in your own life.

26 · Vogan applies the third movement by confronting the congregation's natural responses to betrayal—vengeful self-vindication ('John Wick') or resigned despair ('Eeyore')—then pivots to the gospel, showing that David is a type of Christ and that Jesus, facing the ultimate betrayal by Judas and God's own people, modeled forgiveness rather than vengeance

Which brings us to our final application, our hope in salvation. Remember, in the face of betrayal or when we've been wronged, the Christian's hope rests in protection. And salvation is all a work of the Lord. When we've been wronged, our desire is often to be our own vindicators. And an overdeveloped sense of justice can lead us to to misplaced attempts at self vindication. I must make this right. In a moment of betrayal, there's two paths. We can go full John Wick and destroy everyone who killed our puppy who deemed that wronged us. Or we lean to resigned apathy and grief, giving up on hope, giving up on anything that's good. Much like Eeyore, David's way shows us a third way that actually points to our only ultimate hope. This is the gospel. The Bible is the story of Jesus. So we would be remiss if we did not see Jesus here in Psalm 3. In the face of one of the deepest betrayals imaginable, the picture of David can actually be seen as a dim image of our ultimate hope in the life and death of Jesus. In fact, if we look closely at Jesus, we see a picture of true betrayal. Specifically, he was betrayed by Judas, one of his closest followers and confidants. More broadly, Jesus was betrayed by those who he came to serve. God's chosen people were the ones who turned on him and crucified him. And yet among his last words were, what Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do.

27 · Vogan connects the gospel to the baptisms happening that day, explaining that baptism declares the miracle of being brought from death to life through Jesus, who endured the ultimate betrayal so believers could have eternal hope in their own seasons of betrayal

In baptisms today, we see people who have surrendered their lives, their whole lives to the Lord. They've acknowledged their betrayal of the Lord through their sin, turned from their sin, confessed that Jesus is Lord, believed in their hearts that God raised him from the dead, and they have been forgiven and saved. Their baptisms today are outward declarations of that inward miracle being brought from death to life. The only way we find true and eternal hope in the face of our own seasons of betrayal, distress or pain is to look to the One who went to the full lengths of betrayal and pain and death for us that we might have life

28 · Vogan establishes the typological fulfillment: just as David slept and woke sustained by God, believers through Christ can face death itself and rise again, both spiritually in salvation and physically in resurrection, because of God's sustaining grace

In Jesus, we see the ultimate picture of salvation that belongs to the Lord Jesus, for the joy set before him endured the cross, that through him we would find hope and salvation for our souls. So just as David physically slept, was sustained by the Lord and woke again, we through Christ too can. We can sleep, we can die, and we can rise again both in our salvation and eternally because of the sustaining grace of the Lord.

29 · Vogan concludes with a direct pastoral charge to believers to trust fully in God's care despite betrayal, then pivots to address unbelievers present, declaring that apart from Christ there is no hope and all human self-help efforts are futile—only Christ can lift our heads and provide salvation

So Church, no matter the betrayal you have faced or will face in your life, if you are a Christian, you can, with full confidence, trust in the care and salvation of the Lord. We're going to pray for this. We're going to ask that we as Christians would be reminded of this. But can I talk? If you're here this morning and you're not a believer, you would not call yourself a Christian. This is the only hope for life and death apart from Christ. There is no hope apart from Christ. We are stuck trying to pull ourselves up by our bootstraps. We are stuck navel gazing. We are stuck trying to do things of ourselves. And we will never ever, ever be able to lift our heads on our own. We will be stuck in endless patterns of self vindication and resignation. What is our only hope in life and death? The answer, Church, is Christ alone. Christ alone.

30 · Vogan transitions to prayer by inviting those who are carrying specific burdens of betrayal or broken trust to seek prayer ministry, emphasizing that the prayer team exists to help bear burdens and that walking out without seeking help would be to miss the practical application of the sermon

So as we pray, if the Lord has brought anything specific to mind that you're continuing to carry, rather than trusting it completely to the Lord, we want to pray for you. This prayer team over here by the cross, isn't there just for their benefit? Whether you show up or not, they're going to pray for you. But if the Lord is bringing specific things to mind this morning, don't walk out the doors without letting us help you bear that burden and lay it at the foot of the cross. Find the hope and mercy of Jesus.

31 · Vogan closes with a pastoral prayer asking God to help the congregation trust in His care and salvation despite life's challenges, affirming God's goodness, faithfulness, and enduring mercy

Lord, would you help us this morning? Would you cause us in the face of whatever challenges our life brings, betrayal, a broken trust, whatever that is? Lord, would you help us to trust, cause us to trust in the care and salvation of you, O Lord, you are good, you are faithful for all generations. Your mercy does indeed endure forever. In Jesus' name, Amen.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace David's response to betrayal through the cross-references that illumine his trust: from the historical narrative of his flight, through his confession and repentance, to the gospel promise that sustains us when we are betrayed.

Monday 2 Samuel 15:1-14

David's flight from Jerusalem in the face of Absalom's rebellion shows us a man stripped of political power and familial trust—yet his first act is to seek God's counsel, not to marshal forces for self-defense. This historical narrative grounds the psalm's opening cry: betrayal strips away our human props, and in that nakedness, we discover whether we truly trust the Lord to vindicate us or whether we will scramble to save ourselves.

Tuesday Psalm 51:1-10

David's later confession reveals the heart posture that animates Psalm 3: a willingness to bring the full weight of sin, sorrow, and shame before God rather than hiding in false strength. When we compare Psalm 51's anguish with Psalm 3's trust, we see that authentic faith does not deny pain—it pours it out before the God who hears and sustains, the God whose mercy is greater than our breaking.

Wednesday Philippians 4:6-7

Paul's command to bring our anxieties to God in prayer, not to suppress them, echoes David's example: the peace that guards our hearts comes not from pretending danger is absent but from entrusting our very safety to the God who never sleeps. Like David resting while enemies surround him, we can know genuine rest when we release our grip on outcomes and grip instead the faithfulness of our Father.

Thursday Luke 23:34

On the cross, Jesus embodies David's psalm in ultimate form: surrounded by enemies, abandoned by disciples, yet praying for forgiveness rather than vindication. His betrayal was infinite in depth, yet His trust in the Father's sustaining grace was absolute—a trust that moved Him to intercede even for those who pierced Him. In Jesus, we see that betrayal met with trust becomes the very means of our redemption.

Friday Romans 10:9; Hebrews 12:2

Confessing Jesus as Lord and believing His resurrection (Romans 10:9) completes what David's trust in Psalm 3 began: the pattern of dying and rising, of being sustained through mortal danger into new life. As we fix our eyes on Jesus, the author and perfecter of our faith (Hebrews 12:2), we discover that every betrayal we endure—even death itself—is met by a God whose sustaining grace raises us to eternal life. Our trust in His salvation is not naive hope but confident grip on the One who has already conquered the grave.

Pray together this week

A Prayer of Trust When Betrayed

Father, we marvel at Your character—You are our shield, our glory, the lifter of our heads (Psalm 3:3). You do not turn away from us in our deepest pain, but You invite us to cry out to You with our whole hearts. We confess that when betrayal strikes, especially from those we have loved and trusted, our first instinct is to vindicate ourselves, to grasp for our own restoration, to spiral into despair or self-reliance. We want to be our own shield, to lift our own heads. Forgive us for the pride that makes us hesitate to bring our groaning directly to You, as if our distress were too small or our pain too complicated for Your care.

Yet the gospel tells us that Christ Himself endured the deepest betrayal—rejected and crucified by those He came to save—and through that abandonment He secured our eternal salvation and vindication (Luke 23:34). His resurrection declares that no betrayal, no matter how cutting, can separate us from God's sustaining grace. In the gospel we have a Savior who knows our pain from the inside and has triumphed over it forever (Hebrews 12:2).

Grant us, we pray, the faith of David—the courage to run to You in prayer rather than away into self-pity or self-vindication (Philippians 4:6-7). Teach us to sleep in Your sustaining care, knowing that You watch over us when we cannot watch over ourselves (Psalm 3:5). Give us the grace to trust that You alone can vindicate us, defend us, and preserve us through whatever betrayal we face. We commit ourselves afresh to You—the only sure foundation, the only true shield—and we do so together as Your people, upheld by Your unfailing mercy.
