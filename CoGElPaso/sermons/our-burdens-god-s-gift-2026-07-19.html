Our Burdens, God's Gift

Ecclesiastes 3:1-22 July 19, 2026 Pastor James Shoemake
Thesis God handles what belongs to God, and humanity receives what belongs to humanity; therefore, we must release the burdens we were never meant to carry and receive the gift God has placed before us—ultimately, the gift of Christ himself.

Theological claims· 9
  1. None of us get to choose when life's seasons change—they happen according to God's sovereign timing, not our preferences. unit #8
  2. The longing to see the whole story is God-given and good; the sin is our demand to satisfy that longing ourselves by possessing God's perspective. unit #17
  3. God will judge all wickedness and injustice with certainty and comprehensiveness, even though His timing is not ours. unit #22
  4. The deepest burden is the refusal of joyful creaturely dependence—the same temptation that began in Eden when Adam and Eve reached for what belonged to God and refused the gift they had been given. unit #26
  5. The gift is to be God's creature—eating, drinking, working, sleeping, laughing, loving—and these are not consolation prizes but what God intended all along. unit #27
  6. We cling to burdens because each one whispers the lie that carrying it will finally make us enough. unit #30
  7. Jesus Christ carries the burdens we were never meant to carry and offers himself as the gift we were always meant to receive; He is the fulfillment of what Ecclesiastes could only hint at. unit #32
  8. Christ is the gift Himself, and in union with Him, His righteousness, standing, life, and inheritance become ours, transforming us from creatures defined by accomplishment to children defined by union with Him. unit #33
  9. The gospel is God inviting exhausted people to lay every burden down and receive His Son—not to become enough, but to belong to Christ who loved us as dust. unit #34

25 · The pastor reads Ecclesiastes 3:18-22 and interprets the passage's most confronting claim: we are creatures—dust like the beasts—and we resent the creaturely limits God has given us

I'm going to read verses 18 through 22. I said in my heart with regard to the children of man that God is testing them, that they may see that they themselves are but beasts. For what happens to the children of man And what happens to the beasts are the same. As one dies, so dies the other. They all have the same breath, and man has no advantage over the beasts, for all is vanity. All go to one place, all are from the dust, and to the dust all return. Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of beasts goes down into the earth? So I saw that there is nothing better than a man should rejoice in his work, for that is his lot. Who can bring himself to see what will be after him? The preacher arrives at the deepest burden of it all. Throughout this chapter, he has slowly stripped away every illusion that we might carry. We cannot master time, we cannot see the whole story, and we cannot judge the world rightly. So the question becomes, well then, what are we? His answer, we're creatures, we're beasts. We breathe borrowed breath just as the beasts do, and we come from dust just as the beasts do. To the dust we're gonna return just as creatures. We cannot see beyond the limits that God has given us. We die just as every other living creature dies. If we're honest, I think maybe this is a little unsettling. We don't like to be reminded of the limits that we have been given in this life. I think in fact, we resent them. We resent the fact that I have to sleep so much. I'm weak. I resent the moments that I have to wait on something. I resent that I have to be dependent upon other people. I resent the fact that death is true and it becomes us all. We resent the truth that we are simple creatures of this world.

26 · The pastor traces the fourth burden to its origin in Eden: the refusal of joyful creaturely dependence in favor of grasping after God's place

To this idea, the preacher's, he's saying even something more that can be frustrating. God's testing us with this. He's testing us to see if we will remember that this is what we are. At first we might think it's a burden to be a creature, but I think that's just simply impossible. Being a creature is not a burden. No other creature considers it to be a burden to be what it is. Like, I don't think birds resent being birds. I don't think a deer wants to all of a sudden wake up being a mountain lion. I don't think they think that way. Only humans, we spend our lives resisting the limits that God has lovingly given us. The problem isn't that we are creatures. The problem is that we think being a creature is beneath us. The problem then becomes a burden that grows in us. It leads us to refuse the joyful dependence we have upon our Creator. And truly, this is the burden that It all began with the scene in the Garden of Eden. What exactly was wrong with being a human? Adam and Eve, they were made in God's image. They, they were sinless. They were given dominion. They walked with God. I think in every sense of the word, they were blessed. The serpent's temptation wasn't that humanity was somehow lacking. It was the temptation to reject the joyful dependence of being creatures. Instead, he's attempting to grasp after God's place. It was the temptation to determine good and evil for ourselves. It was the temptation to remove our dependence upon God, the temptation to push beyond our limits that God has lovingly established for us. The temptation to become what only God is. Adam and Eve literally refused the gift and took up what belonged to God. Ever since that day, humanity has been doing the very same thing. We keep reaching for what is not ours and neglecting the gift that we have been given. God has already placed it before us. Just as Adam and Eve reached out for the fruit, we are tempted to reach beyond our limits and into what God has for himself.

27 · The pastor names the ultimate burden (being a creator) and identifies the gift (being God's creature through eating, drinking, working, sleeping, laughing, loving), insisting these are God's original intention, not consolation prizes

The ultimate burden that we were never meant to carry is the burden of being a creator. Try as we might to be more than creatures, that simply is what we are. Creatures. To be more than creatures. If the burden we should not be picking up is being a creator, then what is the gift? Let's say it this way. What is the gift? The gift is to be God's creature by eating, by drinking, by working, enjoying life, and sleeping, laughing and loving. Let me say, these are not consolation prizes. This is not what's left over after we fail becoming gods. These are what God has intended for his creatures all along. The prize of being a god, the hope that we could recreate ourselves in our own image, that's not a prize. That is, that is a crown none of us could wear. That is a throne none of us could ever sit on.

28 · The pastor issues the fourth major application (release the burden of being a creator, receive the gift of creatureliness) with pastoral acknowledgment that this is the hardest to implement, and gives concrete diagnostic instructions for recognizing moments of resistance to creaturely limits

So here's the 4th point the preacher is helping us see. Release the burden of being a creator, receive the gift of your creatureliness. Let me ask again, are we exhausted because life is heavy, or is it because we have picked up what doesn't belong to us? When you let God handle what belongs to Him and receive what belongs to us, release the burden of being the creator. God never asked you to become Him. Instead, receive the gift of your creatureliness. I think this one might be the hardest to implement, but let's make an attempt this week. Pay attention to the moments where you become frustrated by your own limits. The weariness, the unanswered questions, your inability to do everything, a reminder of your own fragility. Notice the places where you're fighting your own finiteness. Those moments where you're reminded you cannot do it all, know it all, or even live forever. These are not moments where you have failed. Instead of resisting those limits, receive them as a reminder that you are a creature loved by your Creator. Then rejoice that your life is held by the one who never grows weary.

29 · The pastor synthesizes the sermon's four movements into a four-step process (recognize, release, receive, rejoice) and transitions toward the gospel resolution

So to recap, and before I close, here's a quick 4-step process that matches what we've been discussing today. Step 1, recognize the burden. What have you picked up that isn't yours to carry? Don't ignore it. It's there. Step 2, release the burden. Give up what isn't yours to carry. Step 3, receive the gift. Take hold of what is yours. Step 4, Rejoice in the Creator. Continue in faith that this is what's best for those who take up the calling to follow God's plan.

30 · The pastor diagnoses why we cling to burdens despite knowing we shouldn't: each burden promises to make us 'enough' by offering security, certainty, righteousness, or autonomy

Now, why do we insist on picking up these burdens? Like, we know we should release them. We hear this, we think, everybody's nodding, yes, I should let that go, uh-huh. What's so enticing? Well, the temptation to control is there because we might find security in it. The temptation to try to understand why all things are happening is because we hope to find certainty. We see the temptation to judge wanting a hope for righteousness. We see the temptation of becoming the creator desiring to fulfill this thing inside of us wanting autonomy. The problem is that every burden we cling to is whispering in our heart, saying, if you carry me, you will finally be enough. This is why it's so hard to put them down. We resent the fact that we can't make it all come together in our own efforts, but we keep trying anyway.

31 · The pastor returns to the grocery store metaphor to frame the gospel transition: Ecclesiastes tells us to release burdens but doesn't fully answer who carries them

The last point we'll have for today is this tension that we'll eventually find that Ecclesiastes doesn't fully answer. The preacher has shown us what burdens to release, But he has not yet told us what truly happens to them. So going back to the grocery store where I had everything in my hands, it would have been a blessing to have a cart and put them in there, right? But even if I did, the groceries don't disappear, right? The oil, the sugar, the milk, it'd still be there. The cart simply carries what I was never meant to carry. So if I let God handle what belongs to him and receive what belongs to me, what happens to those burdens? Where do they go? I mean, the better question, and maybe you're thinking of it, who will carry them?

32 · The pastor delivers the Christological climax: Jesus is the one who carries the burdens we cannot, fulfilling what Ecclesiastes could only point toward

The Son of God, Jesus the Christ. Jesus takes upon himself the burdens that we were never meant to carry, and he offers us a gift that we were always meant to receive. We no longer carry the burden of mastering the seasons because Christ is the one that reigns over all time. We no longer carry the burdens of creating lasting significance because Christ's work endures forever. We no longer carry the burden of final judgment because Christ is the righteous judge. We no longer carry the burden of becoming more than creatures because the Creator himself became a creature like us to redeem us. He is the only one who is truly more than a creature yet willingly became one. Jesus, the eternal Son of God, came to dwell with us, as us, and did not merely help us with the, the carrying of the things that was crushing us. Yes, he could only— he's the only one that could carry what we could not. He could carry our sin, our death, and our inability to stand righteous before a holy God. And this is what the preacher could only hint at. He could tell us to receive the gift, but he could not yet tell us how that gift would come. He could tell us that there is nothing better than rejoicing in the work that God has given you, but he lived centuries before the one who would carry the greatest burden that we have. So Ecclesiastes leaves us with this invitation, but Christ reveals its fulfillment. Jesus does not simply say put down the burden. He stretches out his hand and says give it to me.

33 · The pastor develops the gospel's fullness: Christ doesn't merely remove burdens; He gives us Himself—His righteousness, standing, life, and inheritance become ours through union with Him, transforming our identity from dust to children

But here's the wonder of the gospel. Jesus does not simply take something from us. He gives us something. In fact, he gives us someone. He gives us himself. Think about that. The greatest gift that God has given us, it's not forgiveness. It's not heaven. It's not freedom from guilt. It's not even a purpose in our toil. He gave us his Son. That's the greatest gift. So here's the final point for today. Jesus the Christ takes our burden and is the gift himself. What does this mean that Christ gives us himself? It means his righteousness is our righteousness. It means his standing before the Father is our standing before the Father. It means his life becomes our life. It means his inheritance is our inheritance. We spend so much of our lives trying to establish our identity through our work, through our accomplishments, Through our control, through our attempts to do good in life. But Christ, in him, we no longer have to create an identity. We are given an identity. We are still creatures. We are still dust. But in Christ, we are creatures who have become children. We're no longer defined by what we think we can accomplish. Under the sun. We're united to the one who came from above the sun and entered our world beneath it and now shares his own life with us forever.

34 · The pastor defines the gospel in direct contrast to burden-bearing religion: Christianity is not about better circumstances or self-improvement but about belonging to Christ, who loved us as dust and invites exhausted people to receive Him as the gift

Christianity is not ultimately about receiving better circumstances in life. It's about belonging to Christ. This is true and real grace, the grace that God gives by not requiring us to become enough before coming to him. The grace that he's never gonna ask us to carry the weight of making ourselves righteous. The grace that he loves the dust that we are. The grace that he knew our weakness before we admitted it and still came to become our strength. The gospel is not about God asking exhausted people to carry more burdens. The gospel is God inviting exhausted people to lay every burden down before him and receive his Son. If you feel exhausted today, if you know what it is to carry a burden that was never supposed to belong to you, then look to Jesus Christ. He has come to bear your burdens, wipe away your brokenness, pay your ransom, clothe you in his righteousness, and welcome you into his own life. Your response, simply wonderful. Receive the gift.

35 · The pastor issues a pastoral invitation to respond—both for those who have never received Christ and for believers feeling pressed by burdens—calling them to the cross after worship, and then gives a final charge to the whole congregation

If you sense that you've never felt that release, I would say after worship, please come over here to the cross. We would love to tell you more about a love greater than the brokenness you carry. If you know what it is to have a relationship with Jesus, but you still feel the burdens of life pressing in, Please come over here to the cross. We would allow— we would love to join you in reaching out to God and his love for you in your life. There is in our life much more than the burdens we face. Church, this week release the burdens that were never belonging to you. Receive the gift that your Father has already placed into your hands. And there is nothing better than rejoice in the work that he has given you today.

36 · The pastor closes with prayer, returning to the grocery store metaphor one final time and drawing on Matthew 11:28-30 (Christ's yoke is easy and His burden is light) to pray for the congregation's ability to release burdens and receive rest

Would you pray with me? Heavenly Father, we thank you for this day, this time, this space that you have blessed us with to look into your word. God, convict us of where we have picked up things that were yours, the burdens, the fears. You have still yet blessed us with a gift, even where we are sitting at a grocery store carrying things that we shouldn't carry. Your love, your gift is there before us. Your Son has come to us, and his voice, his truth, he tells us that we can come to him as we are burdened and heavy laden, and that we will find rest in him. That you still have a yoke, but we get to learn from you. We know that your love, your gentleness, is a rest that we can find our souls in. Your yoke is easier than the burdens that we pick up. Your burden is lighter than the ones that we are so tempted to carry. Help us lean into that truth and love you all the more for the gift that we are given. It's in Jesus Christ's name we pray, amen.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the four burdens that exhaust us—control, significance, judgment, and refusal of creatureliness—and discover in each the gift God has placed in our hands, culminating in Christ himself as the gift we were always meant to receive.

Monday Psalm 118:23-24

The psalmist declares 'This is the day the Lord has made'—not a day we engineered or controlled, but a gift fashioned by God's own hand. When we grasp that every season, every transition, every moment belongs to God's ordering, we release the exhausting illusion that we can script our own timeline. The natural response is to 'rejoice and be glad' not because life is easy, but because the One who holds all seasons is good.

Tuesday James 1:2-4

James calls us to 'count it all joy' when trials come—not because we see the whole picture, but because we trust the One who does. Our ache to understand 'why' is real and rooted in God's design; what crushes us is the demand that we see the ending before we'll trust His goodness. When we release the burden of needing to comprehend everything and instead embrace our limited perspective, we find the freedom to rejoice in what we cannot yet see.

Wednesday Genesis 3

In the garden, Eve and Adam possessed everything good: food, work, love, presence. Yet they grasped for one thing they were told not to touch—the knowledge of good and evil, God's exclusive domain. That same spirit haunts us: we reject the gift of being creatures and instead burden ourselves with the impossible task of becoming our own gods. Until we see that the serpent's lie has not changed—that satisfaction comes through rebellion rather than reception—we will remain exhausted in our refusal to simply be what God made us.

Thursday Matthew 11:28-30

Christ's invitation to the weary is not to work harder but to come to Him and find rest—to exchange the unbearable weight we've carried for His yoke, which is 'easy' and 'light.' In the gospel, we discover that Someone has already borne what crushes us: our inadequacy, our sin, our ache to be enough. When we lay our burdens at His feet and receive Him as our gift, we stop striving to become gods and begin living as beloved children, which is infinitely more than we could ever achieve.

Friday Ecclesiastes 3:12-13

The preacher returns us to the most elemental truth: that to eat, drink, and find satisfaction in our toil is 'the gift of God.' We do not need to earn our way into significance through achievement or unlock some hidden meaning in suffering; we simply need to receive what is already in our hands—today, breath, work, the person beside us, food at our table. As we lay down the exhausting burden of becoming enough, we discover we were always held in the palm of an all-glorious God, and that is more than enough.
