How Do You Live Well Knowing You'll Die Someday

Ecclesiastes 9:1-10 July 26, 2026 Pastor Ricky Alcantar
Thesis Acknowledging the reality of death frees us to release what is passing and embrace both the daily gifts of this fleeting life and the imperishable life secured by Christ's defeat of death.

Illustrations· 11
  1. historical example · unit #3 — The preacher introduces the 1777 New England Primer's alphabet as a historical example of how earlier cultures taught children to confront death from the earliest age. The humor and shock value ('wow, this is for 5-year-olds?') establish the contrast between Puritan directness and modern avoidance while building curiosity about why such an approach existed.
  2. cultural reference · unit #5 — A series of concrete cultural comparisons illustrate how modern Americans sequester death: fake deaths in entertainment vs. real deaths hidden in hospitals; blunt language ('they died') replaced with euphemisms ('passed away'); public grief (wearing black for a year) replaced with private grieving. The cumulative effect indicts the listener's own participation in death-avoidance.
  3. cultural reference · unit #9 — The preacher introduces a secular productivity book that echoes Ecclesiastes' death-awareness. The provocative opening line and chapter title ('In the Long Run, We're All Dead') serve as contemporary witnesses to the text's timeless truth. The humor ('if you're interested in that book, I've got it available') lightens the moment without undercutting the seriousness.
  4. personal story · unit #12 — A personal story of the preacher's first encounter with death in a pastoral role — a child dying at El Paso Children's Hospital. The vivid sensory detail (the beeps and whirs) and the emotional aftermath ('I need to rethink everything in my life') make the abstract theological claim concrete: death clarifies what matters.
  5. personal story · unit #16 — A personal family illustration makes the 'sandcastle' metaphor concrete: the preacher and his kids building a sandcastle, getting emotionally invested, then frustrated as the tide comes in. The self-aware humor ('I'm yelling at the tide') makes the point accessible — we all rage against the inevitable. The payoff: 'when you see it, you can finally let go. And that's a gift.'
  6. historical example · unit #23 — Return to the Puritan alphabet, this time with a direct address to teenagers ('this one's especially for you'). The preacher quotes G ('like the glass our life doth pass') and W ('while youth do cheer, Death may be near'), then breaks the tension with humor about Puritan rap skills. The humor doesn't undercut the point — it makes it memorable for the very audience most resistant to mortality themes.
  7. analogy · unit #30 — The preacher uses two extended analogies to make Christ's defeat of death vivid: death goes from 'roaring lion' to 'house cat,' and from 'endless flood' to 'ferryman.' The ferryman image is particularly effective — death now merely transports believers to eternal life rather than destroying them. This is imaginative preaching making doctrine accessible.
  8. historical example · unit #33 — Return to the Puritan primer to show that their death-awareness was paired with gospel hope: C (come to Christ), I (draw near to God), N (now is the time). The preacher vindicates the Puritans as gospel people, not downers. The phrase 'death of death' becomes a refrain for Christ's victory. This illustration serves both as historical defense and gospel summary.
  9. personal story · unit #37 — A personal family story illustrates gift-seeking: the preacher's boys at the Shavers' house for a scavenger hunt. Once they knew gifts were hidden, they looked at everything differently. The application: Ecclesiastes trains us to see life as a scavenger hunt — 'God gives gifts, and he basically says, I want you to just be attuned and go find them.' This is the interpretive lens for the passage's commands.
  10. personal story · unit #45 — A self-deprecating story about picking up a bucket-list book and realizing how few of the 1,001 places he'll actually see. The humor ('I was so stupid and hopeful') and exaggerated examples (4-week hikes, 2 years of training) set up the contrast with eternal hope. This is a contemporary version of the sandcastle/tide illustration — our aspirations vs. reality.
  11. historical example · unit #52 — The preacher returns to the Puritans one final time to vindicate them as joyful, life-affirming people. He cites Leland Ryken's Worldly Saints to correct the cultural stereotype. The list (kids, marriage, music, art, reading, exploring) and the aside about Puritan frankness on marital intimacy ('became uncomfortable for me to read') is both informative and humanizing. This is the setup for the final Puritan quotation.
Theological claims· 13
  1. Modern American culture excels at pushing death far from consciousness, in contrast to earlier cultures that confronted it directly because death was an immediate, unavoidable reality. unit #4
  2. Ecclesiastes 9 pushes death to the forefront not to depress or paralyze but to free us to live well. unit #6
  3. The text calls us to acknowledge that death is a universal event that happens to all, regardless of our willingness to acknowledge it. unit #8
  4. Death exposes the futility of both libertine autonomy ('I'll live however I want') and moralistic self-salvation ('I'll be good enough to avoid suffering') — both end in the same place. unit #10
  5. Acknowledging the finitude of life produces wisdom by cutting through the smoke and mirrors of life and forcing us to reckon with reality. unit #11
  6. Humans are profoundly unhappy because they are trying to find a source of life they have cut themselves off from through sin. unit #19
  7. At the final judgment, those who have chosen to be cut off from the source of life will receive that choice forever, along with the penalty for the evil they have done. unit #21
  8. Jesus Christ is uniquely the man who defeated death — in contrast to all other religious or political figures who died and stayed dead. unit #28
  9. Christ's resurrection opens the way to life for all who follow him, and he will ultimately destroy death itself as the last enemy. unit #29
  10. We receive Christ by reversing the Genesis 3 rebellion — submitting to God as his people, in his place, under his rule — and putting faith in Christ as Savior and Lord. unit #32
  11. The Book of Ecclesiastes teaches that God gives both humbling gifts (our mortality) and joyful gifts (food, marriage, work), and we are to embrace both. unit #36
  12. Beyond embracing the gifts of this life, we embrace the life after life — the hope that God will redeem our souls from the grave and receive us, a hope that goes far beyond this frustrating life. unit #42
  13. The unmet longings of this life will pale in comparison to what Christ has prepared for us in eternity — in 100 years in his presence, we will have forgotten all about the dream homes, achievements, and aspirations that consumed us here. unit #46

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through death's reality and Christ's victory over it — from acknowledging what we naturally avoid, to receiving the freedom that comes from facing it, to embracing both the gifts of this fleeting life and the imperishable life Christ has secured.

Monday Hebrews 9:27

The writer of Hebrews states it plainly: 'people are destined to die once, and after that to face judgment.' This is not a negotiable fact. Modern America specializes in pushing death out of sight, but the reality does not change because we ignore it. What we avoid in consciousness still waits for us — and Ricky invites us this week to stop running from that truth and let it free us instead.

Tuesday 2 Corinthians 4:16-18

Paul describes the Christian life as a paradox: 'Though outwardly we are wasting away, yet inwardly we are being renewed day by day.' This is not depression — it is clarity. We acknowledge that our bodies are temporary without despair because our spirits are being renewed by the Holy Spirit. We can release our grip on the things of this world precisely because we are fixed on 'what is unseen' and 'eternal.' This is the freedom Ecclesiastes points toward.

Wednesday 1 Corinthians 15:51-54

Paul proclaims that in Christ, 'death has been swallowed up in victory.' The gospel is not a philosophy for accepting death — it is a proclamation that death itself has been defeated. Christ did not merely teach us how to think about death; he rose from it. And because he rose, all who trust in him will rise too, and death will be erased from creation forever. This is why facing death now does not crush us — we face it knowing it has already been conquered.

Thursday John 5:24

Jesus says: 'Whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life and will not be judged but has crossed over from death to life.' Notice the tense: *has eternal life* — not will have, but has. To believe in Christ is not to purchase insurance against future judgment; it is to cross over *now* from death to life. The judgment that every human faces finds no condemnation for those who are in Christ. This is the invitation Ecclesiastes prepares us to hear and accept.

Friday Psalm 49:15

The psalmist declares: 'But God will redeem my life from the grave; he will surely take me to himself.' This is the promise that anchors the whole Christian life: God does not merely leave us to dust. He reaches down, takes us up, and receives us into his presence. All the unfulfilled dreams, unmet loves, and unfinished pursuits of this life will fade like morning mist when we stand in the presence of Christ. Acknowledging death does not lead to despair — it leads us to grip this truth: our soul, our self, belongs to God, and he will not let us go.
