New Mercies in the Ruins of Life

Lamentations 3:1-24 June 28, 2026 Pastor Daniel Everhart
Thesis When we find ourselves in the ruins of life, we can lift our eyes to the God whose steadfast love never ceases and whose mercies are new every morning, finding hope not by accident but by actively calling to mind His faithful character.

6 · Establishes the historical-literary context of Lamentations: a collection of dirges over Jerusalem's destruction by Babylon in 587 BC, with chapter 3's first-person voice positioning Jeremiah as representative of the people's suffering

This book, the book of Lamentations, is a collection of laments or melancholy dirges. They are are sad songs, if you will, or sad poems. These laments describe the horrors of Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 587 BC, and they express grief over the ruined city. In chapters 1 and 2, the author Jeremiah describes the destruction of Jerusalem and all its atrocities from the third person. But here in chapter 3, he describes it in the first person as if what has happened to Jerusalem and the subsequent grief that it caused people is happening to himself. He is, if you will, a representative of Jerusalem's suffering.

7 · Establishes the theological framework for interpreting the lament: Jeremiah is not accusing God of injustice but acknowledging that God's judgment is just, deserved, and promised — the people's covenant violation met with God's covenant faithfulness

Now, on the outset, there's something important that we need to know. About Jeremiah's heart here. Jeremiah is not complaining to the Lord in disbelief for such seemingly cruel punishment. He is not blaming God for their suffering, nor is he accusing God of injustice. God is a just God. He has acted justly toward His people. He has been so patient with His people through their faithless rebellion, and He's a man of His word because He promised through the prophets that this would be their punishment if they didn't repent and turn back to the Lord. Even in his lament, Jeremiah is clear, the people sinned and God has justly judged the people for their sins.

He has driven and brought me into darkness without any light. Surely against me He turns His hand again and again the whole day long." Now, the Hebrew word here man is used to describe a valiant warrior of a man. The original language gives the impression that this man of affliction is a strongman. He is a warrior champion, a man in the arena, if you will. But this strongman has been stricken by the Lord through the Babylonians. What irony we will see that this strongman is pummeled under the wrath of the Sovereign Lord. The irony in this text continues as it's the rod of the Lord's wrath that has afflicted this strongman. Here we begin to get sheep-shepherd imagery. This strongman is now depicted as a feeble sheep. The rod is a weapon that the shepherd would use to defend his sheep from predatory attacks. In this instance, however, the shepherd has turned on his own sheep and inflicted injury upon it from the same tool that he used to protect it once before. The shepherd who normally leads his sheep to good places, to green pastures and beside still waters, has driven this sheep into darkness. And in poetic language in the following verses, Jeremiah will go on to say that God has broken his bones and beaten him to the point of death. 13 · Unpacks the theological content of what Jeremiah calls to mind: God's steadfast love that never ceases and His mercies that are new every morning

And what exactly does Jeremiah remember? The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases. His mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning. Great is your faithfulness! 14 · Applies the doctrine of God's new mercies to contemporary Christian life

God, may these people be comforted by You as they share in Your sufferings. May they be able to say, "The Lord is my portion. I will hope in Him." I pray this for our friends in Jesus' name. Amen.

Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we walk through God's covenant faithfulness in judgment and mercy, tracing how Christ's resurrection secures our hope even in life's deepest ruins.

Monday Romans 8:28

Paul's declaration that God works all things together for good to those who love Him echoes the theology Jeremiah discovered in lament: our suffering is not meaningless chaos but falls within the sovereign rule of a covenant God. When we grasp that the God who permitted Jerusalem's devastation is the same God orchestrating all our griefs toward our ultimate good, we find the courage to lift our eyes in the midst of ruin.

Tuesday Psalm 23

David's shepherd psalm teaches us what Jeremiah learned in the rubble: even when we walk through the valley of the shadow of death, the Shepherd's rod and staff comfort us, and His table of mercy is prepared before us in the presence of enemies. God's loving provision is not absent from our darkest valleys but is precisely *there*, renewed each morning, meeting us in our specific affliction with the exact grace we need.

Wednesday Isaiah 53:3

Isaiah's portrait of the suffering Servant — despised, rejected, acquainted with grief — gives us the face of Christ as the One who descended deeper into affliction than any of us ever will. He did not lament from a distance but entered into our pain as the God-Man, bearing God's judgment against our sin so that our suffering need never separate us from His love or prove that He has abandoned us.

Thursday Matthew 27:46

When Jesus cried out, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" He was experiencing what Jerusalem experienced in exile — the real abandonment that comes with bearing God's wrath for covenant breach. Yet Christ's cry was not the end of the story; it was the necessary prelude to resurrection, proving that even in the depths of God's judgment, the faithful God remains faithful to complete redemption and restore His people.

Friday Revelation 21:4-5

John's vision of all things made new is the culmination of the hope Jeremiah began to grasp in lament: God's mercies do not merely sustain us through ruin; they are moving toward the day when ruin itself is undone forever. We can have confidence in suffering because Christ's resurrection guarantees that He will return, that our tears are numbered and treasured by Him, and that every loss we mourn will be redeemed in the restoration of all things — making our present endurance not meaningless but purposeful.

For the parent

This prompt invites your family to think about a time when things felt really broken or sad — and to notice that God's help showed up in small, unexpected ways. The goal is to help them see that even in hard seasons, God is faithful and His kindness doesn't run out.

Can you think of a time when something really hard happened — maybe you were sad, scared, or things felt messy — and then you noticed God caring for you in a small way? What was that like? (It could be a person He sent to help, a verse someone shared, something in nature, or just a moment when you felt less alone.)
Works for ages 7+ — younger children can share simple examples with parent help; teens and adults can reflect on deeper seasons of suffering and God's provision

Small-group discussion

6 questions for your group this week

  1. In Lamentations 3:1-3, Jeremiah describes himself as 'the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath.' What does the sermon help us understand about why Jeremiah frames his suffering this way—that is, why does he acknowledge God as the agent of his affliction rather than complaining against God's injustice?
    Lamentations 3:1-3
    → How does recognizing God's sovereignty over our suffering—even suffering that feels unjust—change the way we might process our own difficult seasons?
  2. Walk us through Jeremiah's movement from despair to hope in verses 19-24. What shifts in his thinking, and what does he actively choose to do to move from 'my soul is bereft of peace' (v. 17) to 'the Lord is my portion' (v. 24)?
    Lamentations 3:19-24
  3. The sermon emphasizes that God's steadfast love 'never ceases' and His mercies are 'new every morning' (v. 22-23)—even during Jerusalem's worst horrors. What does it mean practically for God's mercies to be 'new' each morning, and how might that truth reshape the way we face tomorrow if today has been devastatingly difficult?
    Lamentations 3:22-23
    → Can you think of a specific way God's mercies have been newly present for you in a moment of real pain?
  4. The sermon traces Jeremiah's lament forward to Christ as 'the true Man of Affliction who bore God's wrath on our behalf.' How does understanding that Jesus experienced the fullest measure of God's judgment and affliction change the way we read Jeremiah's cry, and what does it promise us about our own suffering?
    Isaiah 53:3
  5. The sermon points out that God's sovereignty over suffering is comforting precisely because 'evil does not rule with no bounds'—there is a God bigger than our atrocities. How does this differ from the way culture typically tries to comfort people who are hurting, and why is God's rulership over pain more stabilizing than other kinds of comfort?
    Romans 8:28
    → What area of suffering in your life or in our world needs this reminder that God is sovereign and His purposes will not be thwarted?
  6. If 'great is his faithfulness' and we can 'have confidence in suffering because' of this (as the sermon applies unit #16), what specific act of faith or trust is the Lord inviting you to take this week—whether in grief, caregiving, parenting, or another storm you're walking through?
