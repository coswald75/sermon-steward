Thesis Because Jesus sat in our sorrow and sowed himself as the seed that defeated death, believers are freed to sit in their own grief without escaping it and to sow generously into an uncertain future without fearing it.

Theological claims· 11
  1. The Genesis 3 curse is the source of all sorrow, manifesting both in death ('dust you shall return') and in the ongoing suffering of thorns and thistles throughout life. unit #7
  2. Sitting in sorrow is not wallowing but active engagement—letting grief teach you the way a student learns in a classroom. unit #15
  3. Sorrow makes you a comforter because the comfort God gives you in grief becomes the comfort you can offer others. unit #16
  4. Sorrow teaches humility by forcing believers to confront the truth that they are not in control—God is. unit #18
  5. Sorrow makes you thankful by exposing the futility of seeking security in created things, freeing you to enjoy them as gifts from God rather than saviors. unit #20
  6. A heart that has sat in sorrow is finally free to give, risk, and receive God's ordinary gifts without demanding control. unit #21
  7. God's sovereignty means he ordains both prosperity and adversity, and believers are wrong to expect only earthly blessings or to expect no earthly blessings at all. unit #34
  8. The blessing of sowing is not in guaranteeing the harvest but in the obedience of sowing and trusting God's sovereign timing. unit #35
  9. Jesus is the one who perfectly sat in the deepest sorrow and sowed the greatest seed. unit #38
  10. Jesus's resurrection is the harvest of his sowing, making him both the Man of Sorrows who bore our grief and the risen Lord who sovereignly holds our tomorrow. unit #47
  11. Believers can grieve honestly and live freely because Jesus entered our sorrow and conquered it in his resurrection. unit #48

He was acquainted with grief before he could even speak, but he never stopped Stepping toward sorrow. Everywhere He went, He walked into the wreckage of the curse. The blind, the lame, the sick, the grieving, His own creation, the people He left heaven for, and He watched them suffer.

He didn't run to the party. No, John 11:35 says He stood at His friend's grave and Jesus wept. He sat in our sorrow.

And at the very end, in a garden called Gethsemane, Matthew tells us he said this, my soul is very sorrowful, even to death. In Luke, he was in such anguish that his sweat became like great drops of blood. He could have escaped. He could have called it all off. But he didn't. He sat in it.

And then they pressed the crown down onto his head. Matthew calls it a crown of thorns. Thorns, the very first mark of the curse back in the garden, thorns and thistles in Genesis 3. The same thorns that come up free in your backyard, that grow in the desert when nothing else will, The curse that puts sorrow in your life, He took it and He wore it on His head. He didn't just teach us how to sit in sorrow, He sat in ours.

But He didn't only sit in the sorrow, He sowed. Jesus said, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone, but if it dies, it bears much fruit. Fruit. John 12:24. Why did he do that? Hebrews 12 says, for the joy that was set before him, he endured The cross. The joy, the harvest he found? Us. Our salvation.

And then 3 days later, the seed rose up. Christ has been raised from the dead, the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep, 1 Corinthians 15 says. You see, death, that end of all mankind, the one appointment that none of us can escape, could not hold him. The man of sorrows who sat in our grief is the risen Lord who holds our tomorrow.

48 · The pastor draws the soteriological conclusion—believers can grieve honestly and live freely because Jesus both entered sorrow and conquered it

That is why we can do both, grieve honestly and live freely. Because the God who lets sorrow come in is the same God who walked into it And conquered it.

49 · The pastor cites Jesus's teaching about losing life to gain it, then testifies to his own conversion—he ran to the party for years, but sorrow, not laughter, led him to Christ and repentance

And as Jesus said, whoever loves his life loses it, and whoever hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life. John 12:25. And this, this isn't theory for me. For years I ran to the party, and every time the music died down. And it wasn't the laughter that saved me, no, it was the sorrow. Sorrow is what led me to the Man of Sorrows. Sorrow is what led me to repentance of my sins.

50 · The pastor issues an evangelistic call, addressing unbelievers who have been escaping sorrow through distraction, declaring that Jesus bore their sorrow and sin, and calling them to repentance

Now if you've been running your whole life to the party from the funeral, the Man of Sorrows, he carried your sorrow already. He bore your sins. He died the death that is the end of all mankind. And he walked out of that grave. You don't have to escape your sorrow and sin anymore. Why? Because he already sat in it for you. You don't have to fear tomorrow because he already holds tomorrow. So turn to him. Repent.

51 · The pastor reads the book's conclusion, Ecclesiastes 12:13, and distinguishes two kinds of fear—the fear of an uncontrollable future and the fear of God—arguing that fearing God replaces the fear of tomorrow

That's where this whole book And the teacher has been driving. Ecclesiastes 12:13 says, fear God and keep his commandments, for this is the whole duty of man. Probably my favorite verse in the whole Bible. And there are 2 fears in this verse. One, the fear that drives you to the bunker, the fear of tomorrow you can't control. And there's the fear of God, not being scared of him, but standing in awe of the one who holds all of it. And then trusting him. A fear of God is— that's the only thing that sets you free. So trade the first fear for the second. Fear God and you will never fear tomorrow again.

52 · The pastor issues three concrete action steps for the week—sit with someone grieving, sow by taking a risk, and trust by surrendering control of one specific thing—and adds a fourth instruction to enjoy one ordinary gift as an act of worship

But don't just walk out here and let this fade by lunchtime. Take 3 things with you. 3 things to actually do this week. 1, sit. Find someone that you know is grieving and just sit with them. Sit with them. 2, sow. Take one God-honoring risk you've been putting off. Is that the conversation? Is that the calling? Is that the reconciliation? Do it. 3, trust. Name the thing that you keep trying to control. Is it your future? Is it money? Is it your kids? Is it your health? Is it the relationship you can't fix or you don't have? And pray it into God's hands. And then enjoy one ordinary gift on purpose. Sit with a cup of coffee. Linger at dinner with your friends. Take the walk with the family. Take those things as a gift from God who holds your tomorrow.

Because of Jesus, I don't have— I don't have to escape the sorrow of Larry. I don't have to fear Tomorrow, I get to live the way Larry lived. He held every ordinary day with open hands, and he enjoyed it. He enjoyed the cup of coffee, the good conversation, the work that was in front of him. He lived with the end in full view, and so can I. And so can you. That's the freedom of Ecclesiastes. That's the freedom that's— that the teacher has been after all along. The one who has faced sorrow is finally free to live. So don't escape and don't fear. Sit in the sorrow, sow the seed. And receive today for the gift that it is.

Let's pray. Lord, you are kind. You are a good Father who gives good gifts, and we trust in your plan. Lord, fill us with your Spirit as we go out into the world. May you help us to grieve and grieve with others. May you help us to take those chances, Lord. May you give us the faith to just trust you. And God, we— may we be the person that enjoys the gifts that you give us every single day. It's only through you You are our boast. You are our firm foundation. Amen. Amen. Amen.

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc from sorrow's sovereignty to our freedom to grieve and give: beginning with the curse that grounds all suffering, moving through Christ's perfect descent into grief, and arriving at the gospel's liberating power to sit in sorrow and sow generously.

Monday Genesis 3:18-19

We live in a world fundamentally altered by sin's curse—not merely distant, abstract judgment, but the daily reality of thorns, thistles, and the grave itself. These are not aberrations or exceptions to life; they are its texture. Understanding sorrow begins here, with the humble acknowledgment that the fallen world we inhabit *is* fallen, and our grief is the appropriate response to that truth.

Tuesday Isaiah 53

In Isaiah's portrait of the Suffering Servant, we see the God-man entering not above our sorrow but into its depths—bearing the weight of our curse, not as spectator but as substitute. His willingness to be acquainted with grief and to be wounded for our transgressions is the pivotal act that makes our own grief meaningful rather than meaningless. We do not grieve in isolation; we grieve in the company of the one who grieved most deeply on our behalf.

Wednesday John 11:35

Jesus wept—not in stoicism, not in denial, but in genuine tears over death's sting and his beloved's loss. His brevity here is profound: emotion unguarded, grief unashamed, the Son of God present to the reality of human heartbreak. When we sit in our own sorrow without rushing to fix it or numb it, we follow his pattern and give sorrow the dignity it deserves as a teacher of truth.

Thursday 2 Corinthians 1:4

God's mercy toward us in our affliction is never private—it flows through us to comfort others in the very same afflictions we have endured. Our scars become our credentials, our grief our empathy. The pain we have sat with, honestly and prayerfully, is recycled by grace into tenderness for those around us who suffer. This is how the body of Christ learns to carry one another's burdens.

Friday 2 Corinthians 8:9

Christ's poverty—his voluntary descent and self-emptying—purchased our spiritual riches and models the generosity the gospel demands. When we have truly grieved what we have lost and trusted God through it, our grip on resources loosens; we become free to sow generously into an uncertain future, knowing that neither our seed nor our harvest rests finally on our control. This is the freedom of the cross applied to our wallets, our time, our tomorrow.
