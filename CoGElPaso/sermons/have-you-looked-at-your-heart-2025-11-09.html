Theological claims· 12
  1. God is not concerned with outward appearance alone but with the condition of the heart underneath. unit #3
  2. King Saul, though chosen for his worldly qualifications, lacked the spiritual qualities necessary to lead God's people. unit #10
  3. Samuel's grief reveals the misdirected hearts of both Israel (trusting worldly wisdom) and Saul (trusting himself and his own glory). unit #11
  4. God's provision of David is an act of sovereign seeing and providence—God sees the true king He desires and provides him as a gift to His people. unit #20
  5. The story of David's anointing teaches Israel that God can be trusted, that obedience is the path to care, and that they must attend to their hearts and trust God's plan. unit #23
  6. Conviction of sin is an ongoing grace that drives us to Christ, our all-sufficient physician—we must feel our malady to rightly prize our healer. unit #25
  7. Israel experiences both the cost of rejecting God's plan (through Saul's failure) and the mercy of God in bringing about His plan (through David's anointing) despite their failings. unit #26
  8. David, the unexpected shepherd king from Bethlehem and the line of Jesse, points forward to Jesus, the ultimate King and Savior. unit #29
  9. Jesus is the King of the universe who chose to come despite humanity's obsession with outward appearance, living perfectly and dying as a substitute to provide hope for us now and in eternity. unit #32
  10. Because Christ died for us despite seeing our failures, He covers our sin and actively reshapes us into His image. unit #33
  11. Attending to our hearts and trusting God's plan means using the biblical narratives to diagnose misdirection, recognize God's redirecting work, and rest in Christ's redemption. unit #46
  12. Awareness of our own depravity paired with confidence in Christ's infallibility leads not to despair but to hope, as we press on toward holiness under the care of the all-sufficient Savior. unit #48

And Saul sent to Jesse saying, let David remain in my service, for he has found favor in my sight. And whenever the harmful spirit from God was upon Saul, David took the lyre and played it with his hand. So Saul was refreshed and was well, and the harmful spirit departed from him. Look at how David is described primarily. Yes, he's skilled, but he's a man of valor, of war. He's prudent, a good presence. Most importantly, the Lord is with him. These are all characteristics demonstrating inner quality. Rather than focusing on a traditionally kingly external quality, the Lord sovereignly places him in a position of influence in the court of the king whom he will replace.

29 · Elevates the David narrative to its Christological significance: David is an unexpected king from Bethlehem, from the line of Jesse, pointing forward to Jesus, the ultimate King and Savior

The anointing of David is the anointing of an unexpected king. A shepherd boy anointed as God's chosen king. A shepherd boy from where? Bethlehem, whose dad was Jesse. A shepherd boy from whose family lineage would come the long awaited Messiah, Jesus, the Savior of the world.

30 · Establishes the typological relationship between David and Jesus via Isaiah 11

He is the one who redeems our hearts. As we look to a heart redeemed, we see that this Savior of the world shows God's heart not to just redirect misdirected hearts, but to redeem heart hearts. If we see in Saul what is known as an antitype of Christ, in David we see a gracious precursor to Christ. He is a type or a foreshadowing of Jesus. David was God's gift to his people then. And Jesus is the true and better David, God's gift to us. Now God uses the story of Saul as one of judgment for Israel, following its own heart and the world around it, rather than trusting God. And the story of David is a picture of God's care for Israel even in their failings. The story of Jesus is the story of the ultimate redemption of the hearts of God's People. Earlier we noted that David being a son of Jesse from Bethlehem was an important detail. What we see in Isaiah the Prophet four hundred years after David's death. Isaiah speaks of the coming Messiah in this way in Isaiah chapter 11 There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse, and a branch from his roots shall bear fruit. And I drew out a minute ago the Spirit was capitalized, and the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the Spirit of wisdom and understanding, Spirit of counsel and might, the Spirit of knowledge, and the fear of the Lord and his delight shall be in the fear of the Lord. 31 · Traces the typological connections between David and Jesus through Isaiah 11 and 53

David was from the house of Jesse. Jesus is from the line of Jesse. The Spirit of the Lord rushed upon David, and the Spirit of the Lord is now with us through Christ. The picture of the Savior of the world in Isaiah 11 was foreshadowed by David and fulfilled in Jesus Christ. The world was looking for an outwardly victorious Savior as their Messiah. And just as David was described by his character, Jesus is described as delighting in the fear of the Lord, judging not with aggression, but with righteousness, equity, justice, faithfulness, and so on. David is put forward as a wise king. Jesus is the wiser king. Isaiah goes even further in chapter 53 when the Prophet is clear that the Messiah Jesus would not be one who would be adorned with outward majesty or particular beauty. Jesus through his ministry showed us time after time that his focus was inward. The inward is what produces the outward fruit that God has determined. Isaiah 53 who has believed what he has heard from us, and to whom has the arm of the Lord been revealed? For he grew up before him like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground. He had no form or majesty that we should look at him, and no beauty that we should desire him. He was despised and rejected by men, a man of sorrows acquainted with grief, and as one from whom men hide their faces. He was despised, and we esteemed him not.

32 · Synthesizes the Christological argument: Isaiah predicted humanity's continued obsession with external appearance, yet Jesus came anyway—living perfectly, dying willingly, and providing hope

Church this is the king of the universe, Israel. Isaiah even predicts that we will continue to be enamored with the outward appearance over the inward. But Jesus still chooses to come to live a perfect life, to die a willing substitutionary death on our behalf and provide a future and a hope for us now and in eternity. We didn't plan I did not give Andrew my manuscript before he planted the worship center. We sang about our all sufficient merit. Who is Christ? The one foreseen in Isaiah 53 and 11, the one foreshadowed in David. That's who we are singing about even now.

33 · Establishes the soteriological consequence of Christ's work: Jesus' death covers our failures and His ongoing work reshapes us into His image

Jesus sees our desires, sees our failings, sees our shortcomings, and still died for us because of what Christ did. It changes what we're trying to become. He covers our failures and reshapes us himself into his image.

34 · Signals the shift from theological exposition to direct application

So if our focus today is to attend to our hearts and trust God's plan, then there's a real clear warning and admonishment for us, as well as a brief encouragement.

35 · Issues the core admonishment: don't ignore your heart and don't trust your own plan over God's

Here's the admonishment. Simply put, don't ignore the importance of your heart and don't trust your plan over God's plan. As you navigate the highs and lows of life, may I ask, where is your primary attention? Are you more concerned with how you look to the world or how you look to the Lord? Do you find yourself focusing primarily on the things held out by the world or on the state of your heart? I want to be careful to avoid both legalism and hyper spiritualization here. I don't want you to go away from this message going just be better please. That's not what the Lord is calling us to, but the Lord might be calling us to be a little better. But I don't think if I were to guess that our problem is over spiritualization of our circumstances, I would wager that it's maybe more we are more prone to under spiritualization?

36 · Provides concrete examples of how to spiritualize ordinary life: in relationships, prioritize God's glory over external attraction or compatibility; in marriage, pray for hearts knit together for God's glory rather than merely for practical help

Here's what I mean. What if in relationships our primary question before the Lord was is this relationship honoring and glorifying to God? In other words, if you're dating instead of asking the Lord for somebody you're compatible with, someone that looks the way you want, someone that acts the way you want, what if the question was Lord, would you provide a partner for my life whose desire is for your glory, who honors and trusts you above all things? Again, it's not that those other things don't matter, especially when we're young, but even when we're old, attraction matters. But skin gets wrinkly and saggy and things don't work like they did before. What if the prayer of our spouses in the morning moved from things like help us relate to one another well, help us budget well, help us stay in shape and control our life. What if it moved from prayers like that to a prayer to honor and glorify God in all things? Things. What if our first prayer with our spouse each day was, lord, knit our hearts together in all things for your glory. We trust you with all of our life. Practicals matter, but true wisdom comes from the Lord. Now we just did an exercise as a pastoral team called A Journey of Generosity. So let's move into the realm of finances for a second. What about work vocation? Maybe you're in a job that's absolutely terrible. Maybe you're just, you can't stand it, you're having a terrible job and you've been praying, lord, I cannot believe you want me in this. Please get me out of this. It moves it from primarily focusing on what's challenging in our external life to putting it in the heart before the Lord.

44 · Returns to John Newton as the final illustration: a man who was objectively terrible, immune from no sin, yet apprehended by God's grace and transformed into an instrument of gospel proclamation

John Newton. This is what Tony Reinke says about John Newton in his book. John Newton's moral life prior to Christ had already sunk. 45 · Applies the Newton illustration directly: even if we're not as obviously depraved as Newton, our sins are no less serious and we are no less in need of a Redeemer

Now we may not be where Newton was, we may not be that depraved, but let's not pretend that our own sins are any less important or in need of a redeemer.

46 · Synthesizes the sermon's entire argument into a single theological claim: attending to our hearts means using the biblical narratives (Israel, Saul, David) to diagnose our own misdirections, recognize God's redirecting work, and rest in Christ's redemptive grace

Attending to our own hearts and trusting God's plan looks like using the story of Israel, Saul and David to help us notice where our own hearts might be misdirected, where the Lord is helping to redirect our actions and affections, and understanding the grace and mercy of God, redeeming our hearts through Christ.

48 · Establishes the theological resolution via Newton: awareness of our depravity paired with awareness of Christ's infallibility is the path to hope

And so these two convictions stand as marvelous evidences of grace. An awareness of our own heart's depravity and an awareness of our physician's infallibility. Newton wrote of a friend, wrote to a friend, but I check myself. I am vile indeed. But Jesus is full of grace and truth. He leads and guides, he feeds and guards, he restores and heals. He is an all sufficient Savior. Under the care of such an all sufficient Christ, the chief of sinners does not despair, but presses on towards holiness.

49 · Applies the entire sermon pastorally to three possible heart conditions in the congregation: misdirected (wandering), being redirected (aware of sin and being restored), or convicted of sin

Church, you cannot attend to your hearts by yourself. If you do that, we have no hope. We will be misdirected with no hope but God because of the great love with which he loved us. Us while we were still what Christ died for us. That is the hope. Maybe your heart's misdirected this morning. Maybe you're finding yourself in a season of wandering from the Lord. Lean in. That is a picture of love. Church, when you recognize sin in your own life, remember this. There is therefore now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus. That recognition of sin is recognition of the Holy Spirit's work in your life and a kindness of the Lord to remind you of his grace. Deal with the sin, confess it, because he is faithful and just to forgive you your sins and what cleanse you from all unrighteousness.

Let this sermon not be. Let this word not be just. Be better. Christ was the best on your behalf. Turn to him today. If you have not turned your life to Christ yet, do it today. He is drawing you to Himself. He is causing your eyes to go open. Come to him today. Church, as you go through your week, attend to your hearts. Trust God's plan for your life. Daily readings · Monday–Friday

5-day reading plan

This week we trace the arc of God's heart-seeing sovereignty: from His rejection of outward appearance, through His sovereign provision of the true king, to our need for Christ the physician, and finally to the hope of transformation under His care.

Monday 1 Samuel 9:1-2

Saul's external credentials—tall, handsome, from a prominent family—made him the obvious choice by worldly standards. Yet 1 Samuel 9:1-2 shows us the very qualifications that made him appear kingly became the foundation of his spiritual failure, for he never learned to look inward at his own heart before God. We must ask ourselves whether we are building our lives on the same hollow scaffolding of appearance and status that collapsed under Saul.

Tuesday 1 Samuel 15:23

Saul's rebellion against God's explicit command reveals that outward obedience divorced from a transformed heart is worthless—"rebellion is like the sin of divination, and arrogance like the evil of idolatry." His refusal to fully destroy the Amalekites exposed his love of glory and approval over submission to God's plan. In this we see that no external achievement or appearance can compensate for a heart that refuses to bow before the Lord.

Wednesday Isaiah 11:1-5

Where Saul failed through misdirected ambition, Isaiah prophesies of a king from Jesse's line whose heart would be fixed entirely on God's pleasure: "The Spirit of the Lord will rest on him." David's anointing and Isaiah's promise both reveal God as the one who sees what we cannot—who discerns the hidden character and shapes history according to His purposes, not ours. We rest in the assurance that our sovereign God sees and provides exactly what His people need.

Thursday Isaiah 53:1-3

Just as David was overlooked by his own father and chosen by God despite his humble appearance, Jesus came without form or comeliness that we should desire Him—yet He is the true and better David, the King whom all the world rejected but God exalted. His despised appearance masks the infinite worth of the God-man who came to see and heal our broken hearts. In Him we see that God's choice of leaders and saviors operates by an entirely different logic than the world's.

Friday 1 John 1:9

Our task this week is not merely to confess our misdirected hearts—it is to confess them to the God who is faithful and just to forgive us and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness through Christ. We examine our hearts not to drive us to despair but to drive us to the Savior, who sees all our failure and yet chose to die for us anyway. This is the hope that transforms our hearts: the knowledge that the all-glorious King has already purchased our healing and now reshapes us into His image.

Pray together this week

A Prayer for Examined Hearts

Father, we stand before You in awe of Your piercing sight—You alone see not as man sees, looking at outward appearance, but You examine the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). We confess that we, too, are prone to the very misdirection that plagued Israel: we trust in worldly wisdom and external affirmation rather than in Your sovereign plan. We construct carefully curated appearances while neglecting the spiritual condition beneath. We grieve, as Samuel grieved, over the ways we—and those we love—have chosen the world's measure of success over obedience to You.

Yet the gospel invites us to feel our malady so that we might rightly prize our Healer (1 John 1:9). In Jesus, the true and better David, You have given us a King who saw through all pretense and came anyway—who lived perfectly in our place and died as our substitute, covering our sin and reshaping us into His image. By His Spirit, conviction of sin becomes not despair but a grace that drives us nearer to His cross.

We ask, therefore, for courage to examine our own hearts this week. Show us where we are more concerned with how we appear to the world than how we stand before You. Grant us the humility to recognize our ongoing need for the Physician who alone can heal. Free us from both the legalism of external righteousness and the deceit of under-spiritualizing our circumstances. As we encounter the biblical narratives, help us diagnose misdirection in our own hearts, see Your sovereign redirecting work, and rest in Christ's redemption.

May the awareness of our depravity, paired with unwavering confidence in Christ's infallibility, lead us not to despair but to joy. We commit ourselves to press on toward holiness under the care of our all-sufficient Savior, together trusting His plan rather than our own. To Him be glory forever.
