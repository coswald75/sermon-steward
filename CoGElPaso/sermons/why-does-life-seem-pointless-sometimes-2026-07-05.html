Theological claims· 15
  1. Ecclesiastes' uncomfortable truths are the very thing that will reinvigorate us and equip us to face life if we will fully engage them rather than avoid them. unit #7
  2. Ecclesiastes teaches both that work is good and that work often proves futile—these are not contradictory but complementary truths about life under the sun. unit #12
  3. Even though Ecclesiastes will ultimately commend wisdom, it also teaches that wisdom cannot overcome life's hebel nature—the wise cannot escape the human condition any more than the foolish. unit #20
  4. Ecclesiastes presents a true but limited perspective on life from ground level, which must be complemented by the rest of Scripture's perspective from above to understand why life is hebel. unit #22
  5. Much of our unhappiness comes from trying to be God—grasping at what only God can hold—rather than accepting that we are finite, limited image-bearers who need to receive God's rule rather than seize it. unit #23
  6. Sin is fundamentally the refusal to be human and the attempt to be God—humanity repeatedly tries to ascend to the divine throne, and God frustrates these efforts in kindness. unit #24
  7. Life's hebel nature comes from both divine design (we are finite creatures) and human dysfunction (we are fallen rebels), resulting in a world subjected to futility. unit #25
  8. Ecclesiastes does not teach that life is meaningless but that it is vapor-like—and when we accept God's rule, we are freed to enjoy His gifts of food, drink, work, and joy. unit #27
  9. Ecclesiastes disillusioned us of earthly things so we can grasp what truly lasts—now we will reexamine all four points from above the sun rather than under it. unit #29
  10. While all human work blows away like vapor, God's purposes and Word stand forever—therefore we must align our lives with His work rather than our own. unit #30
  11. While life under the sun seems endlessly repetitive, God is taking history toward a glorious end—new heavens and new earth—and Christians can be confused about the journey but confident in the destination. unit #31
  12. While nothing is new under the sun, God repeatedly interrupts history to do genuinely new things—culminating in Christ's incarnation, which is the only truly new thing in a world of endless repetition. unit #32
  13. While human wisdom cannot overcome life's vapor-like quality, God's wisdom sovereignly rules all things, and we are not His counselors but worshipers of His inscrutable ways. unit #33
  14. Christian happiness comes not from nihilistic resignation but from releasing our attempt to control life and receiving God's sovereign rule—trusting that He has a plan even when we don't understand it. unit #34
  15. Ecclesiastes presents a paradox—we cannot receive God's gifts of joy until we first submit to His rule and accept that life in a fallen world is painful and uncontrollable. unit #39

24 · Establishes the second theological reason for hebel: human rebellion against God

But second point here, second reason that we find this life feels pointless, why does that happen? We rage against God. See, here's the thing. Now, we could just end there. And you could just go this week and just think about your life, you know. Man, you probably felt great 4th of July. 30 · First reversal: under the sun, everything blows away, but above the sun, God's purposes stand forever

Number 1, here's the good news. Under the sun, it will all blow away, but above the sun, number 1, God and his purposes will never blow away. Ecclesiastes 1:3 says, what does man gain by all the toil at which he toils under the sun? 31 · Second reversal: under the sun, life is endless repetition, but above the sun, God is taking history toward a glorious end—new heavens and new earth

Second piece of good news. God, it may seem pointless, but God is taking— God, this life is endless, but God is taking history toward a cataclysmic and glorious end. Look at the contrast here. 5-day reading plan

This week, Ecclesiastes disillusions us of self-sovereignty so we can grasp what truly lasts—moving from the futility under the sun to God's eternal purposes, His sovereignty over history, and our freedom to receive His gifts.

Monday Ecclesiastes 12:13

After walking through the vapor-like nature of all human striving, the Preacher arrives at the foundation: fear God and keep His commandments. This is not resignation but reorientation. Only when we stop fighting against life's uncontrollable nature and submit to God's rule do we find our footing. The very disillusionment of Ecclesiastes is a gift—it strips away false hopes so we can build on what endures.

Tuesday Romans 8:20

Paul names what the Preacher observed: creation itself has been subjected to futility, not by its own choice but by the will of the One who subjected it in hope. This is not cruel randomness—it is God's merciful frustration of our attempts to find life apart from Him. We are finite creatures in a fallen world, and both realities press us toward the only One who transcends them.

Wednesday Isaiah 40:6-8

The Preacher watches the grass wither and the flower fade—the very image Isaiah uses to describe all flesh, all our toil and building and grasping. Yet Isaiah's word does not fade. When we align our lives not with our own vapor-like projects but with God's eternal Word and purposes, we attach ourselves to what cannot be shaken. This is not escape from work but reframing it—we labor, but we labor in service to what lasts.

Thursday 2 Peter 3:10-13

Peter breaks the Preacher's cycle of endless repetition with the promise of God's interruption: the day of the Lord will come, the heavens will pass away, and God will bring new heavens and a new earth. The Preacher is right that nothing is new under the sun—until Christ returns. Christians can be confused about the journey but confident in the destination. We are not trapped in vapor; we are moving toward restoration.

Friday Ecclesiastes 2:24

Having shown us that we cannot grasp or control life, the Preacher offers us freedom: to eat and drink and find satisfaction in our work, receiving it as a gift from God's hand rather than seizing it as our due. This is not shallow pleasure—it is joy rooted in acceptance and trust. When we stop trying to be God and open our hands, we discover that the gifts themselves were always there, waiting for us to receive them with gratitude.
